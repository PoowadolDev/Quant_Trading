## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation.** Institutional allocators typically evaluate **structured investment strategies**—commercially distributed, rules-based products marketed by banks and asset managers—using **pro-forma (backtested) track records** constructed before any live trading. The paper asks how much of that marketed signal survives once a strategy is actually traded, a question the existing **backtest-overfitting** (Harvey et al., 2016; Bailey et al., 2014; McLean and Pontiff, 2016) and **fund-persistence** (Jagannathan et al., 2010; Baquero et al., 2005) literatures do not directly address, since neither examines commercially packaged, bank-distributed rules-based products.

2. **Dataset.** The analysis uses a proprietary sample of **1,726 commercially distributed structured strategies from ten anonymised global institutions (A–J)**, spanning **2009–2025**, across six asset classes (equities 39%, commodities 21%, rates 14%, FX 11%, credit 8%, multi-asset 7%). Strategies are grouped into **seven benchmarking buckets** (Carry, Hedging, Momentum, Multi Premia, Factor, Value, Liquidity) sharing a common return mechanism, and into four broader objective groups (Return-Seeking, Carry/Short-Convexity, Hedging/Defensive, Multi-Premia/Diversifying). Mean pro-forma length is **13.7 years**; mean live length is **5.6 years**.

3. **Method — performance and decay measures.** Returns are rescaled to a common **10% annualised volatility target**, making volatility-adjusted return proportional to the Sharpe ratio. **Performance decay** is defined as the live-minus-pro-forma change in this vol-adjusted return, computed at **six-month and twelve-month** post-launch horizons, alongside four risk metrics (maximum drawdown, CVaR95, downside deviation, Sortino ratio).

4. **Method — the leave-one-out (LOO) peer benchmark.** The paper's central methodological contribution is a **leave-one-out (LOO) bucket-average benchmark**: for each strategy-day, the benchmark is the equal-weighted average return of all *other* strategies in the same bucket, adapting the **Active Peer Benchmark** of Hunter et al. (2014) to this setting. This is corroborated by an independent **Bloomberg total-return index** benchmark (e.g., MSCI ACWI for equities, Bloomberg US Treasury for rates, high-yield/IG credit indices, J.P. Morgan USD Index for FX) via **Jensen-alpha** regressions, including a two-factor variant with a **VIX term-structure convexity proxy**.

5. **Main result — raw decay.** Raw volatility-adjusted returns decline **2.1 percentage points** (6-month horizon) and **3.1 percentage points** (12-month horizon) from pro-forma to live, both significant at **p<0.01**, and this magnitude exceeds what a **moving-block bootstrap** (block sizes 10–63 days, p<0.001) attributes to mechanical mean reversion alone. A per-strategy placebo test confirms launches are not timed to each strategy's own pre-launch peaks, ruling out within-strategy cherry-picking.

6. **Main result — benchmark conditioning collapses the signal.** Without adjustment, the pro-forma predictor of live returns has **β=0.137 (R²=0.148)**. Under the **Bloomberg Jensen-alpha** benchmark this falls to **β=0.025 (R²=0.032)**, an **~81% reduction**; under the **LOO benchmark** it falls to **β=0.034 (R²=0.054)**, a **~75% reduction**. For decay regressions, raw β=**-0.896** shrinks to **-0.377** (Bloomberg) and **-0.372** (LOO)—close agreement between two independently constructed benchmarks. A matched-sample **Wald test rejects equality of raw vs. adjusted coefficients (z=4.03, p<0.001)**, showing the attenuation is substantive, not an artifact of added noise.

7. **Structural channel 1 — regime timing.** **Regime extremity** (the pre-launch bucket-factor return's deviation from its long-run mean) strongly predicts LOO-relative decay: **β=0.716 (p<0.001)** at 12 months and **β=0.469 (p<0.01)** at 6 months. Sorting into quintiles shows the coldest-regime quintile improves by **~+0.8pp** while the hottest quintiles decay **-3.5 to -4.5pp**, a **four-plus percentage point cold-minus-hot spread**.

8. **Structural channel 2 — launch density (crowding).** **Log launch density** (mean 17.8 strategies per bucket-year) does not predict average decay directly at either horizon, but its interaction with the pro-forma signal is **positive and significant only at the 6-month horizon**, consistent with short-lived capacity erosion that dissipates as crowded positions unwind, rather than a persistent level effect.

9. **Practical implications.** The paper proposes a **regime-conditional haircut rule**: expected live return ≈ 0.137×pro-forma return − 5×regime-extremity z-score. A worked FX-carry example with **+12pp pro-forma return** and a **hot regime (z=+1.5)** implies an expected live return of **≈ -5.9pp**, far more conservative than the ad-hoc 50% haircut common in practice. Risk metrics also deteriorate live: **54% worse maximum drawdown (p=0.002)** and **59% worse Sortino ratio (p<0.001)**. A logit/LPM **failure-prediction classifier** achieves **AUC=0.81** for performance decay and **0.78** for multiple failures in the Return-Seeking group (2–3x lift), but weaker discrimination (AUC 0.48–0.64) elsewhere.

10. **Limitations.** The sample is **positively selected**: strategies proposed but never launched are unobserved, so decay estimates are a lower bound. The proprietary dataset limits exact replication, and inference is drawn from only **ten institution clusters** (mitigated with a Webb 6-point wild-cluster bootstrap, Cameron and Miller, 2015). All returns are gross of fees/transaction costs, so live decay likely understates investors' actual net experience. Hedging/Defensive strategies' payoff role is not properly captured by unconditional return decay, an evaluation gap left for future work.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### The Leave-One-Out (LOO) Bucket-Average Benchmark
The paper's central methodological device measures each strategy's benchmark as the equal-weighted return of all *other* strategies sharing its bucket on the same day, eliminating mechanical self-correlation. It requires no discretionary index selection and is corroborated by an entirely independent Bloomberg index benchmark, with both approaches producing nearly identical attenuation of the pro-forma signal (β≈-0.37 in both cases).

**Why it matters:** It demonstrates that peer-relative, not index-relative, evaluation is the appropriate lens for heterogeneous alternative strategies — a single market index is too coarse when products with similar labels differ materially across providers, and the convergence of two independent benchmarks rules out benchmark-construction artifacts as the explanation.

### Regime Timing at Launch
Strategies are disproportionately launched after their bucket factor has performed unusually well (a "hot" regime), and this pre-launch extremity — not the strategy's own skill — is what the marketed backtest largely reflects. The regime-extremity z-score is highly predictive of subsequent idiosyncratic (LOO-relative) decay.

**Why it matters:** It operationalizes an actionable due-diligence rule (haircut scales with regime extremity) that lets allocators discount a backtest quantitatively rather than applying an arbitrary flat haircut, directly linking academic "catering" and factor-timing theory (Célérier and Vallée, 2017; Arnott et al., 2016) to a concrete institutional decision.

### Benchmark-Conditioned Attenuation of Pro-Forma Predictability
Raw pro-forma-live correlation (β=0.137) looks meaningful until the dependent variable is replaced with benchmark-adjusted (peer- or index-relative) performance, at which point the coefficient collapses by 75–81%. A Wald test formally confirms the drop is not a noise artifact.

**Why it matters:** This is the paper's key evidentiary finding — it shows that what looks like "strategy-specific skill" in a marketed backtest is predominantly exposure to a favorable common-factor regime, meaning conventional (unconditioned) backtest screening systematically overstates expected live alpha.

### Risk Metric Deterioration and Position Sizing
Beyond returns, tail-risk and drawdown metrics also worsen live relative to pro-forma (54% worse max drawdown, 59% worse Sortino ratio), while volatility itself is roughly unchanged (49%, not significant).

**Why it matters:** It shows that position sizes calibrated purely on pro-forma volatility and downside statistics are likely too aggressive — allocators risk simultaneously overestimating expected return and underestimating the capital/risk budget needed to hold the strategy.

### Out-of-Sample Failure Classification
A logistic/linear-probability model trained on pro-forma characteristics (vol-adjusted return, volatility, drawdown, CVaR, age, complexity, commonality) predicts live failure outcomes using a time-split (train on earlier launches, test on later cohorts) validation design, achieving AUC up to 0.81 for Return-Seeking decay outcomes.

**Why it matters:** It moves beyond descriptive decay statistics to a genuinely predictive, deployable screening tool — a top-decile exclusion filter captures 29% of realized decay failures at a 3x lift over the base rate, giving allocators a concrete pre-launch triage mechanism.

## 4. Ideas for Development

- **Peer-relative (LOO) benchmarking for other alternative asset classes** — apply the leave-one-out bucket-average benchmark methodology to other commercially marketed but heterogeneous product categories (e.g., private credit funds, structured notes, or crypto strategies) where a single market index is inadequate but enough peer products exist to construct a peer average.

- **Regime-conditional haircut rule as a due-diligence screen** — implement the paper's linear haircut formula (expected live return ≈ raw slope × pro-forma return − regime-extremity penalty) as a standardized pre-investment checklist item for any backtested strategy pitch, quantifying the discount rather than relying on an ad-hoc flat percentage.

- **Failure-prediction classifier as a triage tool** — build a similar logit/LPM classifier using easily observable pro-forma characteristics (return, volatility, drawdown, CVaR, age) with a strict time-split validation to flag high-decay-risk strategies before capital commitment, mirroring the paper's top-decile exclusion approach.

- **Moving-block bootstrap and placebo tests for backtest scrutiny** — reuse the paper's two robustness techniques (moving-block bootstrap against mean-reversion null; per-strategy placebo permutation test against within-strategy cherry-picking) as a general-purpose toolkit whenever evaluating whether an observed pre/post-event performance discontinuity is genuine versus mechanical.

- **Risk-metric deterioration checks alongside return decay** — extend any backtest evaluation pipeline to separately test drawdown, CVaR, and Sortino deterioration (not just mean return), since the paper shows risk metrics can deteriorate even when volatility itself does not, which has direct implications for position sizing.

- **Launch-cohort density as a crowding proxy** — apply the log launch-density variable (count of similar product launches per period) as an additional feature in any strategy-selection model, particularly focusing on short-horizon effects, since the paper found crowding amplifies pro-forma signal degradation only at shorter (6-month) horizons.
