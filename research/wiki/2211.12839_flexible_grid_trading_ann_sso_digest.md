## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation.** Quantitative/algorithmic trading turns investment decisions into fixed, quantifiable logic that removes emotional interference; 60–70% of US stock trading is already automated. Classic **grid trading** — evenly spaced pending orders inside a price range, buying low and selling high — earns spread profit in fluctuating markets, but existing **equal-distance** and **equal-ratio** grids suffer from **premature entry and exit** and cannot adapt when market conditions change. The paper's goals: (1) a new grid architecture fixing premature entry/exit, (2) self-adjustment to changing markets, (3) rule-based logic removing irrational decisions, (4) balanced risk vs. profit.

2. **Classic grid mechanics and setup formulas.** A grid needs five basic parameters: total capital $F_0$, initial price $P_0$, grid upper bound $G_{ul}$, grid lower bound $G_{ll}$, and total grid count $n$. For an **equal-distance grid**, spacing is
   $$G_s = \frac{G_{ul} - G_{ll}}{n} \tag{1}$$
   Worked example: $G_{ul}=130$, $G_{ll}=70$, $n=12$ → $G_s = (130-70)/12 = 5$ price units per grid. For an **equal-ratio grid** the spacing is a fixed ratio satisfying $G_{ul} = G_{ll}(1+G_s)^n$, i.e.
   $$G_s = \left(\frac{G_{ul}}{G_{ll}}\right)^{1/n} - 1 \tag{3}$$
   (the paper's own inline example uses $P_0=100$, ratio $1.1$ → next grids $110$, $121$). Grid cell prices are $g_i = G_{ll} + G_s\,(i-1)$ (Eq. 11, equal-distance) or $g_i = G_{ll}(1+G_s)^i$ (Eq. 12, equal-ratio).

3. **Capital split between spot and cash.** Capital divides into initial spot purchase $S_0$ (to sell as price rises) and initial cash $C_0$ (to buy as price falls): $F_0 = S_0 + C_0$ (Eq. 4). Grid counts above/below the start: $n = n_u + n_l$ (Eq. 5), with $n_u = (G_{ul}-P_0)/G_s$ (Eq. 6) and $n_l = (P_0-G_{ll})/G_s$ (Eq. 7). With per-grid volume $G_v$: $S_0 = G_v\, n_u\, P_0$ (Eq. 8) and $C_0 = G_v \cdot \frac{(P_0-G_s)+G_{ll}}{2}\cdot n_l$ (Eq. 9), giving
   $$G_v = \frac{F_0}{\frac{(P_0-G_s)+G_{ll}}{2}\, n_l + n_u P_0} \tag{10}$$
   Worked example ($F_0=10{,}000$, $P_0=100$, $G_{ul}=130$, $G_{ll}=70$, $n=12$, $G_s=5$): $n_u=(130-100)/5=6$, $n_l=(100-70)/5=6$; $G_v = 10000 / \{\frac{95+70}{2}\cdot 6 + 6\cdot 100\} = 10000/(495+600) \approx 9.13$ units per grid; $S_0 = 9.13\cdot 6\cdot 100 \approx 5479$, $C_0 \approx 4521$.

4. **The proposed Flexible Grid.** The core contribution is an **asymmetric grid split at $P_0$**: the number of upper grids $n_u$ and lower grids $n_l$ are free parameters (not derived from spacing), and each half has its own spacing ratio — upper ratio $G_{su} \in (0,1)$, lower ratio $G_{sl} > 1$ (Eqs. 13–14; the PDF renders these equations garbled — consistent with the stated properties, the upper half satisfies $G_{ul} = P_0\,\frac{1-G_{su}^{\,n_u+1}}{1-G_{su}}$ so upper spacing **shrinks geometrically as price rises**, and the lower half satisfies $G_{ll} = P_0 / G_{sl}^{\,n_l}$ so lower spacing **shrinks as price falls**). Design intent: **sell more frequently when price is high, buy cheap spot more frequently when price is low** — capturing the advantages of equal-distance (middle section) and equal-ratio (lower section) grids simultaneously, plus a novel dense-at-top upper section, so it outperforms whether the market is sideways, rising, or falling.

5. **SSO parameter search.** **Simplified Swarm Optimization** (Yeh, 2009) finds the optimal flexible-grid parameters $(G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l)$ per market window. SSO's update rule for variable $j$ of solution $i$ uses a uniform random $\rho \in [0,1]$ and thresholds $C_g < C_p < C_w$:
   $$x_{i,j}^{t+1} = \begin{cases} g_j & \rho \in [0, C_g) \\ p_{i,j} & \rho \in [C_g, C_p) \\ x_{i,j}^{t} & \rho \in [C_p, C_w) \\ x & \rho \in [C_w, 1) \end{cases} \tag{2}$$
   where $g_j$ = global best, $p_{i,j}$ = personal best, $x$ = fresh random value in the variable's bounds. Worked example with the paper's final setting $(C_g,C_p,C_w)=(0.5,0.6,0.7)$: draw $\rho=0.42$ → take gbest; $\rho=0.55$ → take pbest; $\rho=0.65$ → keep current; $\rho=0.83$ → random restart. **Objective** (Eq. 15): maximize final wealth from spot $S_j$, price $P_j$, and cash $C_j$ after $j$ periods (printed as $\max\; S_j \times P_j \times C_j$; the economically meaningful terminal wealth is $S_j P_j + C_j$). **Constraint** (Eq. 16): every grid step must beat the trading-cost rate $h\%$: $g_{i+1} - g_i > h\% \times g_{i+1}$ — e.g. with $g_{i+1}=105$, $g_i=100$, $h=0.1\%$: $5 > 0.105$ ✓. Bounds (Eqs. 17–19): experiment 1 uses $P_0\times105\% < G_{ul} < P_0\times130\%$ and $P_0\times70\% < G_{ll} < P_0\times95\%$; experiment 2 widens to $150\%/50\%$; grid counts bounded by $10 < n_l < [P_0 \times 100/(\max P_x \times 1.3)] - 10$ — set to keep error space and avoid overfitting the later ANN.

6. **ANN learns the SSO mapping.** SSO answers are expensive per window, so the paper trains neural networks to map **8 market-state inputs** (period high, period low, average price, average volume, price change, volume change, price std. dev., volume std. dev.) to the **4 grid parameters** ($G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l$). Training data: SSO-optimal parameters recorded every **30 days with moving stride 5** over ten years of each index → **~500 samples per index**; first 9 years train, last year validates. Two architectures compared: **FNN** (3 hidden layers × 500 nodes, sigmoid, adam, MSE loss, 300 epochs, batch 40; training rows shuffled) and **LSTM** (3 layers of 256/128/64, relu, adam, MSE, 300 epochs, batch 32; sequential order kept). Fit metrics: $MSE = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2$ (Eq. 20) and $R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - f_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}$ (Eq. 21). Example: predictions $\{128, 131\}$ vs. targets $\{130, 130\}$ → $MSE = ((−2)^2 + 1^2)/2 = 2.5$. **FNN beats LSTM on most indices** (e.g. S&P 500 $G_{ul}$ MSE 129,510 vs. 186,366; $R^2$ mostly 94–99%), with Nasdaq 100 the exception where LSTM fits better.

7. **Experiment 1 — fixed parameters.** On five indices (**S&P 500, Nasdaq 100, DJIA, Euro Stoxx 50, Shanghai Composite, 2011–2022**) with identical bounds and grid counts, the flexible grid beats equal-distance and equal-ratio grids on ROI, accumulated wealth, and Sharpe everywhere. E.g. S&P 500 at $(G_{ul},G_{ll})=(P_0\times1.3, P_0\times0.7)$: ROI **31.69%** (flexible) vs. **27.93%** (equal-distance) vs. **22.23%** (equal-ratio); Nasdaq at $\pm50\%$ bounds: **71.89%** vs. 66.02% vs. 51.18%. Even in the falling Shanghai market the flexible grid loses least (−33.90% vs. −38.94% / −38.53%).

8. **Experiment 2 — SSO-selected parameters.** Wiring SSO (10 runs × 20 generations × 100 solutions) to all three grid types lifts every ROI dramatically and preserves the ranking flexible > equal-distance > equal-ratio: S&P 500 ROI reaches **82.86%/90.27%** (narrow/wide bounds) for the flexible grid vs. 66.38%/77.20% equal-distance; Nasdaq **111.65%/127.27%**; Shanghai turns positive at **58.39%/80.95%**. Sharpe ratios roughly triple vs. fixed parameters (S&P: 0.439 vs. 0.118). A staged tuning study (Tables 6–8) fixed the SSO thresholds: giving **gbest the largest probability (0.5)** and the **random new solution the second largest (0.2)** produced the best and most robust ROI, final $(C_g,C_p,C_w)=(0.5,0.6,0.7)$.

9. **Final benchmark — 9 methods.** ROI (Eq. 22, trading costs included), maximum drawdown (MDD), volatility $Vol = \sigma[\text{return}]$ (Eq. 23), and Sharpe $= \text{return}/\sigma[\text{return}]$ (Eq. 24) compared across Buy&Sell, Sell&Buy, **GTSbot** [10], **Ichimoku (IK/IWOC)** [12], **FG-FNN**, **FG-LSTM**, equal-distance, equal-ratio, and plain flexible grid. **FG-FNN posts top-three ROI on every index** (S&P 11.52%, Nasdaq 11.73%, DJIA 8.85%, Euro Stoxx 12.98%, Shanghai −3.13% — the smallest loss besides shorting in the falling market), top-three volatility (0.0169–0.0215), and **the best Sharpe ratio on four of five indices** (S&P **6.83**, Nasdaq **5.65**, DJIA **4.12**, Euro Stoxx **7.50**). Example Sharpe check: S&P FG-FNN return 11.52% with σ = 0.01687 → $0.1152/0.01687 \approx 6.83$ ✓.

10. **Limitations acknowledged.** FG-FNN's MDD is sometimes elevated (S&P 6.46% vs. GTSbot's 0.52%) for two stated reasons: high return brings high risk, and **a single badly predicted parameter set can cause a large one-period drop**; low volatility across periods suggests such MDD events are isolated outliers. FG-LSTM shows a catastrophic 49.03% MDD on Nasdaq. The paper concludes AI-assisted parameter adaptation brings genuine breakthroughs to grid trading and calls for future work pushing parameter adjustment closer to (or beyond) human judgment.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### Flexible (asymmetric dual-ratio) grid structure
The grid splits at $P_0$ into an upper half with ratio $G_{su}\in(0,1)$ (spacing shrinks as price rises → denser sells at highs) and a lower half with ratio $G_{sl}>1$ (spacing shrinks as price falls → denser buys at lows), with $n_u$ and $n_l$ set independently rather than derived.

**Why it matters:** This is the paper's **core structural innovation** — it merges mean-reversion (equal-distance-like middle) and trend-capturing (ratio-like tails) behaviors into one grid, and with *identical* parameters it already beats both classic grids on all five indices in all three metrics, so the edge is architectural, not just from tuning.

### Delayed entry/exit as the profit mechanism
The paper attributes the fixed-parameter wins to the flexible grid "appropriately delaying the entry and exit timing due to its trading structure," fixing the stated drawback of classic grids entering and exiting the market prematurely.

**Why it matters:** It identifies the **causal mechanism** rather than just reporting numbers — spacing geometry controls *when* capital is committed, which is the actionable design lever for anyone building grid systems.

### SSO as a cheap parameter optimizer
SSO's four-way update (gbest / pbest / keep / random restart, Eq. 2) tunes $(G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l)$ per 30-day window under profit-vs-fee and bound constraints. The staged experiments show **gbest exploitation matters most** and **random-restart diversity matters second** — final $(C_g,C_p,C_w)=(0.5,0.6,0.7)$.

**Why it matters:** ROI roughly **doubles-to-triples** vs. fixed parameters (S&P 31.7% → 82.9%), proving parameter adaptivity dominates structure choice in magnitude; and the tuning methodology (ratio splits 7:1:1:1 → 5:3:1:1 → 3:3:3:1) is a reusable recipe for setting SSO thresholds.

### Learning the market-state → parameters mapping (FG-FNN)
Instead of running SSO live, an FNN learns to emit grid parameters from 8 descriptive statistics of the recent window. FNN generalizes better than LSTM on 4 of 5 indices (R² mostly 94–99%).

**Why it matters:** This makes the strategy **deployable in real time** — one forward pass replaces a swarm search — and delivers the best Sharpe ratio of all nine benchmarked methods on four indices, i.e., the best reward per unit of risk.

### Fee-aware constraint on grid spacing
Constraint $g_{i+1}-g_i > h\%\times g_{i+1}$ (Eq. 16) guarantees every completed grid cycle clears its trading cost.

**Why it matters:** Grid strategies live or die on fees — without this constraint an optimizer will happily create ultra-dense grids whose per-cycle spread is eaten by costs; it converts fee-awareness into a hard feasibility bound on $G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l$.

### Multi-market, multi-metric validation
Five major indices spanning the US, Europe, and China over 2011–2022, evaluated on ROI, accumulated wealth, MDD, volatility, and Sharpe against 7 external/baseline methods.

**Why it matters:** Grid papers often validate on one crypto pair or one FX pair; showing consistent ranking across **rising (Nasdaq), sideways (Euro Stoxx), and falling (Shanghai)** markets is much stronger evidence that the structure generalizes across regimes.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

### Grid Trading Operation Mechanism (baseline engine)

**Goal:** Earn the spread repeatedly by auto-buying at lower grid lines and auto-selling at upper grid lines inside a price range.

**Inputs:** $F_0$ (capital), $P_0$ (initial price), $G_{ul}$, $G_{ll}$ (range bounds), $n$ (grid count), price stream. **Outputs:** Trade log, final spot $S_j$ and cash $C_j$, settled P&L.

**Steps:**

1. **Compute spacing** — $G_s = (G_{ul}-G_{ll})/n$ (Eq. 1) for equal-distance (or the ratio form, Eq. 3). This fixes where every grid line sits: $g_i = G_{ll} + G_s(i-1)$.
2. **Split capital** — compute $n_u, n_l$ (Eqs. 6–7), then per-grid volume $G_v$ (Eq. 10), then buy initial spot $S_0 = G_v n_u P_0$ and reserve cash $C_0$ (Eqs. 8–9). Spot backs the sell orders above; cash backs the buy orders below.
3. **Place initial orders** — sell orders on every grid line above current price, buy orders on every line below.
4. **React to price hits** — price rises to a grid line → sell one grid volume, update $S_j, C_j$, place a buy order back at the vacated line. Price falls to a line → buy, update, place a sell order back at the vacated line. Each up-down round trip through a line pair pockets one grid spread.
5. **Repeat** until stop condition. Even if price returns exactly to $P_0$, completed round trips have banked spreads (the paper's Fig. 3(e) example banks **7 grid spreads** on a round trip).
6. **Settle** — either keep current spot+cash (recommended when price is low) or liquidate spot at market (the method used in this study's accounting).

**Worked example:** $F_0=10{,}000$, $P_0=100$, $G_{ul}=130$, $G_{ll}=70$, $n=12$ → $G_s=5$ (step 1); $n_u=n_l=6$, $G_v \approx 9.13$, $S_0\approx 5479$, $C_0 \approx 4521$ (step 2). Price rises 100→105: sell 9.13 units → cash += 958.7 (step 4). Price falls 105→100: buy 9.13 units back for 913 → **banked spread ≈ 45.7 (= $G_v \times G_s$)** minus fees. Ten such round trips ≈ 457 profit with price ending where it started.

### Flexible Grid Construction

**Goal:** Build a grid whose line density adapts to price level — dense sells near the top, dense buys near the bottom — fixing premature entry/exit of uniform grids.

**Inputs:** $F_0$, $P_0$, $G_{ul}$, $G_{ll}$, and freely chosen $n_u$, $n_l$. **Outputs:** The set of grid line prices $\{g_i\}$ above and below $P_0$.

**Steps:**

1. **Choose grid counts directly** — unlike classic grids, $n_u$ and $n_l$ are independent inputs (this is what SSO/ANN later optimizes), not consequences of a single spacing.
2. **Solve upper ratio $G_{su}\in(0,1)$** — from Eq. (13) so the $n_u$ upper lines span $P_0 \to G_{ul}$ with each successive step scaled by $G_{su}$: steps $P_0 G_{su}, P_0 G_{su}^2, \dots$ (spacing shrinks going up, so sells fire more frequently at highs). Consistent with the paper's stated properties: $G_{ul} = P_0\,\frac{1-G_{su}^{\,n_u+1}}{1-G_{su}}$.
3. **Solve lower ratio $G_{sl}>1$** — from Eq. (14) so the $n_l$ lower lines span $P_0 \to G_{ll}$ geometrically: $g_{-k} = P_0/G_{sl}^{k}$, i.e., $G_{sl} = (P_0/G_{ll})^{1/n_l}$ (absolute spacing shrinks going down, so buys fire more frequently at lows).
4. **Run the baseline engine** (previous algorithm, steps 2–6) on these non-uniform lines.

**Worked example:** $P_0=100$, $G_{ll}=70$, $n_l=6$ → $G_{sl} = (100/70)^{1/6} = e^{\ln(1.4286)/6} \approx 1.0613$. Lower lines: $100/1.0613 = 94.2$, then $88.8$, $83.7$, $78.8$, $74.3$, $70.0$. Successive gaps: 5.8, 5.4, 5.1, 4.9, 4.5, 4.3 — buys pack tighter as price falls, so the cash reserve accumulates proportionally more cheap spot near the bottom than an equal-distance grid (fixed gap 5.0) would.

### SSO for Optimal Flexible-Grid Parameters

**Goal:** Find $(G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l)$ maximizing terminal wealth over a historical window, subject to fee and bound constraints.

**Inputs:** Price series of $j$ periods, $F_0$, $P_0$, bounds from Table 1, SSO settings ($N_{sol}=100$ solutions, 20 generations, 10 runs, $(C_g,C_p,C_w)=(0.5,0.6,0.7)$). **Outputs:** Best parameter vector $X = (G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l)$ and its ROI.

**Steps:**

1. **Encode solutions** — each candidate is $X = (x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4) = (G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l)$; e.g., $X=(150,100,10,30)$ means bounds 150/100 with 10 upper and 30 lower grids (Fig. 6).
2. **Initialize population** — 100 random vectors inside the bounds (Eqs. 17–19), rejecting any violating the fee constraint $g_{i+1}-g_i > h\%\,g_{i+1}$ (Eq. 16).
3. **Evaluate fitness** — simulate the flexible grid over the window; fitness is terminal wealth from $S_j, P_j, C_j$ (Eq. 15).
4. **Update every variable of every solution** by Eq. (2): draw $\rho\sim U[0,1]$ per variable; copy from gbest if $\rho<0.5$, from pbest if $\rho<0.6$, keep if $\rho<0.7$, else re-randomize within bounds. The random branch prevents trapping in local optima.
5. **Track pbest/gbest** — after evaluation, update each solution's personal best and the global best.
6. **Repeat** for 20 generations; take gbest of the best of 10 independent runs.

**Worked example:** Generation $t$: gbest $=(130, 72, 12, 25)$, one solution's pbest $=(126, 75, 14, 22)$, current $=(120, 80, 11, 20)$. Draws per variable: $\rho = (0.31, 0.55, 0.68, 0.91)$ → new solution takes $x_1$ from gbest (130), $x_2$ from pbest (75), keeps $x_3$ (11), re-randomizes $x_4$ within $[10, \text{ub}]$, say 28 → candidate $(130, 75, 11, 28)$. Simulate → if its wealth beats pbest/gbest, it replaces them; iterate.

### FG-FNN: Training the Network that Auto-Adjusts Grid Parameters

**Goal:** Replace per-window SSO searches with a neural network that instantly outputs near-optimal grid parameters from a summary of recent market conditions.

**Inputs (8):** period high, period low, average price, average traded quantity, price change (first − last), quantity change, price std. dev., quantity std. dev. **Outputs (4):** $G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l$.

**Steps:**

1. **Generate labels with SSO** — slide a 30-day window with stride 5 across ten years of each index; run the SSO algorithm (above) per window → ~500 (market-state, optimal-parameters) pairs per index.
2. **Split** — first 9 years train, last year validates. FNN rows are shuffled (breaks temporal autocorrelation); LSTM keeps sequence order (its design needs it).
3. **Train** — FNN: 3 hidden layers × 500 nodes, sigmoid activation, adam optimizer, MSE loss (Eq. 20), 300 epochs, batch 40. LSTM: layers 256/128/64, relu, adam, MSE, 300 epochs, batch 32. Backpropagation computes loss gradients w.r.t. weights; gradient descent updates them until convergence.
4. **Validate fit** — MSE (Eq. 20) and $R^2$ (Eq. 21) per output variable. FNN wins on 4/5 indices; LSTM wins on Nasdaq 100.
5. **Deploy** — each period, feed the current 8-value market summary; the network emits the 4 grid parameters; rebuild the flexible grid; trade; evaluate with ROI/MDD/volatility/Sharpe (Eqs. 22–24).

**Worked example:** Suppose a validation window gives inputs (high 4720, low 4380, avg 4550, avg qty 2.1B, price change −180, qty change +0.2B, price σ 96, qty σ 0.3B). Trained FNN outputs, say, $(G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l) = (5010, 3980, 14, 22)$. With true SSO labels $(4950, 4020, 15, 21)$, squared errors are $(60^2, 40^2, 1, 1) = (3600, 1600, 1, 1)$ — magnitudes matching Table 14's reported per-variable MSE scale (bounds in the $10^4$–$10^6$ range, grid counts in the $10^2$ range). The grid built from the predicted parameters then trades the next period; on the S&P 500 this pipeline yields ROI 11.52%, σ 0.0169, Sharpe 6.83 — the study's best risk-adjusted result.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **Asymmetric dual-ratio spacing for the DGT prototype** — replace the uniform spacing in an existing grid implementation with the Flexible Grid Construction algorithm (Section 4, steps 2–3): dense sells near the range top, dense buys near the bottom, with $n_u \ne n_l$ as free parameters.
  - **Strong:** sideways and volatile-rising markets — with identical parameters it beat both classic grids on all five indices (Tables 3–5, e.g. S&P ROI 31.69% vs. 27.93%). *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** falling markets, relative to other grids — smallest loss on Shanghai (−33.90% vs. −38.94%) because tighter lower spacing averages down at better prices: for geometric lower lines $g_{-k}=P_0/G_{sl}^k$, average purchase price is lower than the equal-distance average $\frac{(P_0-G_s)+G_{ll}}{2}$ over the same range. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** sustained trend that exits the range — once price crosses $G_{ul}$ (all spot sold) or $G_{ll}$ (all cash deployed), no further trades occur; per Eq. (15) wealth then moves as $S_j \cdot P_j$ only, so a breakout below $G_{ll}$ leaves maximum spot exposure exactly when price keeps falling. Shanghai's absolute ROI stayed negative (−33.90%) despite winning relatively. *(From paper + From AI)*

- **SSO as the grid-parameter optimizer** — use the SSO algorithm (Section 4, all steps) to tune $(G_{ul}, G_{ll}, n_u, n_l)$ over rolling windows in any grid backtester; adopt the paper's staged 7:1:1:1 → 5:3:1:1 threshold-tuning recipe and the final $(0.5, 0.6, 0.7)$ setting.
  - **Strong:** any market where parameters were previously hand-set — SSO parameters lifted S&P ROI from 31.69% to 82.86% and turned Shanghai from −33.90% to +58.39% (Tables 9 vs. 3). *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** small search spaces — with only 4 variables, 100×20 evaluations suffice; the four-branch update (Eq. 2) needs one random draw and one copy per variable, far cheaper per iteration than PSO's velocity arithmetic. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** in-sample overfitting — parameters maximizing Eq. (15) on one window need not generalize; the paper itself adds bound constraints (Eqs. 17–19) explicitly "to avoid overfitting of subsequent ANN training." A window-optimal $G_{ll}$ hugging the window's minimum price fails when the next window's minimum is lower (extreme-value statistics: the sample minimum underestimates the true support of the distribution). *(From paper + From AI)*

- **Market-state → parameter regression for regime adaptation** — train an FNN (Section 4, FG-FNN algorithm) on SSO-labeled windows so the live system re-parameterizes each period from 8 cheap summary statistics; applicable to any strategy with a small tunable parameter vector, not just grids.
  - **Strong:** regime-shifting markets — FG-FNN achieved the best Sharpe on 4/5 indices (S&P 6.83 vs. GTSbot 3.75) and top-three volatility everywhere (Tables 18–19). *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** latency-sensitive deployment — one forward pass ($O(\text{weights})$, milliseconds) replaces a 100×20-evaluation swarm search per rebalance. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** single bad prediction → large one-period drawdown — the paper states FG-FNN's elevated MDD arises when "the parameters predicted on one period are particularly poor" (Section 4.4 discussion; FG-LSTM hit 49.03% MDD on Nasdaq). *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** distribution shift — MSE-trained regression assumes validation inputs resemble training inputs; the 8 input features are unbounded (prices, volumes), so an unprecedented volatility spike puts the network in extrapolation, where $R^2$ measured in-sample (94–99%) says nothing. Normalizing inputs to returns/ratios would mitigate. *(From AI)*

- **Fee-aware spacing constraint in any grid optimizer** — port Eq. (16), $g_{i+1}-g_i > h\%\times g_{i+1}$, as a hard feasibility check (SSO algorithm, step 2) into any grid parameter search.
  - **Strong:** high-fee venues and dense grids — guarantees every completed cycle nets $> 0$: cycle profit $= (g_{i+1}-g_i) - h\%(g_{i+1}+g_i) \ge (g_{i+1}-g_i) - 2h\% \, g_{i+1} > -h\% g_{i+1} + h\% g_i \approx 0$ margin per the constraint (exact if fees charged on the sell leg only, as Eq. 16 assumes). *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** zero/rebate-fee venues — the constraint binds nothing and the optimizer can still choose spacing below the bid-ask spread + slippage, which Eq. (16) does not model; replace $h\%$ with an effective cost including spread. *(From AI)*

- **Sliding-window SSO labeling as a data-generation pattern** — the 30-day window / stride-5 scheme (FG-FNN algorithm, step 1) converts any expensive offline optimizer into supervised training data (~500 samples from 10 years), reusable for labeling optimal stop-losses, position sizes, or DGT reset thresholds.
  - **Strong:** long histories with slow regime drift — overlapping windows multiply sample count ×6 vs. non-overlapping (stride 5 vs. 30), and the paper's 94–99% validation $R^2$ shows the mapping is learnable at this sample size. *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** overlapping windows share 25 of 30 days, so adjacent samples are heavily autocorrelated — the effective sample size is far below 500 (for ~83% overlap, adjacent-label correlation is high, inflating $R^2$ and understating generalization error; the paper's row shuffling for FNN spreads correlated samples across train batches but does not remove train/validation leakage at the 9-year boundary). Use purged/embargoed splits. *(From AI)*

- **Nine-method, four-metric evaluation harness** — adopt the paper's benchmark set (B&S, S&B, external bots, fixed grids, adaptive grids) and metric set (ROI Eq. 22, MDD, volatility Eq. 23, Sharpe Eq. 24) as the standard comparison table for grid-strategy experiments in this repo.
