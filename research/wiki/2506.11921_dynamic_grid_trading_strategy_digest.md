## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation.** Cryptocurrencies exhibit **substantially higher volatility** than traditional stock markets (Bitcoin daily volume ≈ **$37 billion** as of Aug 17, 2024), and many traders exploit this volatility with **grid trading**. The paper first analyzes the *expected value* of the traditional grid strategy mathematically, finds it to be **zero** under neutral assumptions, and then proposes an improved **Dynamic Grid-based Trading (DGT)** strategy that outperforms both traditional grid trading and buy-and-hold.

2. **Traditional geometric grid setup.** The strategy uses a **geometric grid** with $n$ grids ($n+1$ levels) of equal ratio $k$. Starting at price $P$ with $m$ levels above and $n-m$ below, levels sit at $P(1+k)^i$ for $i = -(n-m), \dots, m$. Investing $M$ USDT, the strategy immediately spends $M \cdot \frac{m}{n}$ to buy crypto and keeps $M \cdot \frac{n-m}{n}$ in USDT. **Worked example:** $M = 600$ USDT, $n = 6$, $m = 3$, $P = 100$, $k = 5\%$ → levels at $\{86.38, 90.70, 95.24, 100, 105, 110.25, 115.76\}$; initial buy $= 600 \cdot \tfrac{3}{6} = 300$ USDT of crypto, $300$ USDT kept in cash.

3. **Trading and termination rules.** A trade fires only when price crosses a "gray" (inactive) grid level: on a rise crossing the $G_i$-th gray level from the top, sell $\frac{1}{G_i}$ of held crypto; on a fall crossing the $G_j$-th gray level from the bottom, spend $\frac{1}{G_j}$ of held USDT to buy. Levels swap black/gray so the grid **self-adjusts without manual intervention**. The traditional strategy **terminates** when price exits the grid: above the top → all in USDT (principal + arbitrage profit, no crypto); below the bottom → all capital converted to crypto (plus arbitrage profit in USDT).

4. **Claim 1 — infinite resources make grids profitable.** With **infinite capital and time**, grid trading is inherently profitable: orders always exist above and below price, every fluctuation is monetized (buy low / sell high per oscillation), and infinite time waits out any drawdown. This claim motivates *not stopping* the strategy at the boundary.

5. **Claim 2 — zero expected value with finite grids.** Assuming price moves up or down by $k$ with **equal probability (50-50)**, no fees, and full liquidation below the grid, the expected value of a finite grid strategy is **exactly zero**. If price rises linearly through the top, the profit is
$$P_u = \frac{M}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n/2} i \tag{1}$$
and if it falls linearly through the bottom, the loss is
$$L_l = \frac{M}{n}\left[\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{2}-1} i\right] \tag{2}$$
so the expectation ignoring arbitrage profit is
$$E(G) = \frac{1}{2}(P_u - L_l) = -\frac{M}{n}\left(\frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4}\right) \tag{3}$$
**Worked example ($M=600$, $n=6$):** $P_u = 100 \cdot (1+2+3) = 600$; $L_l = 100 \cdot [9 + (1+2)] = 1200$; $E(G) = \tfrac{1}{2}(600 - 1200) = -300 = -100 \cdot (4.5 - 1.5)$. So the strategy needs more than $\frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4} = 3$ arbitrage round-trips to be net positive.

6. **Random-walk proof that arbitrage exactly cancels the deficit.** The number of trades before the grid terminates is reframed as a coin-toss problem: expected tosses until heads lead tails (or vice versa) by $\frac{n}{2}$. Modeling this as a finite automaton with states $q_i$ (head–tail difference $= i$) and expected values $E_i$, the recurrence is
$$E_i = 2E_{i-1} - E_{i-2} - 2 \tag{4}$$
proved by induction to give $E_m = E_0 - m^2$. With terminal condition $E_{n/2} = 0$, the expected number of transactions is $E_0 = \frac{n^2}{4}$. Subtracting the transaction cost of the $\frac{n}{2}$ directional moves and dividing by 2 (a buy+sell pair = one arbitrage) gives
$$\frac{\frac{n^2}{4} - \frac{n}{2}}{2} = \frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4} \tag{5}$$
which **exactly equals** the required number of arbitrages from Eq. (3). Hence $E(\text{grid strategy}) = 0$ (Eq. 6). **Worked example ($n=6$):** expected transactions $= 36/4 = 9$; $(9-3)/2 = 3$ arbitrages expected — exactly the 3 needed to break even. With fees added, expectation turns *negative*.

7. **DGT strategy.** The fix: **never terminate**. When price breaks the **upper limit**, the initial capital is fully recovered (all sold) and **reinvested in a new grid centered at the current price**. When price breaks the **lower limit**, the strategy **holds the crypto** (buy-more-when-cheap principle) and starts a new grid using accumulated arbitrage profits as new principal. A `wallet` variable tracks cumulative arbitrage profit; `input money` tracks total invested capital; both update at each grid reset.

8. **Backtest setup.** Data: **Binance spot 1-minute klines** (`https://api.binance.com/api/v3/klines`) for **BTC and ETH, January 2021 – July 2024**; at most one transaction per minute is assumed. Transaction fee **0.0008** (OKX Level 1 maker fee). Backtesting framework built in **Python**; parameter sweeps over *grid size* (geometric step ratio) and *grid numbers half* (levels above/below center at activation).

9. **Results.** DGT's **IRR stays consistently positive**, reaching **60–70%** at the best parameter combinations; ETH outperforms BTC (smaller market → more volatility → more grid profit). Versus buy-and-hold on BTC: **higher IRR and significantly lower MDD**. On ETH: IRR roughly comparable (ETH's strong uptrend favors holding) but MDD much better — when the market fell ≈ **80%**, DGT's drawdown was limited to ≈ **50%**. Versus a traditional grid whose limits perfectly contain the whole price range (BTC 10,000–80,000; ETH 500–5,000): no-fee IRR fluctuates around a flat level, empirically confirming the zero-expectation theorem; with fees the traditional grid worsens; **DGT beats it even with the boundaries chosen with hindsight**, thanks to profit reinvestment and fuller capital use.

10. **Parameter findings and limitations.** **Too-small grid size** → fees consume the per-cycle arbitrage profit; **too-large grid size + many levels** → grid rarely activates, low crypto holdings, poor IRR. Balancing grid size against grid count is the central tuning problem. The authors note the backtest period's strong bull market flatters spot grid results, and future work is to **derive DGT's profit probability and expected return analytically**. Code released on GitHub.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### Zero expected value of traditional grid trading
Under a symmetric ±k random walk without fees, the expected arbitrage count $\frac{n^2}{8}-\frac{n}{4}$ exactly offsets the expected boundary-exit loss, so $E = 0$; with fees, expectation is strictly negative.

**Why it matters:** **Kills the naive belief** that grid trading is intrinsically profitable "free money from volatility." Any real edge must come from something beyond the mechanism itself — regime, trend capture, or reinvestment — which is precisely what DGT adds.

### Random-walk / finite-automaton framing of grid dynamics
Grid transaction counts are mapped to a classic gambler's-ruin-style coin toss problem, solved with the recurrence $E_m = E_0 - m^2$ by induction.

**Why it matters:** Gives a **reusable analytical tool**: any grid variant's expected trade count and break-even condition can be computed by the same automaton method, before writing a single line of backtest code.

### Never-terminate reset rule (the DGT core idea)
Instead of liquidating at boundaries, DGT re-centers the grid at the breakout price — recovering capital on upside breaks, holding coins on downside breaks.

**Why it matters:** Converts the strategy from a **bounded-range bet** (which has zero expectation) into a hybrid of grid arbitrage plus long exposure, which is what produces the observed 60–70% IRR and turns Claim 1's "infinite time" intuition into a finite-capital implementation.

### Fee-versus-grid-size trade-off
Small grid size → fee ratio dominates per-cycle profit (per-cycle gross ≈ $k$, fee cost ≈ 2×0.0008); large grid size → infrequent triggers, high volatility of results, low holdings.

**Why it matters:** Identifies the **single most important tuning axis** for any grid deployment; the profitability floor $k \gg 2 \times \text{fee}$ is a hard, checkable constraint before any backtest.

### Drawdown control as the real advantage over buy-and-hold
On ETH, DGT's IRR was only comparable to buy-and-hold, but MDD dropped from ≈80% to ≈50%; on BTC both IRR and MDD improved.

**Why it matters:** The strategy's honest claim is **risk-adjusted** outperformance, not raw-return dominance — critical for position-sizing and leverage decisions downstream.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

### Traditional Geometric Grid Trading

**Goal:** Harvest oscillation profit by mechanically buying at lower grid levels and selling at higher ones within a fixed price range.

**Inputs:** capital $M$ (USDT), grid count $n$, grid ratio $k$, start price $P$, levels above $m$. **Outputs:** final portfolio (USDT + crypto) and accumulated arbitrage profit; strategy state ends when price exits the range.

**Steps:**

1. **Build levels** — place $n+1$ levels geometrically: level $i$ at $P(1+k)^i$, $i$ from $-(n-m)$ to $m$. Geometric spacing keeps the *percentage* gap constant, so the grid handles different volatility scales uniformly.
2. **Initial allocation** — buy crypto with $M\cdot\frac{m}{n}$, hold $M\cdot\frac{n-m}{n}$ in USDT. Allocation is proportional to how many sell levels exist above (each sell level needs inventory to sell).
3. **Sell rule** — when price rises across the $G_i$-th gray level counted from the top, sell $\frac{1}{G_i}$ of current crypto; recolor levels (crossed level becomes the active black one).
4. **Buy rule** — when price falls across the $G_j$-th gray level counted from the bottom, spend $\frac{1}{G_j}$ of current USDT on crypto; recolor likewise. The fractional rule keeps allocation consistent with the remaining level counts.
5. **Terminate** — price above top level → hold only USDT (principal + profit); price below bottom → all capital is in crypto plus banked arbitrage USDT. Strategy stops.

**Worked example:** $M=600$, $n=6$, $m=3$, $P=100$, $k=5\%$. Levels: $86.38, 90.70, 95.24, \mathbf{100}, 105, 110.25, 115.76$. Step 2: hold 3 units of crypto (300 USDT worth at $P{=}100$) + 300 USDT. Price rises to 105 (3rd gray level from top → $G_i = 3$): sell $\tfrac{1}{3}$ of crypto → sell 1 unit at 105, receive 105 USDT (cash = 405, crypto = 2 units). Price falls back through 100 (now 3rd gray from bottom → $G_j=3$): spend $\tfrac{1}{3}\cdot405 = 135$ USDT to buy 1.35 units at 100 (cash = 270, crypto = 3.35). That sell-at-105/buy-back-at-100 pair banked ≈ 5 USDT gross on the cycled unit — one arbitrage cycle; per Eq. (5), expect $\frac{36}{8}-\frac{6}{4}=3$ such cycles before the range breaks, exactly offsetting the expected exit loss of 300 USDT from Eq. (3).

### Zero-Expectation Proof (finite automaton method)

**Goal:** Compute the expected number of grid transactions before the price exits the range, to show arbitrage profit exactly cancels boundary loss.

**Inputs:** grid half-width $\frac{n}{2}$ in levels; symmetric move probability $\frac{1}{2}$. **Outputs:** expected transaction count $E_0$ and expected arbitrage cycles.

**Steps:**

1. **Reframe as coin tossing** — each ±k price move is a fair coin flip; termination = heads leading tails by $\frac{n}{2}$ or vice versa.
2. **Build automaton** — state $q_i$ = current head−tail difference $i$; $E_i$ = expected further tosses from $q_i$; terminal state $q_{n/2}$ has $E_{n/2}=0$. Transition relations: $E_0 = E_1 + 1$, $E_1 = \frac{1}{2}(E_0+1)+\frac{1}{2}(E_2+1)$, giving recurrence $E_i = 2E_{i-1} - E_{i-2} - 2$.
3. **Solve by induction** — conjecture $E_m = E_0 - m^2$; base cases $E_1 = E_0-1$, $E_2 = E_0-4$ hold; inductive step $E_{k+2} = 2[E_0-(k+1)^2] - (E_0 - k^2) - 2 = E_0-(k+2)^2$. ✓
4. **Apply terminal condition** — $0 = E_{n/2} = E_0 - (\frac{n}{2})^2 \Rightarrow E_0 = \frac{n^2}{4}$.
5. **Convert to arbitrage cycles** — subtract the $\frac{n}{2}$ purely directional moves (they are cost, not arbitrage), divide by 2 (buy+sell = one cycle): $\left(\frac{n^2}{4}-\frac{n}{2}\right)/2 = \frac{n^2}{8}-\frac{n}{4}$, which equals the break-even requirement → $E(\text{grid}) = 0$.

**Worked example:** $n = 6$ → terminal state $q_3$. $E_0 = 36/4 = 9$ expected moves. Intermediate check: $E_1 = 9-1 = 8$, $E_2 = 9-4=5$, $E_3 = 9-9 = 0$ ✓. Arbitrage cycles $=(9-3)/2 = 3$; each cycle earns $\frac{M}{n}\cdot 1$ level-gap ≈ 100 USDT ⇒ expected arbitrage profit 300 USDT = exact magnitude of the expected exit loss $|E(G)| = 300$ from Eq. (3). Net zero.

### DGT (Dynamic Grid-based Trading) Strategy

**Goal:** Remove the termination that makes grid expectation zero, by resetting the grid at every boundary break so arbitrage never stops.

**Inputs:** grid size $k$, *grid numbers half* (levels above/below center), transaction fee (0.0008), price stream (1-min klines), initial capital. **Outputs:** cumulative `wallet` (arbitrage profits), `input money` (total invested), final IRR and MDD.

**Steps:**

1. **Set parameters & compute grid** — choose grid size and grid-numbers-half; build geometric levels around the start price (same as Traditional Grid steps 1–2).
2. **Process each price tick** — price up: execute sell if a grid level was crossed; price down: execute buy if a level was crossed (Traditional Grid steps 3–4, with fee 0.0008 applied per trade).
3. **Upper-limit break → reset with full recovery** — all inventory has been sold on the way up; principal plus profit is recovered and **fully reinvested** into a fresh grid centered at the current (higher) price.
4. **Lower-limit break → reset while holding** — do *not* liquidate; keep the accumulated crypto (staying true to buy-low principle) and open a new grid centered at the current (lower) price, funded by accumulated arbitrage profit as new principal.
5. **Bookkeeping per reset** — update `wallet` (cumulative arbitrage profit) and `input money` (cumulative invested capital); repeat from step 2 until data ends, then compute IRR/MDD per parameter combination.

**Worked example:** Capital 600 USDT, $k=5\%$, grid-numbers-half = 3, start price 100 (same grid as above). Price grinds up through 105, 110.25, 115.76 — each cross sells a fraction, banking ≈ 5 USDT-equivalent per cycle into `wallet`; above 115.76 the position is fully in USDT ≈ 615. **Reset:** re-center at 115.76, reinvest all 615 (input money stays 600, wallet ≈ +15). Later price crashes through the new lower bound at $115.76\cdot(1.05)^{-3} \approx 100$: the strategy is now fully in crypto — **it keeps the coins**, and opens a third grid centered at 100 funded by wallet profits. Over Jan 2021–Jul 2024 this loop yields IRR up to **60–70%** with MDD ≈ 50% vs. 80% market drawdown.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **Break-even fee gate as a pre-trade filter** — from the fee analysis (Section 4, DGT step 2): before deploying any grid, require grid size $k > c \cdot 2f$ (fee $f$, safety factor $c$). Use Eq. (5) to compute expected cycles and reject parameter combos whose expected fee drag exceeds expected arbitrage.
  - **Strong:** high-volatility assets with maker-fee tiers ≤ 0.08% — paper shows small grid size loses to fees while mid-size $k$ reaches 60–70% IRR (Figures 3–5) *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** any exchange offering fee rebates — per-cycle net profit ≈ $\frac{M}{n}k - 2f\cdot\text{tradesize}$ becomes positive at smaller $k$, allowing denser grids and more cycles per Eq. (5) *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** low-volatility regimes — expected cycles scale with realized variance; if per-minute moves rarely reach $k$, cycle count collapses while capital sits idle, so IRR → 0 even with favorable fees *(From AI)*

- **Automaton method for pricing any grid variant** — reuse the Zero-Expectation Proof (Section 4, steps 1–5) to analytically derive expected trade counts for variants: asymmetric grids ($m \neq n-m$), trailing grids, or futures grids. Change only the automaton's terminal states and transition probabilities.
  - **Strong:** design-time screening — closed-form $E_0 = n^2/4$-style results verify a backtest's trade counts and catch implementation bugs cheaply *(From paper — the paper itself validates its no-fee backtest IRR flatline against the theorem, Figure 7)*
  - **Strong:** biased-walk extension — replacing $p=\frac{1}{2}$ with drifted $p$ turns the recurrence into a solvable linear difference equation (gambler's ruin with bias), giving expected value of grids under trend *(From AI — standard result: absorption probabilities become $\frac{1-(q/p)^m}{1-(q/p)^{n}}$, fully checkable)*
  - **Weak:** price processes with jumps/gaps — the automaton assumes single-level ±k steps; a gap across multiple levels executes at worse prices than modeled, so the closed form overstates profit in thin markets *(From AI — jump size > k violates the unit-step Markov chain assumption underlying Eq. 4)*

- **DGT reset rule ported to other mean-reverting instruments** — apply DGT steps 3–4 (recover-and-recenter up, hold-and-recenter down) to FX pairs or ETF pairs instead of crypto spot.
  - **Strong:** assets with long-run upward drift (equity index ETFs) — downside "hold" leg accumulates inventory that drift eventually rescues, same mechanism that produced BTC/ETH gains 2021–2024 *(From paper — authors attribute high IRR partly to rising crypto prices, Section 3.2)*
  - **Weak:** assets that can trend to zero or delist (single alt-coins, single stocks) — the hold-below-grid rule has no stop-loss; expected loss of the hold leg is unbounded downward since inventory is never cut, i.e., MDD of the crypto leg → 100% under a permanent decline *(From AI — DGT step 4 converts all capital to the asset with no exit condition)*
  - **Weak:** prolonged bear market — paper's own ETH result shows DGT MDD still hit ≈50% during the 80% drawdown; capital efficiency drops because each downward reset shrinks the deployable principal to wallet profits only *(From paper, Section 4.1)*

- **Volatility-scaled grid size** — extend DGT step 1: set $k$ proportional to trailing realized volatility (e.g., $k = \lambda \sigma_{1min}\sqrt{T}$) rather than a fixed sweep value, re-evaluated at each grid reset.
  - **Strong:** regime shifts between quiet and volatile periods — keeps expected cycle frequency (Eq. 5 scaled by move probability) roughly constant, avoiding both the fee-drag zone (k too small when vol drops) and dead-grid zone (k too big when vol drops) identified in the paper's parameter sweep *(From AI — expected level crossings per unit time is a monotone function of $\sigma/k$, so fixing the ratio stabilizes trade counts; paper's Figures 3/5 show both failure zones)*
  - **Weak:** volatility estimation lag — after a vol spike, trailing σ overshoots and widens the grid exactly when reversion opportunities are densest; expected cycles fall just as they were most profitable *(From AI — σ estimator variance ~ $\sigma^4 \cdot 2/(N-1)$; lag is inherent to any trailing window)*

- **MDD-based capital layering on top of DGT** — use the paper's finding that DGT's edge is drawdown control (Section 4.1): allocate a base tranche to buy-and-hold and a volatility tranche to DGT, sized so blended MDD hits a target (e.g., 30%).
  - **Strong:** portfolio contexts with hard risk limits — BTC results show DGT dominates B&H on both IRR and MDD, so any blend weakly improves the risk-adjusted frontier versus pure B&H *(From paper, Section 4.1)*
  - **Weak:** strong sustained uptrend — ETH case shows DGT IRR ≤ B&H when trend dominates oscillation; the DGT tranche then pays fees and caps upside (sells inventory into the rally at each upper reset) *(From paper, Section 4.1)* 
  - **Weak:** correlation of the two tranches ≈ 1 in crashes — both legs are long the same asset; below the grid DGT is 100% long (step 4), so diversification benefit vanishes exactly at the MDD event *(From AI — at lower-limit break DGT's delta equals B&H's delta, so blended MDD ≥ min of the two, not less)*
