## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation.** Cryptocurrencies are far more volatile than traditional stock markets (Bitcoin daily volume ≈ **$37 billion** as of Aug 17, 2024), and many traders exploit that volatility with **grid trading**. The authors first analyze the mathematical expectation of the traditional grid strategy, prove it is **zero** under neutral assumptions, then design an improved **Dynamic Grid-based Trading (DGT)** strategy that outperforms both traditional grid trading and buy-and-hold.

2. **Traditional geometric grid setup.** The paper uses a **geometric grid**: with $n$ grids ($n+1$ levels), grid ratio $k$, and start price $P$, the levels are $P(1+k)^i$ for $i = -(n-m), \dots, m$, where $m$ levels sit above the start price. On starting the grid with total capital $M$ USDT, the strategy immediately spends $M \cdot \frac{m}{n}$ USDT buying crypto and keeps $M \cdot \frac{n-m}{n}$ in USDT. *Worked example:* $P = 100$, $k = 0.02$, $n = 6$, $m = 3$ → top level $= 100 \times 1.02^3 \approx 106.12$, bottom level $= 100 \times 1.02^{-3} \approx 94.23$; with $M = 600$ USDT, the strategy buys $600 \times 3/6 = 300$ USDT of crypto at start.

3. **Traditional grid algorithm and termination.** A trade fires only when price crosses a gray (inactive) grid level: crossing the $G_i$-th gray level from the top (rising) sells $\frac{1}{G_i}$ of held crypto; crossing the $G_j$-th gray level from the bottom (falling) spends $\frac{1}{G_j}$ of held USDT buying crypto. The crossed level becomes the new active (black) level. The strategy **terminates** when price exits the grid: above the top, the trader holds all USDT (principal + arbitrage profit, no crypto); below the bottom, all invested USDT has been converted to crypto (plus the arbitrage-profit USDT).

4. **Claim 1 — infinite resources make grid trading profitable.** With **infinite capital and time**, grid trading always yields positive returns: orders can be placed at every level forever, so every fluctuation is captured (buy low, sell high, repeatedly), and any drawdown can be waited out.

5. **Claim 2 — zero expected value with finite grids.** Assuming price moves up or down by $k$ with equal probability (50–50), no fees, and full liquidation below the grid, the paper proves the expected value of a finite grid strategy is **exactly zero**. If price rises linearly through the top, profit is
$$P_u = \frac{M}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2} i \tag{1}$$
If price falls linearly through the bottom, loss is
$$L_l = \frac{M}{n}\left[\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{2}-1} i\right] \tag{2}$$
so, ignoring arbitrage profits, the expectation is
$$E(G) = \frac{1}{2}(P_u - L_l) = -\frac{M}{n}\left(\frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4}\right) \tag{3}$$
*Worked example ($n=6$, $M=600$):* $P_u = \frac{600}{6}(1+2+3) = 600$; $L_l = \frac{600}{6}[3^2 + (1+2)] = 1200$; $E(G) = \frac{1}{2}(600 - 1200) = -300 = -100 \times (\frac{36}{8} - \frac{6}{4})$. So the strategy needs more than $\frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4}$ arbitrage round-trips (here **3**) to break even.

6. **Random-walk expected number of transactions.** Modeling price as a fair coin toss and asking how many tosses until heads lead tails by $\frac{n}{2}$ (or vice versa), the paper builds a finite automaton with states $q_i$ (head–tail difference $= i$) and expected values $E_i$ from state $q_i$. The recursion is
$$E_i = 2E_{i-1} - E_{i-2} - 2 \tag{4}$$
and induction proves $E_m = E_0 - m^2$. Setting the terminal condition $E_{n/2} = 0$ gives $E_0 = \frac{n^2}{4}$: the expected number of grid transactions before termination. Subtracting the $\frac{n}{2}$ directional transactions (price-difference cost) and dividing by 2 (a buy+sell pair = one arbitrage) gives
$$\frac{\frac{n^2}{4} - \frac{n}{2}}{2} = \frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4} \tag{5}$$
*Worked example ($n=6$):* $E_0 = 36/4 = 9$ expected transactions; $(9 - 3)/2 = 3$ expected arbitrages — exactly the break-even count from point 5. Expected arbitrage profit exactly offsets the expected directional loss, hence
$$E(\text{grid strategy}) = 0 \tag{6}$$

7. **The DGT strategy.** The zero-expectation proof shows the flaw is **termination**: stopping at the boundary abandons future arbitrage, and selling everything below the grid contradicts "buy more when price falls." DGT therefore **never stops**: whenever price breaks the upper or lower limit, the grid **resets with the current price as the new center**. On an upward break, the initial capital is fully recovered and reinvested in the new grid; on a downward break, the crypto is **held** (not sold) and the accumulated arbitrage profits become the principal of the new grid. A `wallet` variable tracks cumulative arbitrage profit and `input money` tracks total invested capital, both updated at each reset.

8. **Backtest setup.** Data: **Binance** spot klines API (`https://api.binance.com/api/v3/klines`), **1-minute candles, January 2021 – July 2024**, assets **BTC and ETH**, at most one transaction per minute, transaction fee **0.0008** (OKX Level-1 maker fee). The whole backtesting framework is written in **Python**; code is released on GitHub.

9. **Results vs. buy-and-hold.** DGT's IRR (annualized return) stays **consistently positive**, reaching **60–70%** in the best parameter regions. For **BTC**, DGT beats buy-and-hold on IRR *and* has significantly lower **MDD** (maximum drawdown). For **ETH**, IRR is only about level with buy-and-hold (ETH trended strongly upward), but drawdown control is much better: when the market fell ≈ **80%**, DGT limited drawdown to ≈ **50%**. ETH shows higher IRR than BTC overall, attributed to its greater volatility. Parameter effects: too-small grid size → fees eat the arbitrage profit; too-large grid size and grid-numbers-half → grid rarely activates, low crypto holdings, poor IRR.

10. **Results vs. traditional grid trading, and limitations.** Against a traditional grid whose bounds perfectly contain the whole 2021–2024 price range (BTC: 10,000–80,000; ETH: 500–5,000), the fee-free IRR fluctuates around a flat level — empirically confirming the **zero-expectation theorem** — and fees push it lower. DGT outperforms even this idealized traditional grid because it reinvests profits and uses capital more efficiently. Limitations: the strong results partly ride the crypto bull period; the zero-expectation proof assumes a symmetric 50–50 walk and no fees; a mathematical derivation of DGT's profit probability and expected return is left to **future work**.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### Zero expected value of traditional grid trading
Under a symmetric random walk (equal probability of moving up/down by $k$), no fees, and liquidation at the boundaries, the expected arbitrage count ($\frac{n^2}{8}-\frac{n}{4}$) exactly cancels the expected directional loss, so $E(\text{grid}) = 0$.

**Why it matters:** This is the paper's **core theoretical result** — it means retail grid trading with no market insight is, after fees, a **negative-expectation** game. Any profitable grid variant must break one of the proof's assumptions.

### Termination is the design flaw
The proof of Claim 1 (infinite resources → profit) vs. Claim 2 (finite grid → zero) isolates the difference: the finite strategy **stops harvesting arbitrage** at the boundaries and sells everything on a downward exit.

**Why it matters:** It converts a vague intuition ("grid bots lose when price trends") into a **precise diagnosis**, directly motivating the DGT reset rule instead of ad-hoc parameter tuning.

### Grid reset mechanism (DGT)
On boundary break, re-center the grid at the current price; recover-and-reinvest capital on upward breaks, hold crypto and use arbitrage profits as new principal on downward breaks.

**Why it matters:** This single mechanism turns a zero-expectation strategy into one with **60–70% IRR** in backtests — the entire performance gain comes from never abandoning arbitrage opportunities and compounding profits.

### Fee sensitivity vs. grid size trade-off
Small grid size → many trades, but the **0.0008 fee** consumes the small per-trade arbitrage margin. Large grid size (and large grid-numbers-half) → infrequent activation, low crypto exposure, poor IRR.

**Why it matters:** It identifies the **key tuning axis** for any practical grid deployment: grid spacing must be wide enough to clear fees but narrow enough to keep trade frequency and exposure up.

### Random-walk / finite-automaton analysis of trade counts
Reframing "how long until the grid terminates" as "expected coin tosses until a lead of $n/2$" and solving $E_m = E_0 - m^2$ via induction.

**Why it matters:** It supplies a **reusable analytical tool** — the same automaton technique can price the expected life and trade count of any barrier-terminated strategy.

### Risk control vs. buy-and-hold
DGT's MDD is materially lower than buy-and-hold on both assets (ETH: ≈50% vs. ≈80% market drawdown), while IRR is equal or better.

**Why it matters:** For portfolio use, **drawdown reduction at comparable return** is the practically valuable property — it makes the strategy survivable through crypto's deep bear phases.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

### Traditional (geometric) grid trading strategy

**Goal:** Profit from price oscillations by mechanically buying dips and selling rallies within a fixed price band.

**Inputs:** start price $P$, grid count $n$ ($n+1$ levels), grid ratio $k$, levels above start $m$, capital $M$ USDT. **Outputs:** final USDT + crypto holdings; arbitrage profit.

**Steps:**

1. **Build levels** — place $n+1$ levels geometrically: $P(1+k)^m$ at the top down to $P(1+k)^{-(n-m)}$ at the bottom. Geometric (equal-ratio) spacing captures moves proportionally at all price scales.
2. **Initial allocation** — spend $M \cdot \frac{m}{n}$ USDT on crypto, keep $M \cdot \frac{n-m}{n}$ USDT. Crypto held must cover the sells that would fire at every level above the current price.
3. **Sell rule** — when price rises across the $G_i$-th gray level counted from the top, sell $\frac{1}{G_i}$ of held crypto; the old active level turns gray, the crossed one turns black (active).
4. **Buy rule** — when price falls across the $G_j$-th gray level counted from the bottom, spend $\frac{1}{G_j}$ of held USDT on crypto; recolor levels the same way. The fractions keep holdings evenly distributed across remaining levels with no manual intervention.
5. **Terminate** — if price exits above the top: hold principal + arbitrage profit in USDT, zero crypto. If price exits below the bottom: all invested USDT is now crypto, plus arbitrage-profit USDT.

**Worked example:** $P=100$, $k=0.02$, $n=6$, $m=3$, $M=600$ USDT. Levels ≈ {94.23, 96.12, 98.04, 100, 102, 104.04, 106.12}. Step 2: buy 300 USDT of crypto, keep 300 USDT. Price rises to 102 → that is the 3rd gray level from the top ($G_i = 3$), sell $\frac{1}{3}$ of crypto (≈102 USDT worth); 102 becomes the active level. Price falls back through 100 → 100 is now the 3rd gray level from the bottom ($G_j = 3$), spend $\frac{1}{3}$ of USDT buying crypto — repurchasing near 100 what was sold at 102 books ≈2% gross arbitrage on that slice. If price later falls through 94.23, terminate holding all crypto plus banked arbitrage USDT.

### Zero-expectation proof (Claim 2)

**Goal:** Show the traditional finite grid strategy has expected value zero under a symmetric random walk with no fees.

**Inputs:** grid count $n$, capital $M$, price moves $\pm k$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. **Outputs:** $E(\text{grid strategy}) = 0$.

**Steps:**

1. **Directional loss** — compute the linear-rise profit $P_u = \frac{M}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2} i$ (Eq. 1) and linear-fall loss $L_l = \frac{M}{n}[(\frac{n}{2})^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{\frac{n}{2}-1} i]$ (Eq. 2). The loss exceeds the profit because the grid takes profit early on the way up but keeps buying on the way down.
2. **Expected directional value** — average the two: $E(G) = \frac{1}{2}(P_u - L_l) = -\frac{M}{n}(\frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4})$ (Eq. 3). Break-even therefore requires more than $\frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4}$ arbitrage round-trips.
3. **Expected trade count** — model the walk as coin tosses; state $q_i$ = current head–tail difference, $E_i$ = expected remaining tosses. The relations $E_1 = E_0 - 1$, $E_i = 2E_{i-1} - E_{i-2} - 2$ (Eq. 4) yield, by induction, $E_m = E_0 - m^2$; with terminal state $E_{n/2} = 0$, $E_0 = \frac{n^2}{4}$.
4. **Expected arbitrage count** — remove the $\frac{n}{2}$ transactions attributable to the net directional move, halve the rest (buy+sell = one arbitrage): $\frac{n^2/4 - n/2}{2} = \frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4}$ (Eq. 5).
5. **Cancellation** — expected arbitrages exactly equal the break-even requirement from step 2, so total expectation is zero (Eq. 6).

**Worked example ($n=6$, $M=600$):** Step 1: $P_u = 100(1{+}2{+}3) = 600$; $L_l = 100(9 + 3) = 1200$. Step 2: $E(G) = \frac{1}{2}(600-1200) = -300$; break-even needs $> \frac{36}{8} - \frac{6}{4} = 3$ arbitrages. Step 3: $E_0 = \frac{36}{4} = 9$ expected transactions. Step 4: $(9 - 3)/2 = 3$ expected arbitrages. Step 5: expected arbitrage profit (3 round-trips × $100 per trip's grid gain = 300) cancels the −300 directional expectation → net **0**.

### Dynamic Grid-based Trading (DGT) strategy

**Goal:** Remove the termination flaw so the strategy keeps harvesting arbitrage regardless of price direction, turning zero expectation into positive returns.

**Inputs:** grid sizes and levels (parameter grid to sweep), transaction fee (0.0008), 1-minute price series. **Outputs:** per-parameter performance metrics (IRR, MDD); `wallet` (cumulative arbitrage profit) and `input money` (total invested capital).

**Steps:**

1. **Set parameters** — choose grid size $k$, grid-numbers-half (levels above/below center), and fee; initialize `wallet`, `input money`, and grid state around the start price.
2. **Trade inside the grid** — for each 1-minute price point, apply the traditional rules: sell on upward crossings of gray levels, buy on downward crossings (Section 4.1 traditional-grid steps 3–4, fee-adjusted).
3. **Detect boundary break** — if price exceeds the top or bottom limit, calculate the profit of the finished grid instead of terminating.
4. **Reset up** — on an upward break, the position is all-USDT (principal + profit); recover the initial capital and reinvest it fully into a **new grid centered at the current price**.
5. **Reset down** — on a downward break, **hold the crypto** (do not liquidate — the opposite of the traditional strategy) and use the accumulated arbitrage profits in `wallet` as the principal for the new grid at the new center.
6. **Update accounting** — update `wallet` (cumulative arbitrage) and `input money` (total capital committed) at every reset; after the sweep, compute IRR and MDD per parameter combination.

**Worked example:** start BTC grid at center 100 (index units), $k = 0.02$, 3 levels each side (limits ≈ 94.23 / 106.12), $M = 600$ USDT. Minutes 1–500: price oscillates 98–104, grid books e.g. 12 arbitrage round-trips ≈ +20 USDT to `wallet` (net of 0.0008 fee per trade). Minute 501: price breaks 106.12 → step 4: all holdings are USDT ≈ 620; recover 600 principal, reinvest at new center 106.12 (new limits ≈ 100.0 / 112.6). Later price crashes through 100.0 → step 5: keep the crypto bought on the way down (≈ 300 USDT cost basis, unrealized), open a new grid centered at 100.0 funded by `wallet` profits (≈ 25 USDT accumulated). The strategy is never flat and never realizes a boundary liquidation loss; over 2021–2024 this compounding produced IRRs up to **60–70%**.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **Reset-on-break mechanism for other mean-reversion strategies** — take DGT steps 3–5 (re-center instead of terminate; hold on downward breaks) and apply the same treatment to Bollinger-band or channel-trading bots, which suffer the identical boundary-liquidation flaw diagnosed in the zero-expectation proof.
- **Fee-aware grid-size optimizer** — the paper shows fees dominate at small $k$ and inactivity dominates at large $k$ but only sweeps parameters by brute force. Build an optimizer that maximizes expected net arbitrage per Section 4's Claim-2 machinery (expected arbitrage count $\frac{n^2}{8}-\frac{n}{4}$ minus fee cost per round-trip) to pick $k$ and grid count analytically per asset volatility.
- **Automaton-based strategy-life estimator** — reuse the finite-automaton result $E_0 = \frac{n^2}{4}$ (zero-expectation proof, step 3) as a standalone tool to estimate expected time-to-boundary and trade count for any barrier strategy, e.g. for position-sizing or capital-lockup planning.
- **Drift-adjusted expectation model** — Claim 2 assumes a symmetric 50–50 walk. Redo the proof's step 3 with up-probability $p \neq \frac{1}{2}$ (biased automaton) to get the grid's expected value as a function of trend, giving a regime filter for when to run DGT vs. buy-and-hold.
- **DGT on traditional markets** — port the DGT algorithm (all six steps) from crypto to high-volatility equities, FX, or leveraged ETFs; the paper's finding that ETH's higher volatility improves IRR suggests screening instruments by realized volatility first.
- **Downward-reset capital policy study** — DGT step 5 funds the new grid only with `wallet` arbitrage profits after a downward break, which can leave the new grid undercapitalized in long bear markets. Test alternative policies (partial crypto liquidation, fixed capital top-ups) against the paper's MDD results.
