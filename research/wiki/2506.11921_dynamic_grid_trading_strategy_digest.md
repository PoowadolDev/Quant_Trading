## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation**: Cryptocurrency markets show high volatility (BTC daily volume ~$37B as of Aug 2024) and traders widely use **grid trading** to exploit price oscillations. The paper first mathematically analyzes whether the traditional grid strategy actually has positive expected value, then proposes an improved strategy.

2. **Traditional geometric grid setup**: Grid levels are placed at price `P * (1+k)^i` for integer offsets `i` from the reference price `P`, where `k` is the grid step ratio. With `n` grids (n+1 levels), if `m` levels sit above and `n-m` below the current price, and total capital `M` USDT is invested, the initial split is: spend `M * m/n` USDT on crypto, hold `M * (n-m)/n` as USDT.

3. **Traditional grid algorithm**: A trade only fires when price crosses a gray grid level. On an up-cross of the `Gi`-th gray level from the top, sell `1/Gi` of held crypto; on a down-cross of the `Gj`-th gray level from the bottom, spend `1/Gj` of held USDT to buy. The strategy **terminates** if price exits the upper or lower grid boundary — this early-termination rule is later shown to be the strategy's core flaw.

4. **Claim 1 (Infinite Resources)**: With infinite capital and infinite time, grid trading is provably profitable, since it always buys low / sells high on every oscillation and never runs out of funds or patience to wait out drawdowns.

5. **Claim 2 (Zero Expected Value) — the central proof of the paper**: Assuming price moves up or down by `k` with equal probability (50/50), no transaction fees, and a finite grid that terminates at the boundary, the paper proves the strategy's expected value is exactly **zero**. Key formulas:
   - Profit if price rises straight through the top boundary:
     $$P_u = \frac{M}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2} i \tag{1}$$
   - Loss if price falls straight through the bottom boundary:
     $$L_l = \frac{M}{n}\left[\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{n/2-1} i\right] \tag{2}$$
   - Combining with equal up/down probability gives the naive linear-move expectation:
     $$E(G) = \frac{1}{2}(P_u - L_l) = -\frac{M}{n}\left(\frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4}\right) \tag{3}$$
   - **Worked example** (n = 6 grids, M = 6000 USDT, matching the paper's Figure 1 setup):
     $P_u = \frac{6000}{6}(1+2+3) = 1000 \times 6 = 6000$ USDT.
     $L_l = \frac{6000}{6}\left[3^2 + (1+2)\right] = 1000 \times (9+3) = 12000$ USDT.
     $E(G) = \tfrac{1}{2}(6000 - 12000) = -3000$ USDT, which matches $-\frac{6000}{6}\left(\frac{36}{8}-\frac{6}{4}\right) = -1000 \times 3 = -3000$.
   - This negative figure only accounts for the boundary-crossing loss; it must be offset by the **arbitrage profit** collected from in-grid oscillations before termination. The paper derives the number of arbitrage trades needed to break even by modeling the price path as a random walk / gambler's-ruin problem (expected number of coin tosses to reach `n/2` net heads or tails), solved via a finite automaton and proven by induction:
     $$E_i = 2E_{i-1} - E_{i-2} - 2,\qquad E_m = E_0 - m^2 \tag{4}$$
     Setting the terminal state $E_{n/2}=0$ gives $E_0 = (n/2)^2 = n^2/4$. After subtracting the price-difference cost and dividing by 2 (since a buy+sell pair = one arbitrage round-trip):
     $$\frac{n^2/4 - n/2}{2} = \frac{n^2}{8} - \frac{n}{4} \tag{5}$$
     **Worked example** (n = 6): $E_0 = 6^2/4 = 9$; required arbitrage opportunities $= \frac{36}{8}-\frac{6}{4} = 4.5-1.5 = 3$ — exactly matching the magnitude of $E(G)=-3000$ derived in formula (3) above (i.e., the arbitrage profit from 3 round-trips is precisely what is needed to cancel the $-M/n(n^2/8-n/4)$ boundary loss). This confirms:
     $$E(\text{grid strategy}) = 0 \tag{6}$$

6. **Why traditional grid trading is risky in practice**: Since expected value is zero *before* fees, and real trading incurs transaction costs (the paper uses **0.0008**, the OKX Level-1 maker fee), traditional grid trading has **negative** expected value after fees — investors are expected to lose money on average.

7. **Proposed fix — Dynamic Grid-based Trading (DGT)**: Instead of terminating when price exits the grid boundary, DGT **resets the grid** with the current price as the new center whenever a boundary is breached. On an upper breakout, capital is fully recovered and reinvested into the new grid; on a lower breakout, the strategy keeps holding crypto and uses accumulated arbitrage profit as new principal. A running **wallet** (cumulative arbitrage profit) and **input money** (total capital) are tracked and updated at every reset (Algorithm 1 in the paper).

8. **Experimental setup**: Backtested on **BTC and ETH** spot pairs using **1-minute Binance kline data from January 2021 to July 2024**, assuming at most one trade per minute, with the 0.0008 fee applied. Performance measured via **IRR (annualized internal rate of return)** and **MDD (maximum drawdown)** across a sweep of grid-size and grid-numbers-half parameters.

9. **Results — DGT vs. buy-and-hold**: DGT achieved IRR up to **60–70%**, generally higher than buy-and-hold. For BTC, DGT beat buy-and-hold on both **IRR and MDD**. For ETH, IRR gains over buy-and-hold were smaller (ETH's strong uptrend favored buy-and-hold), but MDD was far better controlled: during a market crash of **~80%**, DGT limited drawdown to only **~50%**.

10. **Results — DGT vs. traditional grid**: With fees excluded, traditional grid IRR was flat/near-zero (confirming the zero-expectation proof); with fees included it turned **negative** and volatile. DGT **outperformed** traditional grid trading in all tested configurations because it keeps reinvesting profits instead of halting at the boundary. Smaller grid size → lower volatility but more fee drag; larger grid size → higher volatility but fewer fee-eroding trades.

11. **Limitations / future work**: The paper is empirical/backtest-only for DGT — no closed-form proof (yet) of DGT's expected value or profit probability, only the zero-expectation proof for the *traditional* strategy. Authors state future work will derive DGT's expected return mathematically.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### Zero Expected Value Proof for Traditional Grid Trading
The paper formally shows (Eqs. 1–6 above) that under a symmetric random walk and no fees, terminating grid trading nets exactly zero expected value — the arbitrage profit collected during oscillation exactly offsets the loss incurred at boundary termination.

**Why it matters:** This directly contradicts the common retail-trader belief that grid trading is a low-risk way to "buy low, sell high" automatically. It quantitatively shows that, after typical exchange fees, most traditional grid strategies have **negative** expected value — a risk warning for anyone deploying grid bots on exchanges.

### Grid Reset Mechanism (DGT's core innovation)
Rather than stopping at the grid boundary, DGT re-centers a fresh grid at the breakout price and rolls forward capital (or held crypto) as the new principal.

**Why it matters:** This single structural change converts a strategy with proven zero/negative expectation into one empirically achieving 60–70% IRR — it demonstrates that *when* a strategy stops trading can matter as much as *how* it trades.

### Risk Control via Maximum Drawdown
DGT's MDD stayed far below market MDD (~50% vs. ~80% market crash) even when its IRR advantage over buy-and-hold was thin (as in ETH).

**Why it matters:** For practitioners, drawdown control that survives a genuine bear market is often more valuable than raw IRR, since it determines whether a trader can psychologically and financially sustain a strategy through a downturn.

### Fee Sensitivity of Grid Parameters
Both traditional grid and DGT show that small grid size increases trade frequency and fee drag, while large grid size reduces trade frequency but increases volatility/slippage from wider price gaps.

**Why it matters:** This is a practical tuning guide — grid size and number of levels must be jointly optimized against the specific fee schedule of the exchange used, not chosen arbitrarily.

## 4. Ideas for Development

- **Closed-form DGT expectation model** — Extend the gambler's-ruin / finite-automaton proof method used for the traditional grid (Eqs. 4–6) to derive an analytic expected-value formula for DGT under grid resets, rather than relying purely on backtests.
- **Fee-aware grid parameter optimizer** — Build a small optimizer (grid search or Bayesian optimization) over grid size `k` and grid-numbers-half `n/2` that directly targets IRR/MDD trade-offs for a given exchange fee tier, generalizing the paper's manual parameter sweep.
- **Cross-asset generalization** — Apply the DGT reset logic to other volatile, range-bound-prone assets (altcoins, FX pairs, volatile small caps) and test whether the zero-expectation proof and reset benefit generalize beyond BTC/ETH.
- **Regime-aware grid activation** — Combine DGT with a trend/regime filter (e.g., only reset-and-continue in ranging regimes, switch to buy-and-hold in strong trends) to address the paper's own finding that ETH's strong uptrend reduced DGT's IRR edge.
- **Live execution risk overlay** — Add slippage and partial-fill modeling on top of the 1-minute backtest assumption (at most one trade/minute), since real order books may not fill grid orders instantly at the exact crossed level.
