## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation — the high-dimensional gap.** The stochastic-control literature on pairs trading (Jurek & Yang 2007, Mudchanatongsuk et al. 2008, Leung & Li 2015) solves an optimal-allocation problem for **two** cointegrated assets. Separately, **Avellaneda & Lee (2010)** build a **factor model with mean-reverting residuals** across hundreds of US equities but then trade it with simple **threshold rules** (enter at ±2σ, exit at 0). Nobody had combined the two. This paper fills that gap: a factor model supplies the mean-reverting signal, and stochastic control supplies the optimal position size, for an **arbitrary number N of assets**.

2. **Setup — three assumptions.** The investor observes returns on N risky assets. **Assumption 1** is a factor model in differential form, $$dR_t = \Lambda\, dF_t + dX_t$$ where $R_t$ is the cumulative asset-return process, $\Lambda$ the constant loadings matrix, $F_t$ the cumulative factor returns and $X_t$ the **cumulative residual returns**. **Assumption 2** is that factors are recoverable linearly from returns, $$dF_t = \tilde\Lambda\, dR_t$$ which holds for PCA and for a broader class. **Assumption 3** is that the residuals mean-revert as a **matrix Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process**, $$dX_t = A(\mu - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\, dB_t$$ with $A$ a constant $N\times N$ matrix (mean-reversion speeds), $\mu$ the equilibrium vector, and $\sigma\sigma'$ invertible. Crucially, unlike Avellaneda-Lee the residuals **may be correlated and interdependent** — $A$ and $\sigma$ need not be diagonal.

   *Worked example.* Take one residual coordinate with $A_{ii}=0.5$, $\mu_i=0$, current $X_{it}=-0.06$ (the asset is 6% cheap relative to its factor fit). The drift term is $A(\mu - X_t)_i = 0.5\times(0 - (-0.06)) = +0.03$ per unit time — a pull back up toward zero. Half-life is $\ln 2 / 0.5 = 1.386$ time units.

3. **First main result — market-neutral portfolios with no optimisation.** Combining the two model equations gives $dR_{ti} = \sum_k c_{ik} dR_{tk} + dX_{ti}$ with $$c_{ik} := \sum_j \tilde\Lambda_{jk}\Lambda_{ij}$$ So holding the **constant-in-time** portfolio $$\tilde p_i := (-c_{i1},\, -c_{i2},\, \ldots,\, 1 - c_{ii},\, \ldots,\, -c_{iN})$$ yields an instantaneous return of exactly $dX_{ti}$ — the *i*-th residual alone, factor-neutral **by construction**. This is Proposition 2.1, and its significance is that market-neutrality requires **no matrix inversion and no numerical optimiser**, unlike Yeo & Papanicolaou (2017) or Boyd et al. (2017). The capital consumed is $\pi_t \cdot p$ where $p := (\tilde p_i \cdot \mathbf{1})_i$, a vector depending **quadratically on the loadings**.

   *Worked example.* Two assets, $\Lambda$ and $\tilde\Lambda$ such that $c_{11}=0.3,\ c_{12}=0.4$. Then $\tilde p_1 = (1-0.3,\ -0.4) = (0.7,\ -0.4)$: long 0.70 of asset 1, short 0.40 of asset 2. Capital used $p_1 = 0.7 - 0.4 = 0.30$ per unit of exposure.

4. **The control problem.** Wealth evolves as $$dW_t = \pi_t\cdot dX_t + (W_t - \pi_t\cdot p)\,r\,dt$$ with $r$ the risk-free rate, and the investor maximises $H(t,x,w) = \sup_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{t,x,w}[u(W_T)]$ over a finite horizon $T$. The associated HJB equation is non-linear and $(N+2)$-dimensional: $$0 = \partial_t H + (\mu-x)'A'\nabla_x H + \tfrac12\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma\sigma'\nabla_{xx}H) + \sup_\pi\Big(\big(\pi'A(\mu-x) + (w-\pi'p)r\big)\partial_w H + \tfrac12\pi'\sigma\sigma'\pi\,\partial_{ww}H + \pi'\sigma\sigma'\nabla_{xw}H\Big)$$ with terminal condition $H(T,x,w)=u(w)$.

5. **Second main result — closed-form optimal strategy, exponential utility.** For $u(w) = -e^{-\gamma w}$, **Theorem 3.1** gives $$\pi^*_t = \frac{(\sigma\sigma')^{-1}A(\mu - X_t) - pr}{\gamma e^{r(T-t)}} + \frac{A'(\sigma\sigma')^{-1}}{\gamma e^{r(T-t)}}\left((A(\mu-X_t) - pr)(T-t) - \frac{Apr(T-t)^2}{2}\right)$$ The first term is **Merton-like**: signal drift over volatility over risk aversion. The second is a **non-myopic correction** that vanishes as $t\to T$ and has no analogue in the classical geometric-Brownian-motion Merton problem — it appears because the drift of the OU process is itself stochastic. The strategy is **affine in the deviation of residuals from their mean**, i.e. exactly "buy more the cheaper it is", as in classical pairs trading, but with the coefficient derived rather than chosen.

   *Worked example.* Single coordinate, $\sigma\sigma'=0.04$, $A=0.5$, $\mu=0$, $X_t=-0.06$, $\gamma=1$, $r=0$, so $p r = 0$ and $e^{r(T-t)}=1$. Myopic term $= (1/0.04)\times 0.5\times 0.06 / 1 = 25\times0.03 = 0.75$. With $T-t = 2$, the correction adds $0.5\times(1/0.04)\times(0.03\times 2) = 0.75$, total $\pi^*_t \approx 1.50$ dollars per unit. Doubling $\gamma$ to 2 halves both to $0.75$.

6. **Mean-variance case, and how the two arbitrageurs differ.** **Theorem 3.2**: maximising $\mathbb{E}[W_T - \int_t^T \frac{\gamma(s)}{2}\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathrm{Var}_s(W_\tau)|_{\tau=s}ds]$ gives $$\pi^*_t = (\gamma(t)\sigma\sigma')^{-1}\big(A(\mu-X_t) - pr\big)e^{r(T-t)}$$ For $\gamma(t) = \gamma e^{2r(T-t)}$ this is exactly the **myopic term of the exponential solution** — the mean-variance arbitrageur therefore **ignores the expected number of mean-reversion cycles remaining before $T$**. With non-zero $r$ and constant $\gamma$, she is also **more aggressive by a factor of $e^{2r(T-t)}$**.

7. **Third main result (a) — dollar neutrality as a soft constraint.** A strategy is dollar-neutral when $p\cdot\pi_t = 0$. Rather than a hard constraint (which forces numerical optimisation and is infeasible at $N$ in the hundreds), the paper penalises the wealth process, $d\tilde W_t := dW_t - \alpha(t)(\pi_t\cdot p)^2/2\,dt$. **Corollary 4.1** then gives, for mean-variance, $$\pi^*_t = \big(\gamma(t)\sigma\sigma' + \alpha(t)pp'\big)^{-1}\big(A(\mu-X_t) - pr\big)e^{r(T-t)}$$ The entire effect is the extra $\alpha(t)pp'$ term **inside the inverse** — structure preserved, still closed-form.

8. **Third main result (b) — quadratic transaction costs.** Switching the control from position $\pi_t$ to **trading intensity** $I_t$ (where $d\pi_t = I_t dt$) and charging $\tfrac12 I_t' C I_t$, **Theorem 4.1** shows the optimum "**tracks a moving aim portfolio**": $$I_t = \mathrm{Aim}(t,X_t) + \mathrm{Rate}(t)\,\pi_t, \qquad \mathrm{Aim}(t,x) = \int_t^T f(s)\,\mathbb{E}_{t,x}[\mathrm{Frictionless}(s)]\,ds$$ The aim is a **weighted average of future frictionless optimal portfolios** — with costs you trade toward where the signal will be, not where it is. For constant volatility aversion and $C = \lambda\sigma\sigma'$, the rate collapses to a scalar: $$\mathrm{Rate}(t) = \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma}{\lambda}}\tanh\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma}{\lambda}}(t-T)\right)$$ bounded by 1 in magnitude, **decreasing in the cost parameter $\lambda$** and increasing in risk aversion $\gamma$.

   *Worked example.* $\gamma=1$, $\lambda=0.25$, so $\sqrt{\gamma/\lambda}=2$. At $t-T=-1$ (one unit before the horizon), $\mathrm{Rate} = 2\tanh(-2) = -1.928$; the position decays toward the aim at roughly 1.93 per unit time. Raise costs to $\lambda=4$, so $\sqrt{\gamma/\lambda}=0.5$: $\mathrm{Rate}=0.5\tanh(-0.5)=-0.231$ — **eight times slower**, i.e. higher costs mean trading toward the target far more gradually.

9. **Simulation setup.** Monte Carlo with **N = 100 residuals**, $\mu = 0$, $X_0 = \mu$; $A$ diagonal with entries drawn i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(0.5, 0.1)$; off-diagonal $\sigma$ entries i.i.d. $U[-0.3, 0.3]$ and diagonal entries $U[0, 0.5]$; horizon $T = 20$ with $L = 400$ steps ($\Delta t = 0.05$ of horizon, stated as $T/L$); **M = 1,000 paths**. Parameters swept: $\gamma = 1,2,3,4$; $r = 0, 2\%$; dollar-neutrality penalty $\alpha = 0, 20, 50$; cost parameter $\lambda = 0.1, 0.5, 1$; and $p = 1 + \epsilon_a$ with $\epsilon_a \sim U[-a,a]^N$ for $a = 1,2,4,8$. The choice $T=20$ with mean-reversion speed $\approx 0.5$ gives roughly **10 full oscillation cycles** before the horizon.

10. **Simulation findings.** (1) The **exponential-utility strategy is consistently more profitable** than mean-variance under every regime of $r$ and $\alpha$, and takes **more extreme positions**. (2) Wealth paths show initial volatility then **stabilise**, a consequence of the OU process's asymptotic properties. (3) **Raising $\gamma$ concentrates terminal wealth around smaller values** — both mean and dispersion fall. (4) Raising $\alpha$ is mildly negative unless the increment is large. (5) Raising $r$ is **positive overall**, more so for mean-variance. (6) Raising $\lambda$ lowers expected terminal wealth **but skews the distribution right**, producing a considerable right tail where all other distributions are near-symmetric. (7) As $p$ grows — i.e. **heavier factor loadings** — positions become more aggressive and terminal-wealth variance rises sharply, and **at the largest $p$ with transaction costs there are heavy losses**.

11. **Stated limitations — the important one.** The author is explicit that **real-data experiments are deferred to a separate paper**, because asset selection, rebalance frequency, factor-model construction, high-dimensional parameter estimation and risk control would each need proper treatment. And in his own words about the simulations: *"since the model is perfectly specified and the parameters are known, the derived strategies will always produce benefits by construction… This situation, however, might not apply under parameter misspecification, where the additional constraints would help the investor mitigate the model risk."* The profits in Section 5 are therefore a **check that the control algebra is right, not evidence of edge**. Loadings $\Lambda$ are also assumed **constant in time**.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### Residuals, not price levels, as the tradable signal
The mean-reverting object is $X_t$, the **cumulative residual return** after factors are removed — not a cointegrating combination of price levels. Assumption 3 puts the OU process on the residual, so the strategy never requires two prices to hold a stable long-run *ratio*.

**Why it matters:** Level cointegration demands a **stable long-run price ratio**, an assumption that fails whenever relative valuations reprice permanently. A residual construction sidesteps that requirement entirely — the factor model absorbs the common movement each period and only the idiosyncratic part must revert. This is the single most transferable idea in the paper.

### Market neutrality for free, by construction
Proposition 2.1 builds $N$ factor-neutral portfolios from the loadings alone, with **no optimiser and no matrix inversion**, and neutrality then holds identically rather than approximately.

**Why it matters:** Every alternative in the literature (Yeo & Papanicolaou 2017, Boyd et al. 2017) reaches neutrality through numerical optimisation, which at $N$ in the hundreds or thousands is expensive and can fail. Getting it as an algebraic identity means neutrality **cannot silently degrade** — a real failure mode when a hedge ratio is fitted and then drifts.

### Position size is derived, not chosen
The optimal $\pi^*_t$ is affine in $A(\mu - X_t)$ with coefficients from matrix Riccati ODEs. Threshold rules ("enter at 2σ, exit at 0") are replaced by a continuous function of how far the residual sits from its mean and how fast it reverts.

**Why it matters:** A threshold is a **free parameter that must be searched**, and every value searched is a trial that inflates selection bias. Deriving the size from $\gamma$, $A$, $\sigma\sigma'$ and $r$ removes a whole dimension of the search — the remaining free parameter is risk aversion, which is a **preference**, not something to be fitted to past returns.

### Transaction costs change *what* you trade toward, not just how much
Theorem 4.1's aim portfolio is a **forward-looking average of future frictionless optima**, and $\mathrm{Rate}(t)$ slows as costs rise.

**Why it matters:** The naive treatment of costs is to subtract them from a signal computed cost-free. This paper shows the optimum is structurally different: with costs you **partially trade toward a target you expect to hold later**, which mechanically reduces turnover instead of merely penalising it after the fact.

### Heavier factor loadings mean more leverage, and the paper quantifies the danger
The vector $p$ depends **quadratically on the loadings**. Bigger $p$ means more capital needed to achieve neutrality, more aggressive positions, higher terminal-wealth variance — and, at the largest $p$ **combined with transaction costs, outright heavy losses** (Figure 5(c),(i)).

**Why it matters:** This is the paper's own warning that the method has a regime where it breaks. It is the direct analogue of a large hedge ratio in a pair trade, and it says the failure arrives through the **interaction of leverage and cost**, not from either alone.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

### A. Constructing factor-neutral residual portfolios (Proposition 2.1)

**Goal:** Turn $N$ correlated assets into $N$ tradable portfolios, each of which earns exactly one residual and carries zero factor exposure — without running an optimiser.

**Inputs:** Historical returns for $N$ assets; a factor model giving loadings $\Lambda$ ($N\times K$) and the factor-recovery matrix $\tilde\Lambda$ ($K\times N$).
**Outputs:** $N$ constant weight vectors $\tilde p_i$; the capital vector $p$; the residual process $X_t$.

**Steps:**

1. **Fit the factor model.** Run PCA (or a variant) on the return covariance to get $\Lambda$, whose rows are the leading eigenvectors, and $\tilde\Lambda$ such that $dF_t = \tilde\Lambda\,dR_t$. Factors are recovered by regressing returns on a rescaling of $\Lambda$.
2. **Form the pass-through matrix.** Compute $c_{ik} = \sum_j \tilde\Lambda_{jk}\Lambda_{ij}$. This is how much of asset $k$'s return leaks into asset $i$ purely through shared factors.
3. **Subtract the leakage.** Set $\tilde p_i = (-c_{i1},\ldots,1-c_{ii},\ldots,-c_{iN})$: hold one unit of asset $i$ and short each other asset by exactly its factor pass-through. What remains is $dX_{ti}$ — asset $i$'s idiosyncratic return.
4. **Record capital used.** $p_i = \tilde p_i\cdot\mathbf{1}$, the net dollar cost of portfolio $i$. Any combination $\pi_t$ consumes $\pi_t\cdot p$.
5. **Estimate the OU parameters.** From the history of $X_t$, estimate $A$ (mean-reversion speeds), $\mu$ (equilibrium) and $\sigma$ (residual volatility).

**Worked example.** Three assets, one factor. Suppose the pass-through row for asset 1 is $c_{11}=0.25,\ c_{12}=0.30,\ c_{13}=0.20$. Then $\tilde p_1 = (0.75,\ -0.30,\ -0.20)$. Capital $p_1 = 0.75-0.30-0.20 = 0.25$. If today asset 1 returns $+1.2\%$, asset 2 $+0.9\%$, asset 3 $+1.1\%$, the portfolio earns $0.75(1.2) - 0.30(0.9) - 0.20(1.1) = 0.90 - 0.27 - 0.22 = +0.41\%$ — asset 1's move net of what the factor explains. Over time these $0.41\%$ increments accumulate into $X_{1t}$, and *that* is what must mean-revert.

### B. Optimal frictionless allocation, exponential utility (Theorem 3.1)

**Goal:** Given the residual state, decide how many dollars to put in each neutral portfolio to maximise expected exponential utility of terminal wealth.

**Inputs:** Current residuals $X_t$; OU parameters $A,\mu,\sigma$; capital vector $p$; risk aversion $\gamma$; rate $r$; time remaining $T-t$.
**Outputs:** Dollar allocation vector $\pi^*_t$.

**Steps:**

1. **Measure the mispricing pull.** Compute $A(\mu - X_t)$ — the instantaneous expected drift of each residual. Far-from-mean and fast-reverting both raise it.
2. **Charge the cost of neutrality.** Subtract $pr$: holding the neutral portfolios consumes capital $p$, financed at $r$. Heavier loadings → bigger $p$ → bigger drag.
3. **Scale by risk and volatility.** Pre-multiply by $(\sigma\sigma')^{-1}$ and divide by $\gamma e^{r(T-t)}$. This is the **myopic Merton term**: $\dfrac{(\sigma\sigma')^{-1}A(\mu-X_t) - pr}{\gamma e^{r(T-t)}}$.
4. **Add the non-myopic correction.** Add $\dfrac{A'(\sigma\sigma')^{-1}}{\gamma e^{r(T-t)}}\Big((A(\mu-X_t)-pr)(T-t) - \frac{Apr(T-t)^2}{2}\Big)$, which accounts for how many reversion cycles remain. It **vanishes at $t=T$**.
5. **Check the verification condition.** Proposition 3.2 requires $4\max_s\|\Lambda_0(s)\| < 1$ and $32\max_s\|\Lambda_1(s)\| < 1$; otherwise the exponential value function can blow up. Notably this depends **only on $X$'s parameters and $T$** — not on $\gamma$, $r$ or the factor model.

**Worked example.** One coordinate: $A=0.5$, $\mu=0$, $X_t=-0.06$, $\sigma\sigma'=0.04$, $\gamma=1$, $r=0$, $T-t=2$.
Step 1: $A(\mu-X_t) = 0.5\times 0.06 = 0.03$.
Step 2: $pr = 0$, so still $0.03$.
Step 3: myopic $= (0.03/0.04)/1 = 0.75$.
Step 4: correction $= 0.5\times(1/0.04)\times(0.03\times 2) = 12.5\times 0.06 = 0.75$.
Total $\pi^*_t = 1.50$. At $t=T$ the correction is 0 and the position would be $0.75$ — **the same signal justifies half the size at the horizon**, because there is no longer time for another cycle.

### C. Optimal allocation under quadratic transaction costs (Theorem 4.1)

**Goal:** Choose a *trading speed* rather than a position, so that turnover is priced rather than free.

**Inputs:** Current position $\pi_t$; residuals $X_t$; cost matrix $C$; volatility aversion $\gamma(t)$; all OU parameters.
**Outputs:** Trading intensity $I_t$ (dollars per unit time), integrating to the position path.

**Steps:**

1. **Solve the Riccati ODE for the rate.** $\partial_t a - \gamma(t)\sigma\sigma' + aC^{-1}a = 0$, $a(T)=0$; then $\mathrm{Rate}(t) = C^{-1}a(t)$, negative-definite and $\to 0$ at $T$. For constant $\gamma$ and $C=\lambda\sigma\sigma'$ it is the scalar $\sqrt{\gamma/\lambda}\tanh(\sqrt{\gamma/\lambda}(t-T))$.
2. **Compute the aim portfolio.** $\mathrm{Aim}(t,x) = \int_t^T f(s)\mathbb{E}_{t,x}[\mathrm{Frictionless}(s)]ds$ — a discounted average of the **future** cost-free optima, not today's.
3. **Trade toward the aim at the rate.** $I_t = \mathrm{Aim}(t,X_t) + \mathrm{Rate}(t)\pi_t$. Because $\mathrm{Rate}$ is negative-definite, the second term pulls the existing position down; the first pushes toward the target.
4. **Integrate to get the position.** $\pi^*_s = \pi_t + \int_t^s :\!\exp(\int_u^s \mathrm{Rate}(v)dv)\!:\mathrm{Aim}(u,X_u)\,du$ using the time-ordered exponential.

**Worked example.** $\gamma=1$, $\lambda=0.25$ so $\sqrt{\gamma/\lambda}=2$; at $t-T=-1$, $\mathrm{Rate}=2\tanh(-2)=-1.928$. Suppose $\mathrm{Aim}=1.50$ and the current position is $\pi_t=0.50$. Then $I_t = 1.50 + (-1.928)(0.50) = 1.50 - 0.96 = +0.54$ dollars per unit time — still buying, but at roughly a third of the speed implied by the gap alone. Now raise costs to $\lambda=4$: $\mathrm{Rate}=-0.231$, so $I_t = 1.50 - 0.116 = 1.384$… yet the *aim itself* shrinks because $f(s)$ falls, and the position converges far more slowly. The mechanism is that **high costs lengthen the time spent away from the frictionless optimum**, which is the correct economic answer.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **Replace level cointegration with factor-residual reversion** — adopt Algorithm A wholesale: fit PCA factors across a universe, trade the residual $X_t$ rather than a fitted price spread. This substitutes the entire screening premise (does a *pair* cointegrate?) with a portfolio premise (does each *residual* revert?).
  - **Strong:** universes where a single dominant factor explains most covariance — the residual is then well-identified and small relative to the factor *(From AI: if $\Lambda$ captures a share $\rho$ of return variance, residual variance is $(1-\rho)$ of total; a large $\rho$ means the factor removal is doing real work and $X_t$ is genuinely idiosyncratic rather than a rescaled market bet)*
  - **Strong:** assets whose relative valuations trend permanently, where level cointegration is structurally wrong — the method never requires a stable price ratio, only a reverting residual *(From AI: Assumption 3 places the OU on $X_t$, the residual of a per-period return regression; a permanent repricing shifts $\Lambda$'s explained component and leaves $X_t$'s equilibrium $\mu$ unchanged, whereas a level-cointegration test would reject)*
  - **Weak:** small universes — with $N$ of 15 or 20, PCA eigenvectors are estimated from few cross-sectional observations and the "factor" is unstable *(From AI: the sample covariance of $N$ assets from $T$ observations has estimation error growing with $N/T$; at small $N$ the leading eigenvector is poorly separated from noise, so the residual inherits the estimation error rather than removing it)*
  - **Weak:** constant-loadings assumption is stated explicitly by the author (footnote 2, "for analytic tractability") and fails when the factor structure itself shifts *(From paper)*

- **Adopt the closed-form neutrality construction to replace a fitted hedge ratio** — use Step 3 of Algorithm A ($\tilde p_i$ from pass-through $c_{ik}$) instead of regressing one asset on another and monitoring the beta for drift.
  - **Strong:** neutrality is an algebraic identity given the model, so it cannot silently degrade the way a fitted ratio does *(From paper, Proposition 2.1 — neutrality holds "by construction", no optimisation needed)*
  - **Strong:** sparse factor models make $\tilde p_i$ mostly zeros, so each neutral portfolio touches few assets and costs less to rebalance *(From paper, Remark 2.1, citing Pelger & Xiong 2018)*
  - **Weak:** neutrality is only as good as the factor model — if $\Lambda$ is misestimated, the construction is exactly neutral to the *wrong* factors *(From AI: the identity $dR_{ti}=\sum_k c_{ik}dR_{tk}+dX_{ti}$ is derived from Assumptions 1–2; any error in $\Lambda$ or $\tilde\Lambda$ propagates directly into $c_{ik}$ and hence into the residual, with no diagnostic inside the construction to reveal it)*

- **Derive position size instead of searching an entry threshold** — use Algorithm B's affine rule $\pi^*_t \propto A(\mu - X_t)$ in place of a ±z-score entry grid.
  - **Strong:** eliminates a searched parameter, which directly reduces the multiple-testing burden on any later validation *(From AI: the deflated-Sharpe benchmark grows with the logarithm of the number of trials $N$; removing an entry-threshold sweep of $k$ values divides the trial count by $k$ and lowers the benchmark by $\approx\sqrt{2\ln k}$ in Sharpe units)*
  - **Strong:** the non-myopic term correctly shrinks the position as the horizon approaches, something a fixed threshold cannot express *(From paper, Theorem 3.1 — the correction term vanishes as $t\to T$)*
  - **Weak:** requires $A$, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ to be estimated, trading one free parameter for three estimated ones *(From AI: $\pi^*$ is affine in $A(\mu-X_t)$ with $(\sigma\sigma')^{-1}$ pre-multiplying, so a relative error $\varepsilon$ in $A$ produces a proportional error in position size, and an overstated $A$ — a too-fast assumed reversion — produces systematic oversizing)*
  - **Weak:** the verification conditions $4\max\|\Lambda_0\|<1$, $32\max\|\Lambda_1\|<1$ can fail, and then the exponential-utility solution is not valid *(From paper, Proposition 3.2)*

- **Use the aim-portfolio mechanism to control turnover** — implement Algorithm C's $I_t = \mathrm{Aim}(t,X_t) + \mathrm{Rate}(t)\pi_t$ rather than entering and exiting at thresholds.
  - **Strong:** where financing and spread dominate the edge, trading toward a forward-looking target structurally reduces turnover instead of penalising it afterwards *(From paper, Theorem 4.1 — the aim is an average of *future* frictionless optima)*
  - **Strong:** the scalar form $\sqrt{\gamma/\lambda}\tanh(\sqrt{\gamma/\lambda}(t-T))$ makes the cost/aggression trade-off explicit and tunable with one number *(From paper, Remark 4.1)*
  - **Weak:** quadratic costs model **price impact**, not a fixed bid-ask spread plus commission; a retail cost structure is closer to a fixed per-trade charge, for which this solution is not the optimum *(From AI: the derivation charges $\tfrac12 I_t'CI_t$, which is zero at zero trading rate and grows with the square; a fixed per-round-trip cost is instead a constant independent of size, so the optimal policy under it involves a no-trade band rather than continuous tracking)*

- **Run the model's own failure regime as a test, not just the success case** — reproduce Figure 5: sweep $p = 1+\epsilon_a$ for $a = 1,2,4,8$ with transaction costs switched on, and confirm the heavy-loss regime appears.
  - **Strong:** the paper documents that large $p$ plus transaction costs produces heavy losses, giving a known-answer test for an implementation *(From paper, Section 5.5.4(2) and Figure 5(c),(i))*
  - **Weak:** the simulations assume perfectly known parameters, so reproducing them validates the control algebra only — not the estimation, which is where a live implementation actually fails *(From paper, Section 5.5.1: "since the model is perfectly specified and the parameters are known, the derived strategies will always produce benefits by construction")*
