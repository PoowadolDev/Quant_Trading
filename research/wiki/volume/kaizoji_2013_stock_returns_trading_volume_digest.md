## 2. Summary

Notation used throughout this digest, matching the paper: $n$ = number of interacting (noise) traders; $m$ = number of fundamentalists; $u_i \in \{+1,-1\}$ = investment attitude of interacting trader $i$ ($+1$ buyer, $-1$ seller); $X$ = average investment attitude over interacting traders; $\kappa$ = intensity of interaction among interacting traders; $\xi$ = the **conformity effect** (the constant scaling the randomness of $\kappa$); $b$ = fixed number of shares each interacting trader trades per day; $a$ = fundamentalists' reaction coefficient on mispricing; $S_j$ = market price on day $j$; $S_j^{*}$ = fundamental value on day $j$; $R_j$ = daily log return; $V_j$ = daily trading volume in shares; $W(t)$ = Wiener process; $\Delta t_j$ = simulation time step.

1. **Motivation and gap.** More than half a century of research has studied trading volume and its relation to asset returns, and the paper opens by noting the tension in that literature: any exploitable relationship between volume and *future* prices is inconsistent with the **weak form of market efficiency**, yet the relationship keeps attracting attention precisely because many practitioners believe price movements can be predicted from volume. The author positions his work against two traditions. Mainstream finance (Osborne 1959; Ying 1966; Epps 1975; Copeland 1976; Karpoff's 1987 survey; Schwert 1989; Gallant–Rossi–Tauchen 1992; Campbell–Grossman–Wang 1993; Jones–Kaul–Lipson 1994; Lo–Wang 2000, 2002) studies correlations. **Econophysics** (Iori 1999; Bonanno–Lillo–Mantegna 2000; Plerou et al. 2001, 2002, 2003; Lillo–Farmer–Mantegna 2003; Gabaix et al. 2003; Kaizoji–Nuki 2004) looks instead for **universal characteristics of complex systems with many interacting units** — chiefly **power laws** — but the author's own criticism of that camp is that its studies "tend to be very data-based, and the models are more statistical than economic in character". The paper's stated aim is two-fold: document power laws empirically, then supply an *economic* mechanism that generates them.

2. **Empirical part: one stock, 28 years, two power laws.** The empirical work uses a database of **daily transactions for all securities listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange**, from which the author selects **Fujita Corporation (ticker 1806)**, a mid-size Japanese construction company, as a "typical example". The window is **January 1975 to January 2002 — 28 years** — which the paper explicitly frames as spanning **Japan's asset bubble and its burst**. Return is the log price change from one day's close to the next; trading volume is the number of shares traded in a day. Figure 1 shows returns with **clustered volatility consisting of laminar phases and chaotic bursts**, and a semi-log probability plot whose tails are visibly fatter than an exponential (footnote 2 states the diagnostic: a straight line on a semi-log plot means an exponential, so curvature means fatter). Figure 2 repeats the exercise for volume. The claimed functional form for the tails is a power law:
   $$P(R > x) \sim x^{-\beta}$$
   where $R$ denotes returns, $x$ a threshold, and $\beta$ the **power-law exponent**. The same form is asserted for the trading-volume tail. *Worked example:* if $\beta = 3$ (the "inverse cubic law" reported for equity returns by Gabaix et al. 2003, reference [19] here) and the exceedance probability at a $2\%$ daily move is $P(R>0.02)=0.010$, then the normalising constant is $C = 0.010 \times 0.02^{3} = 8\times10^{-8}$, so $P(R>x) = 8\times10^{-8}\,x^{-3}$. Evaluating: $P(R>0.06) = 8\times10^{-8}/2.16\times10^{-4} = \mathbf{3.70\times10^{-4}}$, and $P(R>0.20) = 8\times10^{-8}/8\times10^{-3} = \mathbf{1.00\times10^{-5}}$. The defining property is **scale-free decay**: the probability halves every time the threshold is multiplied by $2^{1/3} = 1.26$, no matter how far out one already is. An exponential tail anchored at the same point, $P(R>x) = 0.010\,e^{-(x-0.02)/0.02}$, instead halves every time the threshold *increases* by $0.02\ln 2 = 0.0139$ in absolute terms. Because the exponential's chosen scale makes it heavy near the anchor, it actually sits **above** the power law over the near tail ($1.35\times10^{-3}$ versus $3.70\times10^{-4}$ at $x = 0.06$), the two cross at $x \approx \mathbf{0.134}$, and only beyond that does the power law dominate — by $8.1\times$ at $x = 0.20$ and by $2.2\times10^{4}$ at $x = 0.40$. **Critically, the paper reports no fitted value of $\beta$, no standard error, no fitting range, and no goodness-of-fit statistic** for either returns or volume; the entire empirical claim rests on the visual appearance of two semi-log plots.

3. **Model architecture: two trader groups, fixed populations.** To explain the power laws the author reuses the interacting-agent model of Kaizoji & Kaizoji (2003), reference [24], which applies the **Ising model** of statistical mechanics to a stock market. Shares trade at price $S_j$ per share. The market contains exactly two groups with **constant** population counts: $m$ **fundamentalists**, who believe price should equal fundamental value (Gordon 1962), and $n$ **interacting traders**, who "get influenced by the investment attitude of other traders". The model describes price and volume movements over a **trading day**. An important framing point: the interacting trader is *not* the standard noise trader of Kyle (1985) who "trades randomly" — the paper insists an interacting trader "is not idiot but makes decisions regarding buying or selling based on perceived market moods rather than fundamentals", buying when others seem to be buying and selling when others seem to be selling. Sentiment, not randomness, is the primitive.

4. **Interacting traders: transition probabilities driven by the crowd.** Each interacting trader $i \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ carries $u_i = +1$ if a buyer that day and $u_i = -1$ if a seller. The state of the crowd is the **average investment attitude**
   $$X = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i \in [-1,\,1]$$
   which is positive when more than half of the interacting traders are buyers and negative when more than half are sellers. Each trader's attitude flips with probabilities that are logistic functions of $X$:
   $$W_{\uparrow}(X) = \left[1 + \exp(-2\kappa X)\right]^{-1} \quad \text{(seller} \to \text{buyer)} \tag{1}$$
   $$W_{\downarrow}(X) = \left[1 + \exp(2\kappa X)\right]^{-1} \quad \text{(buyer} \to \text{seller)} \tag{2}$$
   where $\kappa$ denotes the **intensity of interaction** among interacting traders. The two probabilities sum to 1 and can be written compactly as $W_{\uparrow} = \tfrac12\left(1+\tanh \kappa X\right)$, $W_{\downarrow} = \tfrac12\left(1-\tanh \kappa X\right)$. The economics is pure herding: as $X$ rises, the seller-to-buyer probability rises and the buyer-to-seller probability falls simultaneously, and vice versa. *Worked example:* with $\kappa = 2$ and $X = 0.20$, $\;2\kappa X = 0.80$, so $W_{\uparrow} = 1/(1+e^{-0.8}) = 1/(1+0.44933) = \mathbf{0.68997}$ and $W_{\downarrow} = 1/(1+e^{0.8}) = 1/(1+2.22554) = \mathbf{0.31003}$; they sum to $1.00000$ ✓, and $\tfrac12(1+\tanh 0.4) = \tfrac12(1+0.37995) = 0.68997$ ✓. With $n = 100{,}000$ traders, $X=0.20$ means $n_{+} = n(1+X)/2 = \mathbf{60{,}000}$ buyers and $n_{-} = n(1-X)/2 = \mathbf{40{,}000}$ sellers; per unit time $40{,}000\times0.68997 = 27{,}599$ sellers turn buyer while $60{,}000\times0.31003 = 18{,}602$ buyers turn seller, a **net inflow of $8{,}997$ buyers** — self-reinforcing herding.

5. **Mean-field limit: a Langevin equation for sentiment, with a random interaction strength.** From the transition probabilities (1)–(2) the author derives (details deferred to [24] and to Gardiner's *Handbook of Stochastic Methods* [27]) a stochastic differential equation for the crowd state:
   $$dX(t) = K(X)\,dt + \sqrt{Q(X)}\;dW(t) \tag{3}$$
   $$K(X) = \tanh(\kappa X) - X, \qquad Q(X) = \frac{2}{n}\left[\,1 - X \tanh(\kappa X)\,\right]$$
   where $K(X)$ is the drift, $Q(X)$ the variance rate, and $W(t)$ the Wiener process. For simulation this is discretised (Euler–Maruyama):
   $$X_{j+1} = X_j + K(X_j)\,\Delta t_j + \sqrt{Q(X_j)}\;\Delta W_j \tag{4}$$
   with $X_j = X(t_j)$, $\Delta t_j = t_{j+1}-t_j$, and $\Delta W_j = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)$ a white-noise increment. Following [24] the interaction intensity itself is made random in time:
   $$\kappa_j = \xi\,\Delta W_j$$
   where the constant $\xi$ is named the **conformity effect** — this single parameter is the paper's control knob. *Worked example:* with $n = 100{,}000$, $\xi = 8$, $\Delta t = 0.1$, a draw $\Delta W^{(\kappa)}_j = 0.25$ gives $\kappa_j = 8\times0.25 = 2.0$. Starting from $X_j = 0.20$: $\tanh(\kappa_j X_j) = \tanh(0.4) = 0.37995$, so $K = 0.37995 - 0.20 = \mathbf{0.17995}$ and $Q = (2/100{,}000)\left[1 - 0.20\times0.37995\right] = 2\times10^{-5}\times0.92401 = \mathbf{1.8480\times10^{-5}}$, hence $\sqrt{Q} = 0.0042989$. With a second draw $\Delta W_j = 0.158114$ (half a standard deviation, since $\mathrm{sd}(\Delta W)=\sqrt{\Delta t}=0.31623$): $X_{j+1} = 0.20 + 0.17995\times0.1 + 0.0042989\times0.158114 = 0.20 + 0.017995 + 0.000680 = \mathbf{0.218675}$. Note the size ordering — the drift contributes $0.018$ while the diffusion contributes $0.0007$, a factor of **26**; at $n = 100{,}000$ the internal noise of (4) is almost irrelevant and the dynamics are driven overwhelmingly by the randomness of $\kappa_j$.

6. **Order flow from each group.** Interacting traders each trade a fixed lot $b$, and since the net of buyers minus sellers is $n X_j$ lots, their **excess demand** is
   $$Q^{I}_{j} = b\,n\,X_j \tag{5}$$
   Fundamentalists estimate a fundamental value $S^{*}_j$ by forecasting future cash flows and discount rates (Gordon 1962, reference [29]); they buy when price is below it and sell when above, so their demand is a linear function of log mispricing:
   $$Q^{f}_{j} = a\,m\left(\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S_j\right) \tag{6}$$
   where $m$ is the number of fundamentalists and $a$ parametrises the reaction to the discrepancy. *Worked example:* with $b = 1$, $n = 100{,}000$ and $X_j = 0.20$, $Q^{I}_j = 1\times100{,}000\times0.20 = \mathbf{+20{,}000}$ shares of net demand (consistent with 60,000 buy lots minus 40,000 sell lots from point 4). With $a\,m = 100{,}000$ and the stock $2\%$ above fundamental in logs ($\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S_j = -0.02$), $Q^{f}_j = 100{,}000\times(-0.02) = \mathbf{-2{,}000}$ shares, i.e. fundamentalists supply 2,000 shares.

7. **Market clearing gives the price, and the return decomposes into sentiment change plus news.** A market maker matches buyers with sellers at the price where total demand equals total supply:
   $$Q^{f}_{j} + Q^{I}_{j} = a\,m\left(\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S_j\right) + b\,n\,X_j = 0 \tag{7}$$
   Solving (7) for price yields the model's central pricing relation,
   $$\ln S_j = \ln S^{*}_j + \gamma\,X_j, \qquad \gamma \equiv \frac{b\,n}{a\,m} \tag{8}$$
   so $\gamma$ is the **price impact of crowd sentiment**: the elasticity of log price with respect to the average investment attitude, equal to the ratio of interacting-trader order size to fundamentalist reaction capacity. This immediately classifies market states: $X_j = 0 \Rightarrow S_j = S^{*}_j$ (efficient); $X_j > 0 \Rightarrow S_j > S^{*}_j$ (**bull-market regime**); $X_j < 0 \Rightarrow S_j < S^{*}_j$ (**bear-market regime**). Differencing (8) gives the return:
   $$R_j = \ln S_j - \ln S_{j-1} = \left(\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S^{*}_{j-1}\right) + \gamma\left(X_j - X_{j-1}\right) \tag{9}$$
   The first bracket is assumed to be **white noise** — footnote 4 states this explicitly — which the author calls a logical entailment of the efficient market hypothesis (Malkiel 2003, reference [30]). Because the second term is present, the efficient market **does not hold** in this model: price depends on sentiment as well as fundamentals. *Worked example:* continuing point 5 with $\gamma = 1$ (i.e. $bn = am = 100{,}000$), $S^{*}_{j-1} = 100$ ($\ln = 4.60517$), $X_{j-1} = 0.20$, $X_j = 0.218675$, and a fundamental news shock of $\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S^{*}_{j-1} = +0.002$: the price before is $S_{j-1} = e^{4.60517+0.20} = \mathbf{122.14}$ — already $22\%$ above fundamental value purely on sentiment — and after, $S_j = e^{4.60717+0.218675} = \mathbf{124.69}$. The return is $R_j = 0.002 + 1\times(0.218675-0.20) = 0.002 + 0.018675 = \mathbf{0.020675}$, i.e. $2.07\%$, of which **90 % ($0.018675$) is sentiment change** and only 10 % is news.

8. **Trading volume is a deterministic function of the absolute sentiment.** From the same market-clearing condition (7), volume — the number of shares traded in a day — is
   $$V_j = b\,n\,\frac{\left|X_j\right| + 1}{2} \tag{10}$$
   The derivation is instructive: interacting traders post $bn(1+X_j)/2$ buy lots and $bn(1-X_j)/2$ sell lots, while fundamentalists take exactly the other side of the imbalance, $-bnX_j$ by (7). For $X_j>0$ total buys are $bn(1+X_j)/2$ and total sells are $bn(1-X_j)/2 + bnX_j = bn(1+X_j)/2$ — equal, as clearing requires — and the matched quantity is $bn(1+|X_j|)/2$; the case $X_j<0$ mirrors it, which is why the absolute value is required. *Worked example:* with $b=1$, $n=100{,}000$: at $X_{j-1}=0.20$, $V_{j-1} = 100{,}000\times(0.20+1)/2 = \mathbf{60{,}000}$ shares (cross-check: 60,000 interacting buy lots meet 40,000 interacting sell lots plus 20,000 fundamentalist sell lots ✓); at $X_j = 0.218675$, $V_j = 100{,}000\times1.218675/2 = \mathbf{60{,}934}$ shares. **A structural consequence the paper does not discuss:** since $X_j$ is an average of $\pm1$ variables it is confined to $[-1,1]$, so $V_j$ is confined to $\left[bn/2,\;bn\right] = [50{,}000,\;100{,}000]$ — the model's volume can vary by **at most a factor of 2**, and it can never be zero.

9. **Simulation results: one parameter sweeps the market through three distribution families.** The simulations of the coupled system (9)–(10) fix $\Delta t_j = 0.1$, a unit-scale parameter set to $1$, and $n = 100{,}000$ interacting traders, then vary only the conformity effect over $\xi = 0.1,\,2,\,8$. (The parameter set to $1$ is printed as a bare symbol that does not survive text extraction; from (9)–(10) it must be either the lot size $b$ or the impact coefficient $\gamma$, and whichever it is, the other is left unspecified — a reproducibility gap, since $R_j$ needs $\gamma$ and $V_j$ needs $bn$.) The results are stated as a clean progression. For a **weak conformity effect $\xi = 0.1$** (Figure 3), both the return and volume series show "only narrow Gaussian fluctuations", and both tails "can be approximated by **normal distributions**". For a **moderate conformity effect $\xi = 2$** (Figure 4) both series are visibly more volatile and both tails follow **exponential distributions**. For a **strong conformity effect $\xi = 8$** (Figure 5) both series show "clearly clustered volatility, and consist of laminar phases and chaotic bursts" — the same qualitative signature the author reported for the real Fujita data in Figure 1(a) — and both tails show **power-law decay**. The summary sentence is: as the interaction among the interacting traders strengthens, clustered volatility becomes more definite and the shape of both distributions changes **from Gaussian to power law**. *Worked check of the mechanism (not in the paper).* Because $\kappa_j = \xi\Delta W_j$ with $\mathrm{sd}(\Delta W_j) = \sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.31623$, the interaction strength is Gaussian with $\mathrm{sd}(\kappa) = 0.31623\,\xi$. The drift $K(X) = \tanh(\kappa X) - X$ has $K'(0) = \kappa - 1$, so $X = 0$ is stable only while $\kappa < 1$: the model has a **pitchfork bifurcation at the critical value $\kappa_c = 1$**. The fraction of steps that cross it is $P(|\kappa|>1) = 2\left[1-\Phi\!\left(1/(0.31623\xi)\right)\right]$, giving $\mathbf{\approx 10^{-219}}$ for $\xi = 0.1$ ($31.6$ standard deviations), $\mathbf{11.4\%}$ for $\xi = 2$ ($1.58$ sd), and $\mathbf{69.3\%}$ for $\xi = 8$ ($0.395$ sd). That is the whole result: at $\xi = 0.1$ the system never leaves the linear regime and behaves as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process $dX = -X\,dt + \sqrt{2/n}\,dW$ with stationary $\mathrm{sd}(X) = \sqrt{1/n} = 0.00316$, so returns and volume are Gaussian and volume sits at $50{,}126 \pm 95$ shares; at $\xi = 8$ the system is supercritical most of the time, and for $\kappa = 2$ and $\kappa = 8$ the non-zero fixed points of $\tanh(\kappa X) = X$ are at $X^{*} = \pm0.9575$ and $\pm0.99999977$, so sentiment slams against $\pm1$ and returns jump by up to $2\gamma$.

10. **Conclusion and stated limitations.** The paper's own verdict is narrow and honest: "the point of the results shown by the simulation is that **intensity of the interaction among the interacting traders is a key factor which generates power-law distributions** of returns and of trading volume." It then names its own principal gap explicitly — "the question, which has been touched in introduction but not explored, is **analysis of the relationships between returns and trading volume**. It needs further consideration." In other words, the paper delivers power laws for returns and for volume *separately*, but does not deliver the volume–return relationship that its introduction motivates. Other limitations are visible rather than stated: a single stock as the entire empirical base; no estimated exponents or statistical tests anywhere; simulation results reported only as figures with no tail-fit numbers; the constants $\gamma$, $b$, $a$, $m$ never calibrated to the Fujita data, so the model is never confronted quantitatively with the empirical section it is meant to explain.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### The Ising / mean-field spin model as a market microfoundation

The paper's mechanism is the two-state Ising model of statistical mechanics transplanted into a market: each interacting trader is a spin $u_i = \pm1$ (buyer/seller), the "magnetic field" is the crowd's own average state $X$, and the "temperature" is inverse to the interaction intensity $\kappa$. Equations (1)–(2) are the logistic (Glauber-type) flip probabilities, and equations (3)–(4) are the mean-field limit — a Langevin equation whose drift $\tanh(\kappa X) - X$ is the classic self-consistency condition for spontaneous magnetisation. The lineage is explicit: Chowdhury & Stauffer's generalized spin model of financial markets and Bornholdt's expectation-bubble spin model (references [25], [26]), plus the author's own earlier interacting-agent papers [21]–[24].

**Why it matters:** It converts herding from a **narrative into an equation with one control parameter**. Everything downstream — clustered volatility, fat tails, bull and bear regimes — is a consequence of a single scalar $\kappa$ passing a threshold, which means the model is **falsifiable and calibratable** in a way that verbal accounts of herding are not. It also inherits half a century of statistical-mechanics results about critical phenomena, giving the modeller a ready-made toolkit (order parameter, critical exponent, susceptibility) for market questions.

### The conformity effect $\xi$ and the critical point $\kappa_c = 1$

The paper's headline finding is that raising the conformity effect from $\xi = 0.1$ to $2$ to $8$ marches the return and volume distributions from **Gaussian to exponential to power-law**. The reason, implicit in the drift $K(X) = \tanh(\kappa X) - X$, is that $K'(0) = \kappa - 1$: the neutral state $X=0$ is stable below $\kappa = 1$ and loses stability above it, spawning two symmetric attractors. Because $\kappa_j = \xi\Delta W_j$ is Gaussian with standard deviation $0.31623\,\xi$, $\xi$ controls how often the market sits on the far side of the critical point — never (${\sim}10^{-219}$) at $\xi=0.1$, $11.4\%$ of steps at $\xi=2$, $69.3\%$ at $\xi=8$.

**Why it matters:** This is the paper's **most transferable idea**, and it is quantitative. It identifies fat tails not as an exogenous statistical assumption but as the fingerprint of a system **intermittently crossing a bifurcation** — the same mechanism as on–off intermittency in random dynamical systems, which is known to produce power laws. It also means the fat-tail exponent is not a free parameter to be fitted but a **derived function of how often the crowd goes supercritical**, which is in principle measurable from sentiment or breadth data.

### Price formation by explicit market clearing, and the impact coefficient $\gamma = bn/(am)$

Rather than postulating a price process, the paper writes down both groups' order flow — (5) for interacting traders, (6) for fundamentalists — and solves for the price that balances them, (7), obtaining $\ln S_j = \ln S^{*}_j + \gamma X_j$ with $\gamma = bn/(am)$. Return then splits cleanly by (9) into a white-noise fundamental component and a sentiment-change component scaled by $\gamma$.

**Why it matters:** It gives the model **economic content rather than statistical curve-fitting** — exactly the deficiency the author charges against the econophysics literature. Three practical consequences follow. First, mispricing is directly observable in the model as $\gamma X_j$, so bubbles and crashes are just excursions of $X$. Second, $\gamma$ is a **structural, interpretable ratio** — speculative order size over arbitrage capacity — so it predicts that markets with fewer or more capital-constrained fundamentalists (small caps, illiquid names, retail-heavy venues) exhibit larger sentiment-driven price swings for identical herding. Third, the return equation is a **first difference of a bounded quantity**, which is what caps the model's return magnitude and is the origin of a structural weakness discussed in Section 5.

### Trading volume as a function of the level of crowd imbalance

Equation (10), $V_j = bn(|X_j|+1)/2$, is the paper's volume model: volume rises linearly with the *absolute* crowd imbalance and is minimal ($bn/2$) when buyers and sellers are evenly split. Combined with (9), the model implies that volume tracks the **level** of sentiment while return tracks its **change**.

**Why it matters:** It is a concrete, testable microstructural claim about *why* volume and volatility co-move, and it makes an unusual prediction: volume is **maximal at sentiment extremes**, where price is furthest from fundamental value, not at moments of maximal price change. That is a genuinely discriminating hypothesis against the sequential-information-arrival tradition (Copeland 1976, reference [4]) in which volume is driven by information flow. It is also where the model is most obviously incomplete — because $|X_j|\le1$, the predicted volume range spans only a factor of two, which cannot match the multi-order-of-magnitude volume distribution the empirical section claims to be power-law.

### The empirical bubble-and-burst sample: Fujita Corporation, 1975–2002

The empirical base is one mid-cap TSE construction stock over 28 years, deliberately chosen to straddle Japan's asset bubble and its collapse. The returns series is described as having clustered volatility made of laminar phases and chaotic bursts, and both the return and volume distributions are asserted to have power-law tails on semi-log plots.

**Why it matters:** The sample choice is the **strongest and weakest part of the paper simultaneously**. Strongest, because a bubble-and-burst window is precisely where a herding model should be tested — it maximises the range of $\xi$ the data plausibly traverses, and the author's qualitative match (laminar-plus-burst in Figure 1(a) versus the same in Figure 5(a) at $\xi=8$) is a fair visual comparison. Weakest, because $N = 1$ stock with no exponent estimates, no confidence intervals and no cross-sectional replication cannot distinguish a power law from a lognormal, a stretched exponential, or a mixture of Gaussians with time-varying variance — all of which look curved on a semi-log plot.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

### 4.1 Interacting-trader sentiment dynamics (the herding engine)

**Goal:** Evolve a single scalar — the crowd's average buy/sell attitude $X$ — forward one time step, in a way that makes traders more likely to join whichever side is currently in the majority, with the strength of that pull itself random.

**Inputs:** current sentiment $X_j \in [-1,1]$; number of interacting traders $n$; conformity effect $\xi$; time step $\Delta t_j$; two independent standard Wiener increments for the step (one for the interaction strength, one for the sentiment noise). **Outputs:** next sentiment $X_{j+1}$, plus the diagnostic quantities $\kappa_j$, drift $K(X_j)$ and variance rate $Q(X_j)$.

**Steps:**

1. **Draw the interaction strength for this step** — Set $\kappa_j = \xi\,\Delta W_j^{(\kappa)}$, where $\Delta W_j^{(\kappa)}$ is a Wiener increment with standard deviation $\sqrt{\Delta t_j}$. Interpretation: the market's willingness to herd is not constant; it flares and subsides. The constant $\xi$ — the **conformity effect** — sets how wildly it flares. Note that $\kappa_j$ can come out *negative*, which means contrarian behaviour for that step.
2. **Convert sentiment into flip probabilities** — Compute $W_{\uparrow}(X_j) = \left[1+\exp(-2\kappa_j X_j)\right]^{-1}$ (eq. 1) and $W_{\downarrow}(X_j) = \left[1+\exp(2\kappa_j X_j)\right]^{-1}$ (eq. 2). These are the per-trader probabilities of switching from seller to buyer and buyer to seller. Because $W_{\uparrow}+W_{\downarrow}=1$ and both are logistic in $\kappa_j X_j$, a positive crowd tilt raises the chance of joining the buyers and lowers the chance of defecting to the sellers *at the same time* — the mechanical definition of herding. Equivalently $W_{\uparrow} = \tfrac12(1+\tanh\kappa_j X_j)$.
3. **Aggregate to a drift** — In the mean-field limit, the net rate of change of $X$ is the drift $K(X_j) = \tanh(\kappa_j X_j) - X_j$. Reading it: $\tanh(\kappa_j X_j)$ is where the crowd *wants* to be given the current tilt, $-X_j$ is a relaxation pulling back to where it *is*, and the balance decides whether herding wins. This is the term that changes sign in character at $\kappa_j = 1$.
4. **Aggregate to a variance rate** — Compute $Q(X_j) = \frac{2}{n}\left[1 - X_j\tanh(\kappa_j X_j)\right]$. This is the residual randomness from finitely many traders flipping independently. Two properties matter: it is $O(1/n)$, so it vanishes in a large market, and it shrinks toward zero as $|X_j| \to 1$ with strong herding, because a unanimous crowd has nothing left to flip.
5. **Step the state** — Apply the Euler–Maruyama update $X_{j+1} = X_j + K(X_j)\Delta t_j + \sqrt{Q(X_j)}\,\Delta W_j$ (eq. 4), with $\Delta W_j$ an independent Wiener increment of standard deviation $\sqrt{\Delta t_j}$. The result stays inside $[-1,1]$ automatically: at $X = \pm1$ the drift points inward ($K(1) = \tanh\kappa - 1 < 0$) and the noise amplitude has collapsed.

**Worked example** (one step, using the paper's simulation parameters $n = 100{,}000$, $\Delta t = 0.1$, and the strong-conformity case $\xi = 8$; start from $X_j = 0.20$):

- Step 1: draw $\Delta W^{(\kappa)}_j = 0.25$ (about $0.79$ standard deviations, since $\sqrt{0.1}=0.31623$) $\Rightarrow \kappa_j = 8\times0.25 = \mathbf{2.0}$ — supercritical, above $\kappa_c = 1$.
- Step 2: $2\kappa_j X_j = 0.80$, so $W_{\uparrow} = 1/(1+e^{-0.8}) = \mathbf{0.68997}$, $W_{\downarrow} = 1/(1+e^{0.8}) = \mathbf{0.31003}$. In head-count terms, $n_{+} = 60{,}000$ buyers and $n_{-} = 40{,}000$ sellers, so per unit time $27{,}599$ sellers convert and $18{,}602$ buyers defect — net $+8{,}997$.
- Step 3: $\tanh(0.4) = 0.37995$, so $K = 0.37995 - 0.20 = \mathbf{+0.17995}$. Sanity check against the head-count route: $(2/n)\times(\text{net conversions}) = 2\times8{,}997/100{,}000 = 0.17994$ ✓ — the drift *is* the net conversion rate.
- Step 4: $Q = (2/100{,}000)\left[1 - 0.20\times0.37995\right] = 2\times10^{-5}\times0.92401 = \mathbf{1.8480\times10^{-5}}$, so $\sqrt{Q} = 0.0042989$.
- Step 5: draw $\Delta W_j = 0.158114$ (half a standard deviation) $\Rightarrow X_{j+1} = 0.20 + 0.17995\times0.1 + 0.0042989\times0.158114 = 0.20 + 0.0179950 + 0.0006797 = \mathbf{0.2186747}$.
- Head-count cross-check of the whole step: $n_{+}$ moves to $60{,}000 + 0.1\times8{,}997 = 60{,}899.7$, so $X = 2\times60{,}899.7/100{,}000 - 1 = 0.217994$, matching the drift-only prediction $0.217995$ ✓ (the $0.00068$ difference is exactly the diffusion term).
- Contrast case for the same starting state with $\xi = 0.1$: the largest plausible draw $\Delta W^{(\kappa)} = 3\sqrt{0.1} = 0.949$ gives $\kappa_j = 0.0949$, so $K = \tanh(0.019) - 0.20 = 0.019 - 0.20 = -0.181$ — strongly **mean-reverting**, and $X$ collapses back toward 0 rather than running away. This one comparison is the entire difference between the paper's Gaussian and power-law regimes.

### 4.2 Market clearing: turning sentiment into price, return and volume

**Goal:** Given the crowd's sentiment for the day, find the single price at which a market maker can match every buy order with a sell order, then read off the day's return and traded volume.

**Inputs:** sentiment $X_j$ and previous sentiment $X_{j-1}$; fundamental value $S^{*}_j$ and its previous value $S^{*}_{j-1}$ (evolving as white noise in logs); lot size $b$; trader counts $n$ and $m$; fundamentalist reaction coefficient $a$. **Outputs:** market-clearing price $S_j$, log return $R_j$, trading volume $V_j$, and a regime label (bull / bear / efficient).

**Steps:**

1. **Compute interacting-trader order flow** — Their net (excess) demand is $Q^{I}_j = b\,n\,X_j$ (eq. 5): each of the $n$ traders trades a fixed lot $b$, and the buyers outnumber the sellers by $nX_j$. Only the *imbalance* enters the price; the matched part enters the volume.
2. **Write fundamentalist order flow as a function of price** — $Q^{f}_j = a\,m\left(\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S_j\right)$ (eq. 6). This is a downward-sloping demand curve in log price: fundamentalists absorb whatever the crowd pushes, in proportion to how far price has been pushed away from fair value. They are the model's only stabilising force.
3. **Impose the clearing condition** — Set total demand to zero: $a\,m\left(\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S_j\right) + b\,n\,X_j = 0$ (eq. 7). Economically, the market maker raises price until fundamentalists are willing to supply exactly the shares the herd wants to buy.
4. **Solve for price** — $\ln S_j = \ln S^{*}_j + \gamma X_j$ with $\gamma = bn/(am)$ (eq. 8). The mispricing is *linear in sentiment* with slope $\gamma$; label the state bull if $X_j>0$, bear if $X_j<0$, efficient if $X_j=0$.
5. **Difference to get the return** — $R_j = \left(\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S^{*}_{j-1}\right) + \gamma\left(X_j - X_{j-1}\right)$ (eq. 9). The first term is exogenous news (assumed white noise, footnote 4); the second is endogenous sentiment revision. All of the model's interesting behaviour lives in the second term.
6. **Compute the volume from the matched quantity** — $V_j = b\,n\,\frac{|X_j|+1}{2}$ (eq. 10). Derivation: interacting traders submit $bn(1+X_j)/2$ buy lots and $bn(1-X_j)/2$ sell lots; fundamentalists take the other side of the imbalance, $-bnX_j$; the two sides then match at $bn(1+|X_j|)/2$. Volume is minimal at $bn/2$ when the crowd is evenly split and maximal at $bn$ when it is unanimous.

**Worked example** (continuing from §4.1's output, with $b = 1$, $n = 100{,}000$, $a\,m = 100{,}000$ so $\gamma = 1$; day $j-1$ has $X_{j-1} = 0.20$ and $S^{*}_{j-1} = 100$):

- Day $j-1$, step 1: $Q^{I}_{j-1} = 1\times100{,}000\times0.20 = +20{,}000$ shares net demand.
- Day $j-1$, steps 2–4: clearing requires $Q^{f}_{j-1} = -20{,}000$, i.e. $\ln S^{*}_{j-1} - \ln S_{j-1} = -20{,}000/100{,}000 = -0.20$; so $\ln S_{j-1} = 4.60517 + 0.20 = 4.80517$ and $S_{j-1} = \mathbf{122.14}$ — a **bull regime** with the stock $22.1\%$ above fundamental value on sentiment alone.
- Day $j-1$, step 6: $V_{j-1} = 100{,}000\times(0.20+1)/2 = \mathbf{60{,}000}$ shares. Book cross-check: buys $= 60{,}000$; sells $= 40{,}000$ (interacting) $+\;20{,}000$ (fundamentalists) $= 60{,}000$ ✓.
- Day $j$: sentiment arrives from §4.1 as $X_j = 0.218675$; fundamental news is $\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S^{*}_{j-1} = +0.002$, so $\ln S^{*}_j = 4.607170$ and $S^{*}_j = 100.200$.
- Day $j$, steps 1–4: $Q^{I}_j = +21{,}867$ shares; clearing gives $\ln S_j = 4.607170 + 1\times0.218675 = 4.825845$, so $S_j = \mathbf{124.69}$ — still bull, now $24.4\%$ rich.
- Day $j$, step 5: $R_j = 0.002 + (0.218675 - 0.20) = 0.002 + 0.018675 = \mathbf{0.020675}$, i.e. $\mathbf{+2.07\%}$. Direct check from prices: $\ln(124.69/122.14) = \ln(1.02088) = 0.02067$ ✓. Decomposition: $\mathbf{90.3\%}$ of the move is sentiment, $9.7\%$ is news.
- Day $j$, step 6: $V_j = 100{,}000\times1.218675/2 = \mathbf{60{,}934}$ shares — volume up $1.6\%$ while price is up $2.1\%$, a positive contemporaneous volume–return co-movement that arises here only because $X$ was already positive and rose further.
- Regime-limit check: pushing $X_j\to1$ (unanimous buying) gives the model's ceiling — price $= e^{\ln S^{*}+1} = 2.718\times S^{*}$ (a $172\%$ premium) and $V = 100{,}000$ shares, exactly twice the $X=0$ floor of $50{,}000$.

### 4.3 The full simulation loop and the conformity sweep

**Goal:** Generate synthetic return and volume series long enough to estimate their distributions, and determine how the shape of those distributions depends on the single parameter $\xi$.

**Inputs:** $n = 100{,}000$; $\Delta t_j = 0.1$; the unit-scale parameter set to $1$; a grid of conformity values $\xi \in \{0.1,\,2,\,8\}$; an initial sentiment $X_0$; a white-noise source for the fundamental log value. **Outputs:** for each $\xi$, a time series $\{R_j\}$, a time series $\{V_j\}$, and semi-log plots of their probability distributions (the paper's Figures 3–5).

**Steps:**

1. **Fix the invariant parameters** — Set $n$, $\Delta t_j$ and the scale parameter once; they are held constant across the whole study so that any change in output is attributable to $\xi$ alone. This is the design that licenses the paper's causal claim about the conformity effect.
2. **Select a conformity value** — Choose $\xi$ from the grid. This is the only quantity that varies between Figures 3, 4 and 5.
3. **Iterate the sentiment engine** — For each step $j$, run §4.1 steps 1–5 to obtain $X_{j+1}$ from $X_j$: fresh $\kappa_j = \xi\Delta W_j^{(\kappa)}$, then the drift-plus-diffusion update (4).
4. **Map each state to observables** — For each step, run §4.2 steps 1–6 to obtain $R_j$ from (9) and $V_j$ from (10). Add the white-noise fundamental increment to the return.
5. **Inspect the time series for volatility clustering** — Plot $R_j$ and $V_j$ against time and look for the laminar-phase/chaotic-burst alternation that characterises the real Fujita data in Figure 1(a).
6. **Plot the distributions on a semi-log scale and classify the tails** — Following footnote 2's diagnostic: straight tails on a semi-log plot mean an **exponential**; tails that fall faster and curve down mean a **normal**; tails that decay more slowly than any straight line mean a **power law**. Assign each $\xi$ its tail family.
7. **Compare with the empirical distributions** — Judge which $\xi$ reproduces the Figure 1(b) and Figure 2(b) shapes for Fujita, and conclude that strong conformity is required.

**Worked example** (the sweep, with the criticality arithmetic that the paper leaves implicit):

- Step 1: $n = 100{,}000 \Rightarrow$ diffusion amplitude $\sqrt{Q}\le\sqrt{2/n} = 0.00447$; $\Delta t = 0.1 \Rightarrow \mathrm{sd}(\Delta W) = 0.31623$.
- Steps 2–3 at $\boldsymbol{\xi = 0.1}$: $\mathrm{sd}(\kappa) = 0.1\times0.31623 = 0.031623$, so reaching the critical $\kappa = 1$ requires a $31.6$-sigma draw and $P(|\kappa|>1)\approx\mathbf{10^{-219}}$ — effectively never. With $\kappa\approx0$ the drift linearises to $K\approx-X$ and (4) becomes the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process $dX = -X\,dt + \sqrt{2/n}\,dW$, whose stationary variance is $Q/2 = (2/n)/2 = 1/n = 10^{-5}$, i.e. $\mathrm{sd}(X) = \mathbf{0.00316}$ and $X$ is exactly Gaussian.
- Step 4 at $\xi = 0.1$: $V_j = 50{,}000(1+|X_j|)$, and for a folded normal $E|X| = \sigma\sqrt{2/\pi} = 0.00252$, $\mathrm{sd}|X| = \sigma\sqrt{1-2/\pi} = 0.00191$, so $V = \mathbf{50{,}126 \pm 95}$ shares — a $0.19\%$ coefficient of variation. Returns are $R_j \approx \gamma\Delta X + \text{news}$, a difference of Gaussians, hence Gaussian. This reproduces the paper's Figure 3 verdict ("narrow Gaussian fluctuations", normal tails) **exactly and analytically**.
- Steps 2–3 at $\boldsymbol{\xi = 2}$: $\mathrm{sd}(\kappa) = 0.63246$; $1/0.63246 = 1.581$ sigma, so $P(|\kappa|>1) = 2\times0.05692 = \mathbf{11.4\%}$ of steps are supercritical. The system now spends about one step in nine being pushed away from $X=0$ before relaxing back — bursts exist but are short, and the tail thickens from Gaussian to roughly **exponential**, matching Figure 4.
- Steps 2–3 at $\boldsymbol{\xi = 8}$: $\mathrm{sd}(\kappa) = 2.52982$; $1/2.52982 = 0.395$ sigma, so $P(|\kappa|>1) = 2\times0.34632 = \mathbf{69.3\%}$. Supercritical is now the norm. The non-zero fixed points of $\tanh(\kappa X) = X$ are $X^{*} = \pm0.9575$ at $\kappa = 2$ and $\pm0.9999998$ at $\kappa = 8$, so sentiment is repeatedly driven to the boundaries and yanked back — the on/off alternation between quiescent ($\kappa<1$) and explosive ($\kappa>1$) steps is textbook **on–off intermittency**, which is the standard mechanism for generating power laws, and it produces the laminar-plus-burst series and power-law tails of Figure 5.
- Steps 5–7: at $\xi = 8$ the return range reaches $|R|\le2\gamma$ (sentiment flipping from $-1$ to $+1$) and volume spans $[50{,}000,\,100{,}000]$; the qualitative shape matches Fujita's Figure 1(a), which is the paper's stated basis for concluding that strong conformity is what real markets exhibit.

### 4.4 Empirical power-law identification protocol (the paper's data procedure)

**Goal:** Decide, from a single stock's daily history, whether the return and volume distributions have power-law tails rather than exponential or Gaussian ones.

**Inputs:** the TSE daily database — closing prices and daily share volume for Fujita Corporation (ticker 1806), January 1975 to January 2002. **Outputs:** a time-series plot and a semi-log probability plot for each of returns and volume, plus a verbal tail classification.

**Steps:**

1. **Extract the two raw series** — Pull daily closing price $S_t$ and daily traded share count $V_t$ for the chosen ticker over the full 28-year window, chosen to include Japan's bubble inflation and collapse.
2. **Construct returns** — Compute the log price change from one day's close to the next, $R_t = \ln S_t - \ln S_{t-1}$. Volume needs no transformation; it is used as the raw share count.
3. **Plot the time series and look for clustering** — Inspect $\{R_t\}$ for the alternation of quiet stretches and violent bursts ("laminar phases and chaotic bursts"), the qualitative signature the model must reproduce.
4. **Build the empirical probability distribution and plot it semi-logarithmically** — Histogram the series, then plot probability on a log axis against the raw value on a linear axis.
5. **Classify the tail against the semi-log ruler** — Apply footnote 2's rule: *if the tails of the probability distribution can be approximated by a linear line, then the probability distribution follows an exponential function.* Tails that bend **above** the straight line decay more slowly than exponentially, which the paper reads as evidence for the power law $P(R>x)\sim x^{-\beta}$.
6. **Repeat for volume and conclude** — Apply steps 4–5 to $V_t$; the paper reports power-law behaviour for both, and hands the finding to the model as the phenomenon to be explained.

**Worked example** (the semi-log ruler applied to concrete numbers, since the paper publishes only plots):

- Step 2: two consecutive Fujita closes of 420 yen and 428.4 yen give $R_t = \ln(428.4/420) = \ln(1.02) = \mathbf{0.019803}$, about $+1.98\%$.
- Steps 4–5, exponential reference: suppose the return tail is exponential with scale $0.02$ and $P(R>0.02) = 0.010$. Then $P(R>x) = 0.010\,e^{-(x-0.02)/0.02}$, and on a semi-log plot $\ln P$ against $x$ is the **straight line** $\ln P = \ln 0.010 - (x-0.02)/0.02$ with slope $-50$ per unit return. Evaluating: $P(0.04) = 0.010\,e^{-1} = \mathbf{3.68\times10^{-3}}$; $P(0.06) = 0.010\,e^{-2} = \mathbf{1.35\times10^{-3}}$; $P(0.10) = 0.010\,e^{-4} = \mathbf{1.83\times10^{-4}}$; $P(0.20) = 0.010\,e^{-9} = \mathbf{1.23\times10^{-6}}$.
- Steps 4–5, power-law alternative with $\beta = 3$ pinned to the same anchor: $C = 0.010\times0.02^{3} = 8\times10^{-8}$, so $P(0.04) = 8\times10^{-8}/6.4\times10^{-5} = \mathbf{1.25\times10^{-3}}$; $P(0.06) = 8\times10^{-8}/2.16\times10^{-4} = \mathbf{3.70\times10^{-4}}$; $P(0.10) = 8\times10^{-8}/10^{-3} = \mathbf{8.00\times10^{-5}}$; $P(0.20) = 8\times10^{-8}/8\times10^{-3} = \mathbf{1.00\times10^{-5}}$.
- Step 5 verdict on this synthetic pair: over the near tail the exponential is actually *above* the $\beta = 3$ power law (at $x = 0.04$: $3.68\times10^{-3}$ versus $1.25\times10^{-3}$; at $x = 0.10$: $1.83\times10^{-4}$ versus $8.00\times10^{-5}$). The two curves cross only at $x \approx \mathbf{0.134}$, and the power law's advantage becomes large just $8.1\times$ at $x = 0.20$ and $2.2\times10^{4}$ at $x = 0.40$. The lesson the worked numbers expose is a genuine methodological weakness in the paper's visual method: **the two tail families are numerically indistinguishable across the bulk and near tail and separate only far out**, where a 28-year daily sample holds almost no observations. With roughly $28\times250 = \mathbf{7{,}000}$ trading days, the expected count beyond $x = 0.20$ is $7{,}000\times1.00\times10^{-5} = \mathbf{0.07}$ observations under the power law and $7{,}000\times1.23\times10^{-6} = \mathbf{0.009}$ under the exponential — both effectively zero, so the very region that discriminates the hypotheses is empty in this dataset and the classification cannot be made by eye.
- Step 6 for volume: the same ruler is applied to raw share counts. Note that a daily volume series of a mid-cap stock spans several orders of magnitude, whereas the model of §4.2 step 6 confines $V$ to a factor of 2 — so the empirical volume tail and the model's volume tail cannot be compared on the same axes without rescaling.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **Criticality gauge from market breadth ($\hat\kappa$ versus $\kappa_c = 1$)** — Take §4.1 steps 1–3 and run them backwards on real data. Build a daily breadth proxy for the crowd state, $X_t = (\#\text{advancers} - \#\text{decliners})/(\#\text{advancers} + \#\text{decliners})$, then estimate the interaction strength from the drift relation of step 3 by regressing $\Delta X_t$ on $X_t$: since $E[\Delta X] \approx \left(\tanh(\kappa X) - X\right)\Delta t \approx (\kappa-1)X\Delta t$ for small $X$, the OLS slope $\hat\phi$ of $\Delta X_t$ on $X_t$ gives $\hat\kappa = 1 + \hat\phi/\Delta t$. Publish $\hat\kappa$ as a rolling regime gauge, with $\hat\kappa \to 1$ as the fragility warning.
  - **Strong:** Bubble-and-crash windows — the paper's own sample was chosen to span Japan's bubble and burst, and it reports that only the strong-conformity setting $\xi = 8$ reproduces the laminar-plus-burst signature of the real Fujita series (Figure 5(a) versus Figure 1(a)), so the regimes the gauge is meant to separate are exactly the ones the paper demonstrates are separable. *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** Broad indices with many constituents — the estimator's noise floor is set by $Q = 2(1-X\tanh\kappa X)/n \le 2/n$, so the standard error of $X_t$ scales as $n^{-1/2}$; going from 30 to 3,000 constituents cuts the measurement noise by a factor of 10 and makes the $\hat\kappa$ regression tractable. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Near the critical point itself, precisely where the gauge is most wanted — at $\kappa = 1$ the linear term vanishes and the drift becomes $K(X) = \tanh X - X \approx -X^{3}/3$, so the regression of $\Delta X$ on $X$ has slope $0$ and $\hat\kappa$ is unidentified from the linear fit; a cubic term must be estimated instead, and its coefficient is $1/3$ of a third-order effect, requiring far more data. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Any market where the advance/decline proxy is not the model's $X$ — the model defines $X$ as the buy/sell attitude of one specific trader group of *fixed size* $n$, whereas breadth mixes fundamentalists and herders; if a fraction $f$ of the index is fundamentalist, the measured breadth is a convex combination and the estimated $\hat\kappa$ is attenuated by roughly $(1-f)$, biasing every reading downward toward "safe". *(From AI)*

- **Sentiment-impact coefficient $\gamma = bn/(am)$ as a cross-sectional fragility score** — Reuse §4.2 step 4 as an estimation target rather than a simulation input. Regress log price on a fundamental anchor (book value, analyst consensus, or a slow-moving fair-value proxy) plus a sentiment proxy: $\ln S_t = \ln \hat S^{*}_t + \gamma X_t + \varepsilon_t$. The fitted $\gamma$ measures how many log points of mispricing one unit of crowd imbalance buys, and by construction is large where speculative order flow ($bn$) is large relative to arbitrage capacity ($am$). Rank a universe by $\hat\gamma$ and use it as a fragility or short-crowding screen.
  - **Strong:** Small and mid-cap names with thin arbitrage capital — $\gamma$ is *defined* as the ratio of interacting-trader lot flow to fundamentalist reaction capacity, so mechanically $\partial\gamma/\partial(am) = -bn/(am)^{2} < 0$: halving the fundamentalist population doubles the sentiment premium for identical herding. The paper chose exactly such a name (Fujita, a mid-size construction company) as its "typical example". *(From paper + AI)*
  - **Strong:** Regime-independence of the specification — because (8) is exactly linear in $X_t$ with an additive fundamental term, and (9) shows the fundamental increment is assumed white noise, the regression's error is by construction serially uncorrelated, so OLS is consistent and the standard errors are usable without a GARCH correction on the mean equation. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Trending markets where the fundamental anchor drifts — the model's identification rests on footnote 4's assumption that $\ln S^{*}_j - \ln S^{*}_{j-1}$ is white noise. If the anchor has drift $\mu$, then $\ln S_t$ acquires a $\mu t$ term correlated with any trending sentiment proxy, and $\hat\gamma$ absorbs it: the omitted-variable bias is $\mathrm{Cov}(X_t, \mu t)/\mathrm{Var}(X_t)$, which is positive during a persistent bull market and inflates every fragility score at once. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Any absolute interpretation of $\hat\gamma$ — the paper never calibrates $b$, $a$, $n$ or $m$ to the Fujita data, and the simulations report a single unit-scale parameter set to 1 without identifying whether it is $b$ or $\gamma$ (see Summary point 9), so there is no published anchor for the scale. Only cross-sectional or time-series *relative* $\hat\gamma$ comparisons are defensible. *(From paper)*

- **Persistent-conformity replacement for $\kappa_j = \xi\Delta W_j$** — Modify §4.1 step 1 only: replace the memoryless draw $\kappa_j = \xi\Delta W_j$ with a mean-reverting process such as $\kappa_{j+1} = \kappa_j + \theta(\bar\kappa - \kappa_j)\Delta t + \sigma_\kappa\Delta W^{(\kappa)}_j$, leaving steps 2–5 and all of §4.2 untouched. This fixes two defects at once: it makes herding intensity persist across days (as sentiment plainly does), and it removes the discretisation artefact described below.
  - **Strong:** Volatility-clustering realism at any $\Delta t$ — the paper's own diagnosis is that clustered volatility comes from the market repeatedly crossing the critical point, so making the crossings autocorrelated with timescale $1/\theta$ produces bursts of controllable duration; the paper obtains bursts only by brute-forcing $\xi = 8$ so that $69.3\%$ of *independent* steps are supercritical (Summary point 9). *(From paper + AI)*
  - **Strong:** It removes a provable scale inconsistency in the original. Since $\mathrm{sd}(\Delta W_j) = \sqrt{\Delta t}$, the specification $\kappa_j = \xi\Delta W_j$ implies $\mathrm{sd}(\kappa) = \xi\sqrt{\Delta t}$ — so the interaction strength depends on the *numerical time step*. Halving $\Delta t$ from $0.1$ to $0.05$ shrinks $\mathrm{sd}(\kappa)$ from $0.632$ to $0.447$ at $\xi = 2$, which drops $P(|\kappa|>1)$ from $11.4\%$ to $2.5\%$ and moves the model out of the exponential regime without any change to the economics. **$\xi$ is therefore not a $\Delta t$-invariant structural parameter, and the paper's three regime labels are properties of the discretisation as much as of the market.** An Ornstein–Uhlenbeck $\kappa$ has a $\Delta t$-independent stationary variance $\sigma_\kappa^{2}/(2\theta)$ and is immune. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Loss of the paper's one-parameter clarity — the replacement introduces $\theta$, $\bar\kappa$ and $\sigma_\kappa$ where there was one $\xi$, and $\theta$ and $\sigma_\kappa$ are only jointly identified through the stationary variance $\sigma_\kappa^{2}/(2\theta)$, so fitting to unconditional return moments alone leaves a one-dimensional ridge in the likelihood. Autocorrelations of $|R_t|$ must be added as moment conditions to pin $\theta$ down. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Persistent supercriticality is explosive in a way the original is not — with $\bar\kappa > 1$ and small $\sigma_\kappa$, $X$ locks onto a fixed point ($X^{*} = 0.9575$ at $\kappa = 2$, $0.9999998$ at $\kappa = 8$) and *stops moving*, so by (9) returns go to zero and by (10) volume pins at its ceiling — a frozen, unanimous market. The parameterisation must keep $\bar\kappa$ below or near 1 and rely on excursions, otherwise realism gets worse, not better. *(From AI)*

- **Multiplicative rebuild of the volume equation (10)** — Keep §4.2 steps 1–5 exactly as they are but replace step 6's $V_j = bn(|X_j|+1)/2$ with a multiplicative form such as $V_j = V_0\exp\!\left(\lambda|X_j|\right)$, or make the lot size state-dependent, $b_j = b_0 e^{\lambda|X_j|}$, so that the *same* clearing logic produces volume spanning orders of magnitude. Fit $\lambda$ to the empirical volume distribution the paper documents for Fujita.
  - **Strong:** It repairs a provable range failure in the original. Because $X_j$ is an average of $\pm1$ variables, $|X_j|\le1$ strictly, so (10) confines volume to $\left[bn/2,\,bn\right]$ — **a maximum dynamic range of exactly $2\times$** (with the paper's $b=1,\,n=100{,}000$: $50{,}000$ to $100{,}000$ shares). A power-law tail is a statement about behaviour across decades of scale; a variable bounded within a factor of two cannot exhibit one, so the model's volume mechanism cannot in principle reproduce the empirical volume distribution of Figure 2(b) that it was built to explain. The multiplicative form has unbounded support for any $\lambda>0$. *(From AI)*
  - **Strong:** Empirical support for the sign of the effect already exists in the model's own logic — volume is maximal at sentiment extremes (Section 3), and the paper's simulations do show volume becoming visibly more volatile as $\xi$ rises from $0.1$ to $8$ (Figures 3(b), 4(b), 5(b)), so amplifying rather than reversing that dependence is consistent with the reported results. *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** It breaks the clearing identity that made (10) exact — the original volume follows from matching $bn(1+X)/2$ buy lots against $bn(1-X)/2 + bnX$ sell lots, an exact accounting identity. Once $b_j$ depends on $|X_j|$, the fundamentalists' absorbed quantity in (7) becomes $-b_jnX_j$, so $\gamma_j = b_jn/(am) = \gamma_0 e^{\lambda|X_j|}$ becomes state-dependent and the return equation (9) is no longer linear in $\Delta X$: an extra term $\gamma_0\left(e^{\lambda|X_j|} - e^{\lambda|X_{j-1}|}\right)X_{j-1}$ appears. The model gains realism in volume at the cost of a closed-form return. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Calibration is unanchored — $\lambda$ must be fitted to a volume distribution whose exponent the paper never reports (no $\beta$, no fitting range, no goodness-of-fit anywhere in the empirical section), so the target has to be re-estimated from scratch before the extension can be validated. *(From paper)*

- **Proper tail-estimation replication of the empirical claim** — Redo §4.4 with modern tail methodology instead of visual semi-log inspection: estimate $\beta$ in $P(R>x)\sim x^{-\beta}$ by the Hill estimator with a data-driven threshold, report confidence intervals, and run formal likelihood-ratio comparisons of power law against lognormal, stretched-exponential and exponential alternatives. Extend from one stock to a full cross-section of TSE names and multiple sub-periods (pre-bubble, bubble, post-burst).
  - **Strong:** The protocol is cheap and completely specified — two series, one log-difference, one histogram (§4.4 steps 1–4); nothing in the paper's empirical procedure requires proprietary code or unavailable data beyond a standard daily TSE panel. *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** The paper hands the replication a sharp, pre-registered hypothesis: strong conformity should coincide with power-law tails and weak conformity with Gaussian ones, so splitting the 1975–2002 window at the 1989 peak yields a genuine out-of-sample directional prediction — bubble sub-period $\hat\beta$ should be *smaller* (fatter) than a quiet sub-period's. *(From paper + AI)*
  - **Weak:** The original evidence base cannot support the claim as stated — the sample is **one stock**, and the paper reports no exponent, no standard error, no fitting range and no test statistic; the tail classification rests entirely on footnote 2's straight-line-on-semi-log ruler applied by eye. With $\approx 7{,}000$ daily observations, the expected number of returns beyond $20\%$ under a $\beta=3$ tail anchored at $P(R>2\%)=0.01$ is $7{,}000\times10^{-5} = 0.07$ — the discriminating region is empty (see §4.4's worked example), so exponential and power-law fits are numerically indistinguishable on this dataset. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Volatility clustering contaminates unconditional tail estimates — the paper itself describes the returns as having clustered volatility, which means the sample is a mixture of Gaussians with time-varying variance. Such a mixture has fat unconditional tails *by construction* ($\mathrm{Var}(R) = E[\sigma^{2}]$ but $E[R^{4}] = 3E[\sigma^{4}] \ge 3\left(E[\sigma^{2}]\right)^{2}$ by Jensen, so excess kurtosis is positive whenever $\sigma$ varies at all), so observing fat tails is not evidence for a power law specifically, and the Hill estimator is badly biased under dependence. Standardising by a conditional-volatility estimate before tail fitting is mandatory. *(From AI)*

- **Volume–return joint modelling, the paper's own declared open problem** — The concluding remarks name it: "the question, which has been touched in introduction but not explored, is analysis of the relationships between returns and trading volume". Attack it directly using §4.2 steps 5–6, which already deliver both quantities from a single latent state: $R_j \approx \gamma\Delta X_j$ (a *change*) and $V_j = bn(1+|X_j|)/2$ (a *level*). Derive and then test the implied joint law — for instance the correlation between $|R_j|$ and $V_j$, and between $|R_j|$ and $\Delta V_j$.
  - **Strong:** The paper explicitly flags this as the unexplored question and provides the two equations needed, so the extension is additive rather than revisionary — no part of §4.1 or §4.2 steps 1–4 has to change. *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** The model makes a sharp, falsifiable prediction that distinguishes it from information-arrival models. Since $V_j$ depends on $|X_j|$ and $R_j$ on $\Delta X_j$, the model implies $\Delta V_j = bn\,\mathrm{sgn}(X_j)\Delta X_j/2 = \frac{bn}{2\gamma}\mathrm{sgn}(X_j)R_j$ (ignoring news), i.e. **$\Delta V_j$ is perfectly correlated with $R_j$ in bull states and perfectly anti-correlated in bear states**, while the level correlation $\mathrm{corr}(|R_j|, V_j)$ is near zero because a bounded process' increment is nearly uncorrelated with its level. Both are directly testable on daily data. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** The prediction is contradicted by the model's own volume-range defect — because $V_j\in[bn/2, bn]$ while empirical volume varies over decades, any fitted $\mathrm{corr}(\Delta V, R)$ on real data is dominated by the volume dynamics the model does not generate, so the test as stated would reject the model for the wrong reason. The multiplicative rebuild above is a prerequisite for a fair test. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** At the parameters the paper uses, the internal noise is nearly absent and the joint law is close to deterministic — with $n = 100{,}000$ the diffusion amplitude is $\sqrt{Q}\le\sqrt{2/n} = 0.00447$, and in the §4.1 worked example the drift contributed $0.018$ against the diffusion's $0.00068$, a ratio of **26:1**. Both $R_j$ and $V_j$ are then essentially deterministic functions of the single random draw $\kappa_j$, so the model's "joint distribution" is a one-dimensional curve embedded in the $(R,V)$ plane and cannot match a genuinely two-dimensional empirical scatter. *(From AI)*
