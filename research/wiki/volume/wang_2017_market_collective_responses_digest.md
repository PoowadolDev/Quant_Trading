## 2. Summary

Notation used throughout this digest, matching the paper: $N=96$ = number of stocks; $i,j=1,\dots,N$ = stock indices; $n=1,\dots,N$ = latent-factor index; $t_j$ = event (trade) time of stock $j$; $m_i$ = midpoint price of stock $i$; $s_i$ = bid–ask spread of stock $i$ (the paper's liquidity proxy); $\varepsilon_j\in\{+1,-1\}$ = trade sign of stock $j$ (+1 buy, −1 sell); $v_j$ = traded volume; $\nu_j=\varepsilon_j v_j$ = signed traded volume; a tilde ($\tilde z$) always means the *standardized* version of a quantity; $R_x$ = the $N\times N$ response matrix for observable $x\in\{m,s\}$; $U_x,V_x,S_x$ = the SVD factors of $R_x$; $C_{xy}$ = overlap matrix of factors; $\mu,\sigma,\beta$ = location, scale and shape of the fitted $t$ location-scale distribution.

1. **Motivation and gap.** The paper starts from **market impact** (also called price impact) — the price change caused by a single trade. It reviews the microstructure chain that produces it: prices come out of a **continuous double auction** of market and limit orders; a market order eats the volume resting at the best quote, and if that volume does not suffice it "goes deeper into the order book", consuming the second, third, fourth best quotes; the best quote therefore moves because incoming limit orders cannot instantly replenish the old best level. The resulting cost is the **liquidity cost**, usually measured by the **bid–ask spread**, so market impact and liquidity are two views of the same event. The literature is then split into two strands: **self-impact** on single stocks (where the well-documented **long-memory correlation in order flow** is attributed to **order splitting** rather than herding) and **cross-impact**, i.e. the response of stock $i$'s price to a market order in stock $j$. Large-scale cross-impact studies found **non-Markovian** features, from which the authors conclude the **Efficient Market Hypothesis cannot hold in a strict form** — efficiency is violated on short time scales and only recovered on longer ones, with consequences such as information asymmetry, latent arbitrage, and partial compensation through the spread. The stated gap: liquidity is *supplied* by limit orders and *taken* by market orders, so measured liquidity is the outcome of a complex dynamical interplay, and nobody has characterized **what role liquidity plays in price changes across the whole market**. The paper's answer is to build response functions for both prices and liquidity, extend them from trade signs to volumes, and factorize the resulting matrices.

2. **The generalized response function — the paper's central definition.** Conventional response functions relate log-midpoint-price differences to trade signs only. The authors generalize to any observable and any trade attribute:
   $$R_{x,ij}=\left\langle\left(\tilde x_i^{(f)}(t_j)-\tilde x_i^{(p)}(t_j)\right)\tilde y_j(t_j)\right\rangle_{t_j} \tag{1}$$
   where $i,j$ run over all $N$ stocks; $\tilde x_i$ is the standardized observable of stock $i$ — either the **midpoint price** $\tilde m_i$ (giving the **price response** $R_{m,ij}$) or the **bid–ask spread** $\tilde s_i$ (giving the **liquidity response** $R_{s,ij}$); the superscripts $(p)$ and $(f)$ mark the quote **prior to** and **following** the trade of stock $j$ at event time $t_j$; $\tilde y_j$ is the standardized trade attribute — **trade sign** $\tilde\varepsilon_j$, **traded volume** $\tilde v_j$, or **signed traded volume** $\tilde\nu_j=\tilde\varepsilon_j\tilde v_j$; and $\langle\cdot\rangle_{t_j}$ is the average over all event times of stock $j$. Because only the *previous* and *following* quotes enter, (1) measures the **primary** impact — the immediate change, with **no time lag** and no accounting for subsequent trades.
   *Worked example (running example "W", reused in point 5 and in §4.1).* Take stock $j$ with four trades in the window: raw volumes $v_j=(100,300,200,400)$ shares and signs $\varepsilon_j=(+1,-1,+1,-1)$. Suppose stock $i$'s midpoint series over the window has standard deviation $\sigma(m_i)=\$0.05$, and the raw midpoint changes bracketing those four trades are $\Delta m_i=(+0.01,-0.01,+0.02,0.00)$ dollars. Since the means cancel in a difference, $\Delta\tilde m_i=\Delta m_i/\sigma(m_i)=(0.2,-0.2,0.4,0.0)$. For the sign channel, $\langle\varepsilon\rangle=0$ and $\sigma(\varepsilon)=1$ so $\tilde\varepsilon=\varepsilon$, and the products are $(0.2,\,0.2,\,0.4,\,0.0)$, giving $R_{m,ij}|_\varepsilon=0.8/4=\mathbf{0.200}$. For the volume channel, $\langle v\rangle=250$ and $\sigma(v)=\sqrt{50000/4}=111.803$, so $\tilde v=(-1.34164,\,0.44721,\,-0.44721,\,1.34164)$ and $R_{m,ij}|_v=(-0.26833-0.08944-0.17888+0)/4=\mathbf{-0.134}$. For signed volume, $\nu=(100,-300,200,-400)$, $\langle\nu\rangle=-100$, $\sigma(\nu)=\sqrt{65000}=254.951$, so $\tilde\nu=(0.78446,-0.78446,1.17669,-1.17669)$ and $R_{m,ij}|_\nu=(0.15689+0.15689+0.47068+0)/4=\mathbf{0.196}$. The sign and signed-volume channels nearly agree (0.200 vs 0.196) while the unsigned-volume channel differs in both size and sign — a miniature of the paper's headline result.

3. **The standardization step, and why it is not cosmetic.** Every quantity entering (1) is z-scored over its own time series:
   $$\tilde z=\frac{z-\langle z\rangle}{\sigma(z)} \tag{2}$$
   with $\langle z\rangle$ the mean and $\sigma(z)$ the standard deviation of that series. The paper's justification is that this "puts all quantities on equal footing" — necessary because a midpoint price in dollars, a spread in cents and a volume in shares cannot otherwise be multiplied together meaningfully. A side effect worth naming: after (2), $R_{x,ij}$ is a **dimensionless correlation-like number**, not a price in currency units, so the matrix says *how reliably* a trade moves a quote, not *by how much*.
   *Worked example.* Let stock $i$'s spread series be $s_i=(0.01,0.02,0.02,0.03)$ dollars. Then $\langle s\rangle=0.02$, deviations $(-0.01,0,0,+0.01)$, $\sigma(s)=\sqrt{(10^{-4}+0+0+10^{-4})/4}=\sqrt{5\times10^{-5}}=0.0070711$, so $\tilde s=(\mathbf{-1.4142},\,0,\,0,\,\mathbf{+1.4142})$ — a one-cent spread widening becomes $+1.414$ standardized units for this stock, and $\sum_k\tilde s_k=0$, $\frac{1}{4}\sum_k\tilde s_k^2=1$ as required.

4. **Data set and the single/multiple-trade split.** The empirics use the **NASDAQ TotalView-ITCH** message data at **one-millisecond resolution** for **96 stocks** of the **NASDAQ 100** index (listed with per-stock trade counts in appendix A), over **five trading days, 7–11 March 2016**, restricted to the intraday window **09:40–15:50 EST** to avoid open/close effects. The order book (best bid, best ask, trade time, buy/sell type) is **reconstructed from the raw order flow**, following the companion paper (reference [22]). For every trade of a stock, the previous and following quotes of every *other* stock are located on a common **event-time axis**, so a quote change triggered by a trade in a different stock becomes visible. Crucially, the authors find that **35 % of trades share their previous and following quotes with other trades** (the **case of multiple trades** — more than one trade was needed to move the other stock's quote) while the remaining **65 % do not** (the **case of single trades**). Summing appendix A's averaged daily trade counts gives **336,343 trades per day across the 96 stocks**, i.e. an average of **3,504 trades per stock per day**, ranging from **FB 14,921** and **AAPL 13,598** at the top down to **VIP 217** and **SIRI 514** at the bottom — a **69-fold spread** in event counts that matters for estimation error (see Section 5).

5. **Four averaging variants and the weighted response.** Equation (1) is evaluated three ways — over all trades ($R_{x,ij}|_{\rm at}$), over single trades only ($R_{x,ij}|_{\rm st}$), and over multiple trades only ($R_{x,ij}|_{\rm mt}$) — plus a fourth, linearly interpolated variant:
   $$R_{x,ij}|_{\rm wt}=w_{ij}R_{x,ij}|_{\rm st}+(1-w_{ij})R_{x,ij}|_{\rm mt} \tag{6}$$
   where $w_{ij}$ is defined as **the ratio of trades identified as single trades to all trades for the stock pair $(i,j)$**. All four variants are carried through the entire analysis and plotted side by side in every figure.
   *Worked example (continuing W).* Split the four trades of W into single trades $\{1,2,3\}$ with products $(0.2,0.2,0.4)$, mean $0.26667$, and multiple trades $\{4\}$ with product $0.0$, mean $0.0$. Then $w_{ij}=3/4=0.75$ and (6) gives $0.75\times0.26667+0.25\times0.0=\mathbf{0.200}$ — **exactly** the all-trades value computed in point 2. This is not a coincidence: with $n_{\rm st}+n_{\rm mt}=n_{\rm at}$ and $w_{ij}=n_{\rm st}/n_{\rm at}$, the arithmetic mean decomposes as $\langle z\rangle_{\rm at}=\frac{n_{\rm st}\langle z\rangle_{\rm st}+n_{\rm mt}\langle z\rangle_{\rm mt}}{n_{\rm at}}=w_{ij}\langle z\rangle_{\rm st}+(1-w_{ij})\langle z\rangle_{\rm mt}$, so **equation (6) is an algebraic identity with $R|_{\rm wt}\equiv R|_{\rm at}$**, not an independent estimator. The paper's own tables corroborate this: for the price response to trade signs the all-trades fit is $(\mu,\sigma,\beta)=(0.00021,0.011,0.980)$ and the weighted fit is $(0.00030,0.011,0.982)$; for the liquidity response to trade signs, $(-0.00136,0.085,6.491)$ versus $(0.00058,0.086,6.781)$. The residual differences are consistent with the standardization (2) being recomputed on each subsample rather than once on the full series.

6. **Singular value decomposition as the factor-extraction engine.** The response matrix $R_x$ is a **non-symmetric $96\times96$ square matrix**, so eigenvalues would be complex and useless; the authors use the SVD instead:
   $$R_x=U_xS_xV_x^{\rm T} \tag{3}$$
   $$S_x={\rm diag}(s_{x1},\dots,s_{xN}) \tag{4}$$
   $$R_x\vec V_{xi}=S_{xi}\vec U_{xi}\quad\text{and}\quad R_x^{\rm T}\vec U_{xi}=S_{xi}\vec V_{xi} \tag{5}$$
   where $S_x$ holds the **real, non-negative singular values**; $U_x$ and $V_x$ are **orthogonal** $N\times N$ matrices whose columns $\vec U_{xi}$ and $\vec V_{xi}$ are the **left** and **right singular vectors** of index $i$. The economic reading the paper attaches: the decomposition exposes **latent factors** that link the price or liquidity change of stock $i$ to the trading information of all other stocks $j$; the vector entries lie in $[-1,+1]$ and are the **weights of the latent factors**. A **large entry in a left singular vector** means that stock's price (or liquidity) depends strongly on that factor; a **large entry in a right singular vector** means that factor is robustly carried by that stock's trades.
   *Worked example.* Take the $2\times2$ response matrix $R=\begin{pmatrix}0.30&0.40\\0.20&0.10\end{pmatrix}$. Then $R^{\rm T}R=\begin{pmatrix}0.13&0.14\\0.14&0.17\end{pmatrix}$ with trace $0.30$ and determinant $0.13\times0.17-0.14^2=0.0025$, so its eigenvalues are $\lambda=\frac{0.30\pm\sqrt{0.09-0.01}}{2}=\frac{0.30\pm0.282843}{2}$, i.e. $\lambda_1=0.291421$, $\lambda_2=0.008579$, giving $s_1=\sqrt{0.291421}=\mathbf{0.53983}$ and $s_2=\sqrt{0.008579}=\mathbf{0.09262}$. Checks: $s_1s_2=0.0500=|\det R|=|0.03-0.08|$ ✓ and $s_1^2+s_2^2=0.300=\|R\|_F^2=0.09+0.16+0.04+0.01$ ✓. The leading right singular vector solves $(0.13-\lambda_1)v_1+0.14v_2=0$, so $v_2=1.15301v_1$ and $\vec V_1=(0.6552,\,0.7555)$; then from (5), $\vec U_1=R\vec V_1/s_1=(0.498742,\,0.206586)/0.53983=(\mathbf{0.9239},\,\mathbf{0.3827})$, and the reverse relation checks out: $R^{\rm T}\vec U_1/s_1=(0.353697,\,0.407819)/0.53983=(0.6552,\,0.7555)=\vec V_1$ ✓. Interpretation: factor 1 explains $s_1^2/(s_1^2+s_2^2)=0.2914/0.30=\mathbf{97.1\,\%}$ of the matrix's squared weight, it drives **stock 1's** price most strongly ($U$-weight 0.92) and it is carried mostly by **stock 2's** order flow ($V$-weight 0.76).

7. **Main empirical result — price responds to *direction*, liquidity responds to *size*.** Rather than the singular *values* (poor statistics with only 96 stocks), the paper studies the empirical **probability densities of the singular-vector entries** pooled over all $N$ factors, with **normal distributions fitted for comparison** (figures 2 and 3). On the **price** side (figure 2, table 1) the verdict is that price change "is not occasional and must be induced by some factors"; for all three trade attributes the **case of multiple trades is close to random**, whereas the all-trades, single-trades and weighted cases are **more informative** (visibly heavier tails than the normal fit). Table 1 quantifies it: price response to **trade signs** gives $\beta=0.980$ ($U$) / $1.028$ ($V$) for all trades but $\beta=2.304$ / $\mathbf{21.156}$ for multiple trades; **signed traded volumes** give $\beta=0.957$ / $1.008$ (all) versus $2.362$ / $12.794$ (multiple); **unsigned traded volumes** sit in between at $\beta=1.984$ / $3.217$ (all), with scales moving in lockstep ($\sigma=0.010$–$0.014$ for the informative cases versus $0.056$–$0.097$ for the multiple-trade cases). On the **liquidity** side (figure 3, table 2), where $\tilde x_i=\tilde s_i$, the **mismatch with the normal fits sits in the heavy tails**, so the link between liquidity change and latent factors "cannot be coincidental", and the decisive row is the middle one: **all cases except multiple trades show remarkable information when the liquidity responds to traded volumes**, implying **liquidity is highly sensitive to traded volumes**. Numerically, liquidity response to **traded volumes** gives $\beta=1.073$ ($U$) / $1.344$ ($V$) with $\sigma=0.024$ / $0.032$ for all trades — the heaviest tails in the whole study — while liquidity response to **trade signs** gives $\beta=6.491$ / $6.033$ ($\sigma=0.085$ / $0.084$) and to **signed volumes** $\beta=7.774$ / $6.549$ ($\sigma=0.088$ / $0.085$), i.e. near-normal; multiple-trade liquidity cases are the most Gaussian of all ($\beta$ up to $\mathbf{27.156}$). The economic reading of the contrast: a **buy trade and a sell trade both widen the spread**, so *signed* aggregation cancels the liquidity effect while *unsigned* aggregation does not — and the mirror image holds for prices, where buys and sells push the midpoint in opposite directions so only signed aggregation survives.

8. **The unified descriptor — the $t$ location-scale distribution.** To quantify all nine (observable × attribute) blocks with one family, the authors fit the non-standardized Student's $t$:
   $$p(x)=\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\beta+1}{2}\right)}{\sigma\sqrt{\beta\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)}\left[\frac{\beta+\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}}{\beta}\right]^{-\left(\frac{\beta+1}{2}\right)} \tag{7}$$
   where $\Gamma(\cdot)$ is the gamma function, $\mu$ the **location**, $\sigma$ the **scale** and $\beta$ the **shape** parameter. The stated logic: **large $\beta$ → the distribution approaches the normal; small $\beta$ → heavier tails**, so a single family can act either as a surrogate for the normal or as a heavy-tailed model, and $\beta$ becomes the *information gauge*. Figures 4 and 5 show the fits describe the empirical densities "perfectly, especially for the heavy tails", and tables 1–2 confirm the section 3.2/3.3 readings numerically.
   *Worked example (using the paper's own fit: price response to trade signs, all trades, left singular vectors, $\mu=0.00021$, $\sigma=0.011$, $\beta=0.980$).* At the mode $x=\mu$ the bracket equals 1, so $p(\mu)=\frac{\Gamma(0.99)}{0.011\sqrt{0.98\pi}\,\Gamma(0.49)}$. Numerically $\Gamma(0.99)=1.00587$, $\Gamma(0.49)=\Gamma(1.49)/0.49=0.88588/0.49=1.80792$, $\sqrt{0.98\pi}=\sqrt{3.07876}=1.75464$, hence $p(\mu)=1.00587/(0.011\times1.75464\times1.80792)=1.00587/0.034897=\mathbf{28.82}$. Five scale units out, at $x-\mu=5\sigma=0.055$: the bracket is $(0.98+25)/0.98=26.5102$ and the exponent is $-0.99$, so the bracket contributes $26.5102^{-0.99}=e^{-0.99\times3.27753}=e^{-3.24475}=0.03897$ and $p=28.82\times0.03897=\mathbf{1.123}$. A normal density of the same scale would give $p_N(\mu)=1/(0.011\sqrt{2\pi})=36.27$ and $p_N(\mu+5\sigma)=36.27\,e^{-12.5}=1.352\times10^{-4}$. The $t$ fit therefore assigns **≈8,300× more density at $5\sigma$** than the normal — this is exactly the "heavy tail" the paper reads as evidence of genuine latent factors. Contrast the near-normal block (price response to trade signs, multiple trades, right vectors: $\sigma=0.097$, $\beta=21.156$): its variance is finite, $\text{sd}=\sigma\sqrt{\beta/(\beta-2)}=0.097\sqrt{21.156/19.156}=0.1019$, and its peak $p(\mu)=\frac{\Gamma(11.078)}{0.097\sqrt{21.156\pi}\,\Gamma(10.578)}=\frac{3.2142}{0.097\times8.15248}=4.06$ versus a matched normal's $1/(0.1019\sqrt{2\pi})=3.91$ — within **4 %**, i.e. indistinguishable from Gaussian.

9. **Overlap matrices — asking which factors move price and liquidity *together*.** Restricting to the informative **single-trades** case, the authors first re-standardize each row of the left singular-vector matrix across factors,
    $$\tilde U_{x,in}=\frac{U_{x,in}-\langle U_{x,in}\rangle_n}{\sigma(U_{x,in})_n} \tag{8}$$
    where mean and standard deviation for each stock $i$ are taken over all $N$ factors, defining the normalized matrix $\tilde U_x$ with $x=m$ for price changes and $x=s$ for liquidity changes. The cross-overlap and the two self-overlaps are then
    $$C_{ms}=\tilde U_m^{\rm T}\tilde U_s \tag{9}$$
    $$C_{mm}=\tilde U_m^{\rm T}\tilde U_m\quad\text{and}\quad C_{ss}=\tilde U_s^{\rm T}\tilde U_s \tag{10}$$
    measuring the overlap of the $N$ price-change factors with the $N$ liquidity-change factors, and of each set with itself. Figure 6 reports the structures: overlaps are **remarkable** in panels (a), (b), (c) and (e) — the **factors of price change are clearly related to trade signs and signed traded volumes** ("the price is easily moved by trade directions"), while the **factors of liquidity change are significantly affected only by traded volumes**. Figure 7 repeats everything for **random response matrices matched to the empirical mean and standard deviation**, and there the small overlaps are randomly scattered — a clear visual contrast. But $C_{ms}$ itself (panels g–i) looks random by eye because large positive and negative overlaps are mixed, which motivates one more decomposition.
    *Worked example.* Take a toy $N=3$ market and apply (8) to the row $U_{m,1\cdot}=(0.40,-0.20,0.10,-0.30)$ (shown with 4 factors for arithmetic clarity): mean $=0$, $\sigma=\sqrt{0.30/4}=0.273861$, so $\tilde U_{m,1\cdot}=(\mathbf{1.4606},\,\mathbf{-0.7303},\,\mathbf{0.3651},\,\mathbf{-1.0954})$, with $\sum_n\tilde U^2=4=N$ as z-scoring guarantees. Now build a $3\times3$ example with $a=\sqrt{3/2}=1.224745$: let $\tilde U_m$ have rows $(a,-a,0),(0,a,-a),(-a,0,a)$ and $\tilde U_s$ have rows $(a,0,-a),(-a,a,0),(0,-a,a)$ — every row sums to 0 with $\sum_n\tilde U^2=3=N$ ✓. Then (10) gives $C_{mm}=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1.5&-1.5\\-1.5&3&-1.5\\-1.5&-1.5&3\end{pmatrix}$ and (9) gives $C_{ms}=\begin{pmatrix}1.5&1.5&-3\\-3&1.5&1.5\\1.5&-3&1.5\end{pmatrix}$. Two structural facts fall straight out and hold for any $N$: the **diagonal of $C_{mm}$ equals $N$** (here 3), because ${\rm tr}\,C_{mm}=\sum_i\sum_n\tilde U_{m,in}^2=N\cdot N$; and **every row and column sum is exactly zero** (here $3-1.5-1.5=0$ and $1.5+1.5-3=0$), because (8) forces each row of $\tilde U$ to sum to zero. Both matter for reading figure 6 — see Section 5.

10. **Decomposing the overlap matrices, and the paper's conclusion.** Each overlap matrix is itself put through an SVD,
    $$C_{xy}=U_{xy}S_{xy}V_{xy}^{\rm T} \tag{11}$$
    where the entries $U_{xy,an}$ and $V_{xy,bn}$ (with $a,b,n=1,\dots,N$) measure the correlation between the $n$-th **common** factor and the $a$-th individual factor of $x$, and between the $n$-th common factor and the $b$-th individual factor of $y$. Figure 8 fits both a normal and a $t$ location-scale distribution to these entries; figure 9 does the same for the random overlap matrices; table 3 lists all parameters. Findings: panels (a), (b), (c) and (e) show heavy tails for $C_{mm}$ and $C_{ss}$, matching the large overlaps of figure 6, and — the key point — the **irregular $C_{ms}$ patterns are verified to be non-random**. Table 3 quantifies it: for the price–liquidity cross-overlap $C_{ms}$, the factors related to **traded volumes** give $\beta=5.655$ ($U$) / $3.421$ ($V$) with $\sigma=0.083$ / $0.070$, whereas the factors related to **trade signs** give $\beta=1.784$ / $\mathbf{13.893}$ ($\sigma=0.041$ / $0.094$) and to **signed volumes** $\beta=1.630$ / $\mathbf{22.677}$ ($\sigma=0.038$ / $0.097$) — the right-vector side is essentially Gaussian for the signed channels. The authors read this as: **factors related to traded volumes significantly interconnect price change with liquidity change**, i.e. "the traded volume plays an important role when the price is changed by the liquidity", which is mechanically plausible because a stock's own trades consume the volume at the best quote and shift the price immediately. Conversely the **signed** channels produce weak interconnections, because liquidity changes are **almost cancelled out by opposite trade directions** — the spread widens for both buys and sells. The concluding caveat is important: those weak responses **cannot be ignored**, because if the market were efficient the liquidity responses to trade signs and signed volumes should be exactly counterbalanced; that they are not implies a residual **imbalance of liquidity, which is itself related to the price change**. Stated limitations of the study, explicit or structural: only the **primary** (zero-lag) responses are measured, so no impact-decay or propagator is estimated; only **five trading days** of one index are used; the singular *values* are deliberately not analyzed because of weak statistics at $N=96$; and no goodness-of-fit statistic, confidence interval or model-selection criterion is reported for any of the $t$ location-scale fits.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### Generalizing the response function beyond trade signs

Equation (1) replaces the usual "log-return versus trade sign" correlator with a template in which the *observable* can be the midpoint price or the bid–ask spread, and the *trade attribute* can be the trade sign, the unsigned traded volume, or the signed traded volume. That produces six response matrices per averaging case, and 24 in total across the four averaging variants. The trick that makes the six comparable is the z-scoring (2), which strips units from every series before multiplication.

**Why it matters:** It converts a single scalar stylized fact into a **2×3 design matrix**, and the paper's whole conclusion lives in the *contrast* between cells rather than in any one cell. Price responds to signed information, liquidity responds to unsigned information — a statement that is **invisible** if you only ever regress returns on trade signs, which is what the prior literature does. For anyone reusing this, the practical lesson is that **the spread and the price are driven by different features of the same trade**, so a liquidity forecaster and a price forecaster should not share features.

### The single- versus multiple-trade classification

Because the analysis lives on an event-time axis shared across stocks, several trades can fall between two consecutive quote updates of another stock. The authors split trades accordingly: **35 %** share their bracketing quotes with other trades (multiple), **65 %** do not (single). Every result is reported separately for the two subsets, and the multiple-trade subset turns out to be **consistently the most Gaussian** — $\beta$ up to 21.2 (price/signs) and 27.2 (liquidity/signs) versus $\beta\approx1$ for the informative cases.

**Why it matters:** It is a clean, mechanical **signal-versus-noise separator** that requires no model. If a quote change cannot be attributed to one identifiable trade, attributing it to any single trade injects **attribution noise**, and the SVD of such a matrix produces near-Haar-random singular vectors — exactly what the large $\beta$ values report. The consequence is **actionable**: any cross-impact estimator built from tick data should discard or down-weight the multiple-trade population, and the paper shows the discard costs only 35 % of the sample while removing the bulk of the randomness.

### Singular value decomposition as market-wide factor extraction

The response matrix is $96\times96$ and **non-symmetric** — stock $i$'s reaction to $j$'s trades is not stock $j$'s reaction to $i$'s — so an eigendecomposition is inappropriate. The SVD (3)–(5) yields two distinct vector sets with distinct meanings: the **left** vectors $\vec U_{xi}$ couple *price/liquidity changes* to factors, and the **right** vectors $\vec V_{xi}$ couple *factors* to *trading information*. The paper deliberately studies the **entries' distributions** rather than the singular values, arguing that with $N=96$ the value spectrum has poor statistics while the $N^2=9{,}216$ vector entries do not.

**Why it matters:** This is the **methodological hinge** of the paper, and it is what lets a single $96\times96$ object be summarized by three numbers per block. It also matters because the asymmetry is **informational**: a factor with a large right-vector weight on one stock but spread-out left-vector weights identifies a stock whose order flow **leads the market**, which is precisely the object an execution desk or a cross-impact arbitrage strategy wants. The choice to look at entry distributions rather than singular values is the paper's main trade-off — it buys statistical robustness at the price of never quantifying **how much variance each factor explains**.

### The $t$ location-scale distribution and $\beta$ as an information gauge

Equation (7) is used as a one-parameter dial between Gaussian ($\beta\to\infty$) and heavy-tailed ($\beta$ small) behaviour, so the fitted **shape parameter $\beta$ becomes a scalar summary of how localized the latent factors are**. The paper's entire empirical narrative can be read off tables 1–3 through $\beta$ alone: $\beta\approx1$ means informative/localized, $\beta\gtrsim7$ means indistinguishable from random.

**Why it matters:** It gives a **single comparable number** across nine heterogeneous blocks, which is what makes the price-versus-liquidity contrast legible. It is also the part most directly reusable as a **monitoring statistic**: fit $\beta$ on a rolling window and you have a scalar that says whether the market's response structure is currently concentrated in a few names or diffused across all of them. The caution is equally important — $\beta$ is reported with **no standard error and no fit diagnostic**, so differences like 6.491 versus 6.781 must not be over-read.

### Overlap matrices: separating individually-acting from jointly-acting factors

Equations (8)–(10) build three matrices: $C_{mm}$ (do price factors overlap with each other?), $C_{ss}$ (same for liquidity), and $C_{ms}$ (do price factors overlap with liquidity factors?). The paper shows $C_{mm}$ and $C_{ss}$ have visible structure versus a random benchmark, but $C_{ms}$ looks random by eye because positive and negative overlaps mix — so it applies the SVD a *second* time, via (11), and the resulting entry distributions show $C_{ms}$ is in fact **non-random**, with the strongest cross-linkage carried by **unsigned traded volume**.

**Why it matters:** This is the step that turns two separate stories (price factors, liquidity factors) into one **causal-flavoured claim**: the channel through which liquidity moves price is **volume**, not direction. It also demonstrates a transferable technique — when a correlation-of-correlations matrix looks like noise because of sign cancellation, **decompose it instead of eyeballing it**, because sign-mixed structure is invisible to a heatmap but obvious in the singular-vector entry distribution. The residual finding — that the signed-channel liquidity responses are weak but non-zero — is the paper's most interesting loose end, since exact efficiency would require them to vanish.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

### 4.1 Building the generalized response matrices

**Goal:** Turn raw exchange message data into six $96\times96$ matrices (price and liquidity, each against trade signs, traded volumes and signed traded volumes) whose entry $(i,j)$ says how reliably a trade in stock $j$ immediately moves stock $i$'s midpoint or spread.

**Inputs:** NASDAQ TotalView-ITCH order-flow messages at 1 ms resolution for $N=96$ stocks over five trading days, 09:40–15:50 EST. **Outputs:** the matrices $R_{m,ij}$ and $R_{s,ij}$ for each of the three trade attributes, in each of four averaging cases (all / single / multiple / weighted trades) — 24 matrices in total.

**Steps:**

1. **Reconstruct the order book** — Replay the order-flow messages to rebuild, at every millisecond, the best bid and best ask of every stock, plus the trade record (time, direction, size). This yields the two observables the paper needs: the midpoint $m_i=\frac{1}{2}(\text{bid}_i+\text{ask}_i)$ and the spread $s_i=\text{ask}_i-\text{bid}_i$, the latter serving as the liquidity proxy.
2. **Build the event-time axis** — Instead of clock time, index everything by **trades**. For each trade of stock $j$ at time $t_j$, locate stock $i$'s **previous** quote ($x_i^{(p)}$) and **following** quote ($x_i^{(f)}$). This is what makes a quote change in a *different* stock attributable to this trade.
3. **Classify each trade as single or multiple** — If a trade shares its bracketing pair of quotes with at least one other trade, label it **multiple** (35 % of trades on average); otherwise **single** (65 %). The multiple label is a warning that more than one trade was needed to move the other stock's quote, so attribution to any one of them is ambiguous.
4. **Standardize every series** — Apply $\tilde z=\frac{z-\langle z\rangle}{\sigma(z)}$ (eq. 2) to $m_i$, $s_i$, $\varepsilon_j$, $v_j$ and $\nu_j=\varepsilon_jv_j$ separately per stock. Because the response uses a *difference* of the observable, the mean cancels and only the division by $\sigma(x_i)$ survives: $\tilde x_i^{(f)}-\tilde x_i^{(p)}=\frac{x_i^{(f)}-x_i^{(p)}}{\sigma(x_i)}$.
5. **Compute the response** — For each ordered pair $(i,j)$ and each attribute $y$, average the product of the standardized quote change of $i$ with the standardized attribute of $j$ over all event times of $j$: $R_{x,ij}=\left\langle\left(\tilde x_i^{(f)}(t_j)-\tilde x_i^{(p)}(t_j)\right)\tilde y_j(t_j)\right\rangle_{t_j}$ (eq. 1). Because only the immediately bracketing quotes enter, this is the **primary** response — no lag, no propagator.
6. **Repeat for the four averaging cases** — Compute the same average over all trades, over single trades only, over multiple trades only, and finally interpolate with $R_{x,ij}|_{\rm wt}=w_{ij}R_{x,ij}|_{\rm st}+(1-w_{ij})R_{x,ij}|_{\rm mt}$ (eq. 6), with $w_{ij}$ the single-trade fraction for that pair.

**Worked example (end-to-end, running example W):** consider the pair $(i,j)$ with four trades of $j$ in the window.
Step 1–2 give the bracketing midpoints of stock $i$: $(m^{(p)},m^{(f)})=(20.00,20.01),(20.02,20.01),(20.00,20.02),(20.03,20.03)$ dollars, so raw changes $\Delta m_i=(+0.01,-0.01,+0.02,0.00)$; the trades of $j$ carry $v_j=(100,300,200,400)$ and $\varepsilon_j=(+1,-1,+1,-1)$.
Step 3 labels trades 1–3 single and trade 4 multiple, so $w_{ij}=3/4=0.75$.
Step 4: with $\sigma(m_i)=\$0.05$, $\Delta\tilde m_i=(0.2,-0.2,0.4,0.0)$; $\tilde\varepsilon=(+1,-1,+1,-1)$; $\tilde v=(-1.34164,0.44721,-0.44721,1.34164)$ from $\langle v\rangle=250,\ \sigma(v)=111.803$; $\tilde\nu=(0.78446,-0.78446,1.17669,-1.17669)$ from $\langle\nu\rangle=-100,\ \sigma(\nu)=254.951$.
Step 5: $R_{m,ij}|_\varepsilon=\frac{0.2+0.2+0.4+0.0}{4}=0.200$; $R_{m,ij}|_v=\frac{-0.26833-0.08944-0.17888+0}{4}=-0.134$; $R_{m,ij}|_\nu=\frac{0.15689+0.15689+0.47068+0}{4}=0.196$.
Step 6: single-trade sign response $=\frac{0.2+0.2+0.4}{3}=0.26667$; multiple-trade sign response $=0.0$; weighted $=0.75\times0.26667+0.25\times0=0.200$ — identical to the all-trades value, as the identity in Section 2 point 5 predicts.
Now do the liquidity channel on the same trades. Let the bracketing spreads of $i$ be $(s^{(p)},s^{(f)})=(0.01,0.02),(0.01,0.02),(0.02,0.03),(0.02,0.02)$, so $\Delta s_i=(+0.01,+0.01,+0.01,0.00)$ — **positive for both buys and sells**, which is the mechanism the paper stresses. With $\sigma(s_i)=0.0070711$, $\Delta\tilde s_i=(1.41421,1.41421,1.41421,0)$. Then $R_{s,ij}|_\varepsilon=\frac{1.41421-1.41421+1.41421+0}{4}=\mathbf{0.354}$, but $R_{s,ij}|_v=\frac{1.41421\times(-1.34164+0.44721-0.44721)+0}{4}=\frac{1.41421\times(-1.34164)}{4}=\mathbf{-0.474}$ — the unsigned-volume channel produces a response **34 % larger in magnitude** than the sign channel, whereas for the price it was the other way around. Scaled up to 96 stocks, this asymmetry is what produces $\beta\approx1$ for liquidity-versus-volume and $\beta\approx6.5$ for liquidity-versus-sign in table 2.

### 4.2 Factorizing the response matrices by SVD and reading the singular-vector statistics

**Goal:** Extract, from a non-symmetric $96\times96$ response matrix, the latent factors that link many stocks' quote changes to many stocks' order flow, and summarize the whole factor structure by a distribution rather than by 9,216 individual numbers.

**Inputs:** one response matrix $R_x$ ($96\times96$, real, non-symmetric). **Outputs:** singular values $s_{x1},\dots,s_{xN}$; left and right singular vectors $\vec U_{xi},\vec V_{xi}$; and the pooled empirical probability density of all $N^2$ entries of $U_x$ and of $V_x$.

**Steps:**

1. **Decompose** — Compute $R_x=U_xS_xV_x^{\rm T}$ (eq. 3) with $S_x={\rm diag}(s_{x1},\dots,s_{xN})$ (eq. 4). In practice this means diagonalizing the symmetric positive semi-definite $R_x^{\rm T}R_x$ to get $V_x$ and the squared singular values, then recovering $U_x$ from $R_x\vec V_{xi}=s_{xi}\vec U_{xi}$ (eq. 5).
2. **Assign meaning to the two vector families** — $\vec U_{xi}$ (left) lives in the space of *responding* stocks, so a large entry $U_{x,in}$ means stock $i$'s price/liquidity is strongly driven by factor $n$. $\vec V_{xi}$ (right) lives in the space of *trading* stocks, so a large entry $V_{x,jn}$ means factor $n$ is robustly carried by stock $j$'s trades. Every entry lies in $[-1,+1]$ because the vectors are unit-norm.
3. **Skip the singular values** — With only 96 of them, the paper judges the value spectrum too poorly sampled to interpret, and works with the $96\times96=9{,}216$ entries of each vector matrix instead.
4. **Pool and histogram the entries** — Build the empirical density of $\{U_{x,in}\}$ over all $i$ and all factors $n$, and separately of $\{V_{x,jn}\}$. Do this for each of the four averaging cases so they can be overlaid.
5. **Compare against a Gaussian null first** — Fit a normal distribution to each pooled density (figures 2 and 3). Agreement means "the factor weights are spread out like random noise"; disagreement **in the tails** means a few stocks carry disproportionate weight, i.e. real structure.
6. **Then fit the $t$ location-scale family** — Replace the Gaussian by eq. (7) and read the shape parameter $\beta$ as the degree of tail heaviness (figures 4 and 5, tables 1 and 2). Small $\beta$ = localized, informative factors; large $\beta$ = delocalized, random-looking factors.

**Worked example (end-to-end, using the $2\times2$ toy of Section 2 point 6, then the paper's own numbers):**
Step 1: for $R=\begin{pmatrix}0.30&0.40\\0.20&0.10\end{pmatrix}$, $R^{\rm T}R=\begin{pmatrix}0.13&0.14\\0.14&0.17\end{pmatrix}$; eigenvalues $0.291421$ and $0.008579$; $S={\rm diag}(0.53983,\,0.09262)$.
Step 1 (continued): $\vec V_1=(0.6552,0.7555)$, $\vec V_2=(-0.7555,0.6552)$; $\vec U_1=R\vec V_1/s_1=(0.9239,0.3827)$, and $\vec U_2=R\vec V_2/s_2=(-0.30\times0.7555+0.40\times0.6552,\,-0.20\times0.7555+0.10\times0.6552)/0.09262=(0.036540,\,-0.085580)/0.09262=(0.3945,-0.9240)$ — orthogonal to $\vec U_1$ to rounding ($0.9239\times0.3945+0.3827\times(-0.9240)=0.3645-0.3536\approx0$).
Step 2: factor 1 drives stock 1's price (weight 0.92) and is carried by stock 2's flow (weight 0.76).
Step 3–4: pooling the four entries of $U$ gives $\{0.9239,0.3827,0.3945,-0.9240\}$; note $\sum U^2=0.8536+0.1465+0.1556+0.8538=2.0095\approx N=2$, because each column is a unit vector — so the **mean square entry is exactly $1/N$** and the pooled RMS is exactly $1/\sqrt N$.
Step 5–6 with the paper's real data: at $N=96$ the pooled RMS is pinned at $1/\sqrt{96}=0.10206$. Check this against table 2's finite-variance fits, where the $t$ location-scale standard deviation is $\sigma\sqrt{\beta/(\beta-2)}$ for $\beta>2$: liquidity/trade signs/all trades, $U$: $0.085\sqrt{6.491/4.491}=0.1022$; same block, $V$: $0.084\sqrt{6.033/4.033}=0.1027$; liquidity/trade signs/single, $U$: $0.089\sqrt{8.722/6.722}=0.1014$; liquidity/trade signs/multiple, $U$: $0.098\sqrt{27.156/25.156}=0.1018$; liquidity/signed volumes/all, $U$: $0.088\sqrt{7.774/5.774}=0.1021$; price/trade signs/multiple, $V$: $0.097\sqrt{21.156/19.156}=0.1019$. **All six land within 1 % of 0.10206** — the unit-norm constraint is doing exactly what the algebra says it must. The informative blocks instead have $\beta<2$ (price/signs/all: $\beta=0.980$; liquidity/volumes/all: $\beta=1.073$), where the model variance **diverges** and the fit expresses the same fixed second moment as "tiny scale plus fat tail". Quantifying that localization: treating $\beta=0.980$ as approximately Cauchy with scale $0.011$, $P(|x-\mu|>0.5)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan(0.011/0.5)=0.0140$, i.e. $0.0140\times9216=129$ entries above 0.5 in magnitude, which is $129/96=\mathbf{1.34}$ dominant entries per singular vector — the signature of factors concentrated on essentially **one stock each**.

### 4.3 Fitting the $t$ location-scale distribution

**Goal:** Describe nine heterogeneous singular-vector entry distributions with one three-parameter family so they can be compared on a common scale, and get a scalar ($\beta$) that measures how far each is from Gaussian.

**Inputs:** the pooled set of $N^2=9{,}216$ singular-vector entries for one block (observable × attribute × averaging case × left/right). **Outputs:** fitted location $\mu$, scale $\sigma$ and shape $\beta$, plus the fitted curve overlaid on the empirical density.

**Steps:**

1. **Choose the family for a reason** — Use eq. (7). It contains the normal as the $\beta\to\infty$ limit and Cauchy-like tails at $\beta\to1$, so a single family can serve both as a Gaussian surrogate (for the random-looking blocks) and as a heavy-tailed model (for the informative ones). No separate model-selection step is then needed to compare blocks.
2. **Estimate the three parameters** — Fit $\mu$, $\sigma$, $\beta$ to the empirical density (the paper reports point estimates only; no estimator, standard errors or fit statistic are specified).
3. **Read $\mu$ as a diagnostic, not a result** — Singular vectors are defined only up to a simultaneous sign flip ($\vec U\to-\vec U$, $\vec V\to-\vec V$ leaves $R$ unchanged), so the pooled distribution has no intrinsic sign convention. A fitted $|\mu|$ of order $10^{-4}$–$10^{-3}$, three orders below $\sigma$, is therefore the expected outcome and carries no information.
4. **Read $\sigma$ together with $\beta$, never alone** — For $\beta>2$ the variance is $\sigma^2\beta/(\beta-2)$, and the unit-norm constraint fixes the pooled second moment at $1/N$; so $\sigma$ and $\beta$ trade off against each other and only their combination is free.
5. **Interpret** — Small $\beta$ with small $\sigma$ = a few large weights, i.e. factors localized on few stocks, i.e. "informative". Large $\beta$ with $\sigma\approx1/\sqrt N$ = weights spread evenly, i.e. indistinguishable from a random matrix.
6. **Cross-check against a matched random benchmark** — Generate random response matrices with the same mean and standard deviation as the empirical ones, run them through the identical pipeline, and compare (figures 7 and 9).

**Worked example (paper's fitted parameters, price response to trade signs, all trades, left vectors: $\mu=0.00021$, $\sigma=0.011$, $\beta=0.980$):**
Step 2 output is the triple above. Step 3: $|\mu|/\sigma=0.00021/0.011=0.019$, i.e. the location is 1.9 % of one scale unit — negligible, as predicted.
Step 4–5: evaluate eq. (7) at the mode. $\frac{\beta+1}{2}=0.99$ and $\frac{\beta}{2}=0.49$, so the prefactor is $\frac{\Gamma(0.99)}{\sigma\sqrt{\beta\pi}\Gamma(0.49)}$. With $\Gamma(0.99)=1.00587$, $\Gamma(0.49)=1.80792$, $\sqrt{0.98\pi}=1.75464$: denominator $=0.011\times1.75464\times1.80792=0.034897$, so $p(\mu)=1.00587/0.034897=\mathbf{28.82}$.
At one scale unit out ($x-\mu=0.011$): bracket $=(0.98+1)/0.98=2.020408$; $\ln=0.703299$; $\times(-0.99)=-0.696266$; $e^{-0.696266}=0.498430$; $p=28.82\times0.49843=\mathbf{14.37}$ — density halves within one scale unit.
At five scale units ($x-\mu=0.055$): bracket $=26.5102$; $26.5102^{-0.99}=0.03897$; $p=\mathbf{1.123}$ — still order 1.
Step 6 comparison: the matched normal ($\sigma=0.011$) gives $p_N(\mu)=36.27$, $p_N(\mu+\sigma)=36.27\,e^{-0.5}=22.00$, and $p_N(\mu+5\sigma)=36.27\,e^{-12.5}=1.352\times10^{-4}$. So the fitted $t$ is **20 % lower at the peak**, **35 % lower at $1\sigma$**, and **8,300× higher at $5\sigma$** — mass moved out of the shoulders and into the tails. Repeating for the near-Gaussian block (price/signs/multiple/right vectors, $\sigma=0.097$, $\beta=21.156$): $p(\mu)=\frac{\Gamma(11.078)}{0.097\sqrt{21.156\pi}\,\Gamma(10.578)}$; using $\Gamma(z+\tfrac12)/\Gamma(z)\approx\sqrt z\,(1-\tfrac{1}{8z})$ with $z=10.578$ gives $3.2142$, and $\sqrt{21.156\pi}=8.15248$, so $p(\mu)=3.2142/0.790791=\mathbf{4.06}$ versus the matched normal's $1/(0.1019\sqrt{2\pi})=\mathbf{3.91}$ — a **4 %** gap, confirming this block is effectively Gaussian and hence uninformative.

### 4.4 Overlap matrices of factors and their second decomposition

**Goal:** Determine whether the latent factors that move prices are the *same* factors that move liquidity, and if so, which trade attribute carries the shared factors.

**Inputs:** the left singular-vector matrices $U_m$ and $U_s$ from §4.2, computed in the **single-trades** case only. **Outputs:** the overlap matrices $C_{mm}$, $C_{ss}$, $C_{ms}$; their heatmaps against a random benchmark; and the fitted $(\mu,\sigma,\beta)$ of the singular-vector entries of each.

**Steps:**

1. **Restrict to single trades** — The multiple-trade population was shown to be near-random in §4.2, so it is dropped: "we focus on the case of single trades, which do not share their previous and following quotes with other trades".
2. **Re-standardize each stock's factor loadings** — Apply $\tilde U_{x,in}=\frac{U_{x,in}-\langle U_{x,in}\rangle_n}{\sigma(U_{x,in})_n}$ (eq. 8), where the mean and standard deviation for each **stock $i$** are taken over all $N$ **factors**. This removes each stock's overall loading level and scale, so the comparison is about *pattern* rather than *magnitude*.
3. **Form the three overlap matrices** — $C_{ms}=\tilde U_m^{\rm T}\tilde U_s$ (eq. 9), $C_{mm}=\tilde U_m^{\rm T}\tilde U_m$ and $C_{ss}=\tilde U_s^{\rm T}\tilde U_s$ (eq. 10). Repeat for each of the three trade attributes, giving nine matrices (figure 6's 3×3 grid).
4. **Build the random null** — Generate random response matrices whose mean and standard deviation match the empirical ones, push them through steps 1–3, and plot (figure 7). Empirical overlaps are "remarkable" where random ones are small and scattered.
5. **Diagnose the sign-cancellation problem** — $C_{mm}$ and $C_{ss}$ show clear structure by eye, but $C_{ms}$ does not, because "largely positive and negative overlaps are mixed and look like the random patterns". A heatmap cannot separate sign-mixed structure from noise.
6. **Decompose again** — Apply $C_{xy}=U_{xy}S_{xy}V_{xy}^{\rm T}$ (eq. 11). Now $U_{xy,an}$ correlates the $n$-th **common** factor with the $a$-th individual factor of $x$, and $V_{xy,bn}$ correlates it with the $b$-th individual factor of $y$.
7. **Fit and compare** — Fit both a normal and a $t$ location-scale distribution to the pooled entries $\{U_{xy,an}\}$ and $\{V_{xy,bn}\}$ (figure 8, table 3), and repeat for the random overlap matrices (figure 9). Heavy tails surviving in $C_{ms}$ prove the cross-overlap is non-random even though it looked random in the heatmap.

**Worked example (end-to-end on a 3-stock, 3-factor toy, then the paper's table 3):**
Step 2 on one row: $U_{m,1\cdot}=(0.40,-0.20,0.10,-0.30)$ has mean 0 and $\sigma=\sqrt{0.30/4}=0.273861$, so $\tilde U_{m,1\cdot}=(1.4606,-0.7303,0.3651,-1.0954)$, with $\sum_n\tilde U^2=4=N$.
Step 3 on a full $3\times3$ pair with $a=\sqrt{3/2}=1.224745$ — $\tilde U_m$ rows $(a,-a,0),(0,a,-a),(-a,0,a)$ and $\tilde U_s$ rows $(a,0,-a),(-a,a,0),(0,-a,a)$:
$C_{mm}=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1.5&-1.5\\-1.5&3&-1.5\\-1.5&-1.5&3\end{pmatrix}$, $C_{ms}=\begin{pmatrix}1.5&1.5&-3\\-3&1.5&1.5\\1.5&-3&1.5\end{pmatrix}$.
Intermediate checks: ${\rm tr}\,C_{mm}=9=N^2$, so the **average diagonal entry is $N=3$** (at $N=96$ it would be **96**); and **every row and column sums to zero**, forced by step 2.
Step 6 on $C_{ms}$: it is circulant with first row $(1.5,1.5,-3)$, so its eigenvalues are $\lambda_k=1.5+1.5\omega^k-3\omega^{2k}$ with $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. $\lambda_0=1.5+1.5-3=\mathbf{0}$; $\lambda_1=2.25+3.897114i$ with $|\lambda_1|=\sqrt{5.0625+15.1875}=\sqrt{20.25}=\mathbf{4.5}$; $\lambda_2=\bar\lambda_1$ with $|\lambda_2|=\mathbf{4.5}$. A circulant matrix is normal, so its singular values are the moduli of its eigenvalues: $S_{ms}={\rm diag}(4.5,4.5,0)$. Verification via the Frobenius norm: $\|C_{ms}\|_F^2=3\,(1.5^2+1.5^2+3^2)=3\times13.5=40.5=4.5^2+4.5^2+0^2$ ✓. The **exactly zero** singular value is not an accident — it belongs to the singular vector $(1,1,1)/\sqrt3$, which is in the kernel because every row sums to zero, so at $N=96$ every overlap matrix has rank at most **95** with a guaranteed null direction along the all-ones vector.
Step 7 with the paper's real numbers (table 3): for $C_{ms}$ the traded-volume block gives $(\mu,\sigma,\beta)=(0.00141,0.083,5.655)$ for $U$ and $(-0.00112,0.070,3.421)$ for $V$; the trade-sign block gives $(0.00108,0.041,1.784)$ and $(0.00148,0.094,13.893)$; the signed-volume block gives $(-0.00063,0.038,1.630)$ and $(-0.00129,0.097,22.677)$. Two things read off immediately. First, for $C_{mm}$ and $C_{ss}$ the $U$ and $V$ parameters are **numerically identical** ($\sigma=0.029/0.029$, $\beta=1.378/1.378$ for price/signs; $0.075/0.075$, $4.107/4.109$ for price/volumes; $0.064/0.064$, $2.889/2.889$ for liquidity/volumes) — as they must be, since $C_{mm}=\tilde U_m^{\rm T}\tilde U_m$ is **symmetric positive semi-definite**, so its SVD *is* its eigendecomposition and $U_{mm}=V_{mm}$ up to signs; half of table 3 is therefore redundant by construction. Second, only $C_{ms}$ has genuinely different $U$ and $V$ statistics, and there the right-vector side of the two *signed* channels is near-Gaussian ($\beta=13.9$ and $22.7$, $\sigma=0.094$ and $0.097\approx1/\sqrt{96}$) while the volume channel is heavy-tailed on **both** sides ($\beta=5.66$ and $3.42$) — which is precisely the paper's conclusion that unsigned traded volume is the factor connecting liquidity change to price change.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **Split-feature liquidity and price predictors** — Take §4.1 steps 4–5 and build two separate short-horizon forecasters from the same tape: a **spread/liquidity** model fed **unsigned** traded volume, and a **price** model fed **trade sign and signed** volume. The paper's whole 2×3 design says these two targets do not share informative features, so sharing a feature set wastes capacity on cancelling terms.
  - **Strong:** Cross-sectionally, across the full NASDAQ-100 universe — the paper's tables 1–2 separate the channels cleanly and consistently: liquidity-versus-unsigned-volume gives the heaviest tails in the study ($\beta=1.073$ for $U_s$, $1.344$ for $V_s$) while liquidity-versus-sign is near-Gaussian ($\beta=6.491$ / $6.033$), and the price side reverses ($\beta=0.980$ for signs versus $1.984$ for unsigned volume). *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** In any sign-balanced regime — the spread response to direction is provably self-cancelling: if a widening $\Delta s>0$ follows both buys and sells with comparable magnitude, then $\langle\Delta\tilde s\,\tilde\varepsilon\rangle\to0$ as the buy/sell mix approaches 50/50, while $\langle\Delta\tilde s\,\tilde v\rangle$ keeps its full magnitude because $\tilde v$ never changes sign. The §4.1 worked example shows this concretely: $R_{s,ij}|_\varepsilon=0.354$ versus $R_{s,ij}|_v=-0.474$ on the same four trades. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Strongly one-sided (trending or news-driven) sessions — there the cancellation argument breaks the other way: with a buy fraction $q$, $\langle\tilde\varepsilon\rangle=2q-1\neq0$, so after the standardization (2) the sign series and the signed-volume series become nearly collinear with the unsigned-volume series ($\text{corr}(\tilde\nu,\tilde v)\to1$ as $q\to1$), the three channels stop being distinguishable, and the two "separate" models converge to the same one. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** Sub-second horizons — equation (1) measures the **primary** response only, between the immediately bracketing quotes, so it estimates no decay profile; a predictor calibrated on it has no notion of how long the effect persists and will mis-size any position held beyond the next quote update. *(From paper — the definition explicitly "quantifies the primary price or liquidity impact of a trade, without accounting for subsequent trades")*

- **$\beta$ as a rolling factor-localization monitor** — Reuse §4.2 (steps 1–4) plus §4.3 in a rolling window: recompute the response matrix daily, take its SVD, pool the singular-vector entries, fit eq. (7), and track $\beta$ as a single market-state scalar. Small $\beta$ = response structure concentrated in a few names; large $\beta$ = response diffused and indistinguishable from noise.
  - **Strong:** As a *relative* signal, because the interpretation of $\beta$ is anchored by an exact constraint rather than by convention — the singular vectors are unit-norm, so the pooled second moment of the entries is exactly $1/N$ and the pooled RMS exactly $1/\sqrt N=0.10206$ at $N=96$. Every finite-variance fit in the paper reproduces it: $\sigma\sqrt{\beta/(\beta-2)}$ equals $0.1022$, $0.1027$, $0.1014$, $0.1018$, $0.1021$ and $0.1019$ for six different table-1/2 blocks, all within 1 % of $0.10206$. So $\beta$ moving below 2 (where the model variance diverges) is a well-defined, checkable state change rather than a fitted curiosity. *(From AI)*
  - **Strong:** The paper demonstrates $\beta$ separates informative from random structure over a wide dynamic range in real data — from $0.957$ up to $27.156$ within the same 96-stock, five-day sample, with the ordering matching the mechanical single/multiple-trade split in every one of the nine blocks. *(From paper, tables 1–2)*
