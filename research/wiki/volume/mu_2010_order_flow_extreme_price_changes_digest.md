Notation used throughout this digest, matching the paper: $p(t)$ = mid-price at minute $t$; $r(t)$ = one-minute logarithmic mid-price return; $R_{\Delta t}(t)$ = cumulative return over a window of length $\Delta t$; $v(t,\Delta t)$ = realized volatility of that window; $X(d,t')$ = raw value of a quantity on trading day $d$ at intraday minute $t'\in\{1,\dots,240\}$; $P(t')$ = intraday (seasonal) pattern of that quantity; $x$ = de-seasonalized ("relative") quantity; $V_b, V_s$ = one-minute volume of buy and sell market orders; $I$ = volume imbalance; $x_{\rm ex}$ = excess quantity above its normal level; $\alpha$ = power-law relaxation exponent; $t=0$ = the minute of the extreme event.

## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation and gap.** The paper starts from the observation that the price-formation mechanism is still poorly understood despite well-catalogued stylized facts, and that the most informative place to look is the *neighbourhood of a large change observed at high resolution*. It reviews two adjacent literatures: **Omori-law relaxation of volatility after shocks** (Lillo & Mantegna on the S&P 500 after Black Monday; Selçuk on ten emerging index series; Selçuk & Gençay on 5-min DJIA; confirmations for intermediate shocks in the USA and China), and **relaxation of order-book measures after large price changes** (Sornette et al. on implied variance with log-periodic decoration; Zawadowski et al. on **NYSE/NASDAQ** 15-min extreme moves, finding price reversal and a volatility exponent near **0.4**; Tóth, Kertész & Farmer on the **LSE**; Ponzi, Lillo & Mantegna on spread/mid-price resiliency being slower than exponential). The stated gap is that **every one of these studies used a mature market**. This paper is the first to run the same statistical machinery on an **emerging market**, and the first to exploit order-level identifiers to split the order flow by **direction (buy/sell)**, **aggressiveness (four classes)** and **investor type (individual vs institution)**.

2. **Data.** The study uses a proprietary order-level database from the **Shenzhen Stock Exchange (SZSE)** covering **all order submissions and cancellations of 23 liquid stocks for the whole of 2003**. Each record carries time, price, size, and — crucially — a flag marking whether the submitting investor is an **individual or an institution**. Only the **continuous double auction** (9:30–11:30 and 13:00–15:00) is used; the opening call auction (9:15–9:25) and the cooling period (9:25–9:30) are discarded, leaving **240 intraday minutes** per day. Two microstructure details of the 2003 SZSE matter for the analysis: **buy orders must be integer multiples of a 100-share board lot while sell orders may be any size**, and **only limit orders were permitted** — so what the paper calls "market orders" are strictly *effective* (marketable) limit orders priced through the opposite best quote. The 23 tickers (000001 Shenzhen Development Bank through 000778 Xinxing Ductile Iron Pipes) span financials, real estate, conglomerates, metals, electronics, utilities, IT, transportation, petrochemicals, paper and manufacturing, and were part of the **40 constituents of the Shenzhen Stock Exchange Component Index** in 2003.

3. **Defining an extreme event — a combined absolute-plus-relative filter.** A minute-by-minute mid-price series is built from the last best bid and ask of each minute. An event must pass **both** filters. The **absolute filter** demands a cumulative return above a fixed threshold, with the paper noting that for small windows the arithmetic and logarithmic returns coincide:
   $$R_{\Delta t}(t)=\frac{p(t)-p(t-\Delta t)}{p(t-\Delta t)}\;\approx\;\ln\!\frac{p(t)}{p(t-\Delta t)}=r(t),\qquad |R_{\Delta t}(t)|\ge 4\%,\quad \Delta t=60\ \text{minutes}$$
   where $p$ is the mid-price and $\Delta t$ the window length in minutes. The **relative filter** demands the same cumulative return to exceed **6 times** the volatility of the same window, with volatility defined as the square root of the sum of squared one-minute returns (Eq. 1):
   $$v(t,\Delta t)=\sqrt{\sum_{\tau=t-\Delta t}^{t} r^{2}(\tau)}\ ,\qquad\text{event requires}\quad |R_{\Delta t}(t)|\ \ge\ 6\,v(t,\Delta t)$$
   where $\tau$ indexes the minutes inside the window and $t$ is the window's end. *Worked example:* a stock moves from $p(t-60)=10.00$ to $p(t)=10.42$ yuan, so $R_{60}=0.42/10.00=\mathbf{4.20\%}$ and $r=\ln(1.042)=\mathbf{4.114\%}$ — the **4% absolute filter passes**. Suppose the move is spread over 40 active minutes of $+0.1029\%$ each (and 21 flat minutes): then $v=0.001029\sqrt{40}=0.006508=\mathbf{0.651\%}$, and $6v=\mathbf{3.905\%}<4.114\%$ — the **relative filter also passes**. Had the identical 4.114% arrived in only 5 minutes of $+0.8229\%$ each, $v=0.008229\sqrt{5}=\mathbf{1.840\%}$ and $6v=\mathbf{11.04\%}\gg 4.114\%$ — the relative filter **rejects it**. The paper is explicit that each filter alone is defective: the absolute filter catches ordinary trading because of the intraday volatility pattern and treats volatile and quiet stocks alike, while the relative filter alone catches quiet-period wiggles whose actual price change is negligible.

4. **Event bookkeeping: window shrinking, edge trimming and de-duplication.** After a 60-minute window triggers, the window is **shrunk as far as it can go while still passing both filters**, and $t=0$ is set to the end of the *smallest* qualifying window — this pins the event time as tightly as the data allow. The first **5 minutes** of each day are dropped to remove opening effects and the last **60 minutes** are dropped so that post-event dynamics can be observed. Applying this to 2003 yields **5450 raw events**; to avoid counting one episode repeatedly, only the **first event per stock per day** is retained, leaving **163 events — 131 positive and 32 negative**. The paper therefore concentrates on positive events throughout, explicitly warning that all negative-event results are noisier because $n=32$.

5. **De-seasonalization and event-aligned averaging.** Because volatility, volume and spread all have a strong U-shaped intraday pattern, every raw series is divided by its own intraday pattern before aggregation (Eq. 2):
   $$x(d,t')=\frac{X(d,t')}{P(t')}$$
   where $X(d,t')$ is the raw quantity on day $d$ at intraday minute $t'=1,\dots,240$ and $P(t')$ is the reference intraday pattern of that quantity. After normalization the "normal" level of every quantity is **1 by construction**, so all plotted values read directly as multiples of normal. For each event $k$ a trajectory $\{x_k(t):t=-100,\dots,200\}$ is cut out (100 minutes before, 200 after; if the day ends first the series simply continues into the next trading day, which is legitimate precisely because the seasonal pattern has been divided out), and the group average is (Eq. 3):
   $$x_K(t)=\frac{1}{\lVert K\rVert}\sum_{k\in K}x_k(t),\qquad t=-100,\dots,200$$
   with $\lVert K\rVert$ the number of events in group $K$ and **every event given equal weight**. *Worked example:* a minute carries $X=50{,}000$ shares where the intraday pattern for that minute is $P=25{,}000$ shares, so $x=50{,}000/25{,}000=\mathbf{2.0}$ (twice normal). If three events give $x_k(-1)=2.0,\ 1.4,\ 3.2$ then $x_K(-1)=(2.0+1.4+3.2)/3=\mathbf{2.2}$.

6. **Main result 1 — a sharp overreaction with a largely permanent impact.** Averaged over the 131 positive events (curves shifted so the cumulative return is zero at $t=0$), the price **climbs about 5% over the preceding 100 minutes**, peaks exactly at minute 0, and then **gives back about 1% within the first 10–15 minutes** before settling on a stable plateau. Negative events mirror this but **do not settle** on a constant level. The reversal is thus only about **one fifth of the move**, so the price impact of the order flow around these events is predominantly **permanent** — the paper's own headline reading of Fig. 1. *Worked example of the permanence ratio:* permanent fraction $=(5\%-1\%)/5\%=\mathbf{80\%}$; transient fraction $=1\%/5\%=\mathbf{20\%}$. Compared with Zawadowski et al.'s NYSE/NASDAQ result the overreaction magnitude is consistent but "the peak is sharper" in the SZSE data.

7. **Main result 2 — four basic quantities peak and then relax, with the imbalance leading.** The paper tracks the 1-minute **absolute return**, the total **volume of market orders**, the **bid-ask spread** (best ask minus best bid at the end of each minute) and the **volume imbalance**, which for positive events is the buy share of executed volume (Eq. 4):
   $$I=\frac{V_b}{V_b+V_s}$$
   where $V_b$ and $V_s$ are the one-minute volumes of buy and sell **market** orders (the paper's prose writes $V_b+V_a$ here while the displayed equation writes $V_b+V_s$ — the same quantity, a typographical slip). Note this differs from Tóth et al., who include limit orders in the imbalance. All four quantities show slow accumulation, a sharp peak and a slow decay, but the **peak times differ**: $t_{\max}=0$ for absolute return and volume, $t_{\max}=+1$ for the bid-ask spread, and $t_{\max}=-2$ for the buy imbalance around positive events ($t_{\max}=-7$ for sell imbalance around negative events, though the caption of Fig. 3 says the imbalance is too noisy to locate a maximum — an internal inconsistency). *Worked example reproducing the paper's 79.9% claim:* the unconditional buy imbalance is $I_0=0.47$ and the relative peak is $1.70$, so the absolute peak is $I_{\max}=1.70\times0.47=\mathbf{0.799}$ — i.e. **79.9% of executed volume sits on the buy side** just before a positive event. Concretely, $V_b=800{,}000$ and $V_s=200{,}000$ shares give $I=800{,}000/1{,}000{,}000=0.80$ and a relative value $0.80/0.47=\mathbf{1.70}$. The negative-event counterpart is $1.4\times0.53=\mathbf{0.742}$, i.e. **74% sell-side volume**. The sub-half unconditional buy share ($I_0=0.47$) is attributed to the Chinese market having been bearish from mid-2001 to 2005.

8. **Main result 3 — power-law (Omori-type) relaxation and its exponents.** Relaxation is studied through the **excess** variable (Eq. 5) and fitted with a pure power law (Eq. 6):
   $$x_{\rm ex}(t)=x(t)-x_0=x(t)-1,\qquad x_{\rm ex}(t)\sim t^{-\alpha}$$
   where $x_0=1$ is the normal level created by Eq. (2) and $\alpha$ is the relaxation exponent, estimated by **ordinary least-squares regression of $\ln x_{\rm ex}$ on $\ln t$** over $t\in[1,300]$ minutes. For **positive events**: $\alpha_{\rm AR}=0.50\pm0.03$ (absolute return), $\alpha_{V}=0.47\pm0.02$ (volume), $\alpha_{\rm BAS}=0.60\pm0.09$ (spread), $\alpha_{\rm Imb}=0.23\pm0.06$ (imbalance). For **negative events**: $0.53\pm0.02$, $0.64\pm0.08$, $0.54\pm0.11$, and the imbalance fit "too vague" to report. *Worked example:* if the excess absolute return at $t=1$ is $x_{\rm ex}(1)=8.0-1=7.0$, then with $\alpha=0.50$ the model gives $x_{\rm ex}(100)=7.0\times100^{-0.5}=\mathbf{0.70}$ (so $x=1.70$, still 70% above normal after 100 minutes) and $x_{\rm ex}(300)=7.0/\sqrt{300}=\mathbf{0.404}$ ($x=1.404$). Checking the regression slope from those two endpoints: $\alpha=-\dfrac{\ln 0.404-\ln 7.0}{\ln 300-\ln 1}=-\dfrac{-0.906-1.946}{5.704}=\mathbf{0.500}$, recovering the fitted exponent. Cross-market comparison (Table 2) shows the **SZSE relaxes faster than mature markets**: $\alpha_{\rm AR}\approx0.50$–$0.53$ versus $0.34$–$0.44$ for NYSE/NASDAQ and $\approx0.37$–$0.38$ for the LSE.

9. **Main result 4 — buy/sell lead-lag and the aggressiveness taxonomy.** Splitting market-order volume by direction around **positive** events, **buy volume peaks at $t_{\max}=-1$ at about 20× normal** while **sell volume peaks later and lower, at $t_{\max}=+1$ and about 12× normal**; around negative events the roles swap (buy 8× at $t_{\max}=0$, sell 13× at $t_{\max}=-1$ — the paper mistakenly writes "positive events" for this last figure). The paper explains this as a **propagation chain**: an exogenous or endogenous impulse starts the price rising, hope of a higher fair value pulls in buy market orders, and with a delay sellers arrive to realize profit; before $t=-1$ buy impact dominates, at $t=0$ the two impacts balance, and after $t=0$ sell impact dominates and the price falls back. Orders are then split into **four aggressiveness classes** — **partially filled market orders (PFO), filled market orders (FO), effective limit orders (LO), canceled orders (CO)** — on each side, giving eight series, with peak values (relative volume $V_{\max}$ at $t_{\max}$; relative number $N_{\max}$ at $t'_{\max}$):

   | Type | Buy $t_{\max}$ | Buy $V_{\max}$ | Buy $t'_{\max}$ | Buy $N_{\max}$ | Sell $t_{\max}$ | Sell $V_{\max}$ | Sell $t'_{\max}$ | Sell $N_{\max}$ |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | PFO | 0 | 22.4 | 0 | 12.5 | 1 | 10.3 | 1 | 7.9 |
   | FO | −1 | 19.4 | 1 | 12.9 | 1 | 11.3 | 1 | 12.2 |
   | LO | 0 | 16.4 | 0 | 11.7 | 2 | 19.0 | 2 | 16.9 |
   | CO | −1 | 24.4 | 0 | 18.4 | 4 | 19.0 | 4 | 10.6 |

   Three findings come out of this table. First, market orders and cancellations follow the buy-leads-sell pattern, but **limit orders invert it** — sell limit orders peak *later and higher* (19.0 at $t=+2$) than buy limit orders (16.4 at $t=0$), i.e. the ask side is aggressively replenished as the move exhausts. Second, **canceled buy orders peak at 24.4× normal**, the largest of all eight series, showing buyers frantically cancelling stale queue positions to resubmit more aggressively. Third, **volume peaks before number** for filled buy orders ($t_{\max}=-1$ vs $t'_{\max}=+1$) and for canceled orders, while $t_{\max}=t'_{\max}$ for the other six series. *Worked example of the implied average order size* $S=V/N$: for filled buy orders at the volume peak, taking $N(-1)\approx12$ (just under its own peak of 12.9), $S(-1)\approx19.4/12=\mathbf{1.62}$ — orders **62% larger than normal**; by $t=+1$ with $V\approx18$ and $N=12.9$, $S(+1)\approx\mathbf{1.40}$, a **14% shrinkage**. The paper reads this as small (individual) orders still rushing in after the large (institutional) orders have already left. Excess volumes and excess numbers of all eight series relax as power laws, with exponents ranging from **0.41** (filled sell orders) to **0.85 ± 0.09** (canceled buy orders) for volume and **0.30** (buy limit orders) to **0.65** (sell limit orders) for number; fit ranges are $[1,300]$ except sell limit orders $[2,300]$ and canceled sell orders $[4,300]$.

10. **Main result 5 — individuals versus institutions, relative order rates, and the stated limits.** Repeating the volume analysis with the investor-type flag shows **almost no difference in volume dynamics**: buy market orders peak at $t_{\max}=-2$, $V_{\max}=19.4$ for individuals and $t_{\max}=-1$, $V_{\max}=17.5$ for institutions; sell market orders at $t_{\max}=+1$ with $V_{\max}=10.4$ and $11.9$ respectively (the text mislabels the last as "buy"). The difference emerges in the **relative order rates**, the shares of order *counts* by type within a minute:
    $$\rho_j(t)=\frac{N_j(t)}{N_{\rm MO}(t)+N_{\rm LO}(t)+N_{\rm CO}(t)},\qquad j\in\{{\rm MO},{\rm LO},{\rm CO}\}$$
    where $N_j$ counts orders of type $j$ (market, limit, cancellation) submitted in that minute by that investor class. *Worked example reproducing the paper's normal-period numbers:* an individual-investor minute with $N_{\rm MO}=26$, $N_{\rm LO}=60$, $N_{\rm CO}=14$ gives $\rho=(\mathbf{0.26},\mathbf{0.60},\mathbf{0.14})$, exactly the paper's individual baseline; $N=(42,49,9)$ gives $\rho=(\mathbf{0.42},\mathbf{0.49},\mathbf{0.09})$, the institutional baseline. **Institutions place proportionally far more market orders (0.42 vs 0.26) and cancel less (0.09 vs 0.14)** — the paper's evidence that institutions are more aggressive. Around the event the rates move only mildly (consistent with the LSE), but for individuals the market-order rate rises before $t=0$, **undershoots below normal right after**, and then recovers with an *increasing* trend, while the institutional market-order rate shows a noisy *decreasing* trend — i.e. individuals keep buying aggressively after the peak while institutions withdraw. The paper also compares the marketable share $\phi=N_{\rm MO}/(N_{\rm MO}+N_{\rm LO})$: **0.30 for SZSE versus 0.25 for the LSE**, and notes the LSE cancellation rate (0.35) is far above the SZSE's (0.14). Splitting by side (Fig. 11) shows a **strong buy-sell asymmetry for individuals** but near-cancellation of buy and sell deviations for institutions, again read as strategic behaviour versus event-following. **Stated limitations:** only **32 negative events** make all downside results noisy and the negative-event imbalance unfittable; both filters are individually flawed; the imbalance definition is non-standard so the comparison with Tóth et al. is not like-for-like; and the whole study is a **single market, a single year (2003), 23 large-cap stocks** in a regime where market orders were not even a legal order type.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### The combined absolute-plus-relative event filter

The paper's operational definition of "extreme" is the conjunction of a fixed 4%/60-minute threshold and a scale-free requirement that the move exceed 6 times the window's own realized volatility $v(t,\Delta t)=\sqrt{\sum r^2}$, followed by a window-shrinking step that localizes $t=0$ as sharply as the data permit and a one-event-per-stock-per-day de-duplication that cuts 5450 raw triggers to 163 studied episodes.

**Why it matters:** this is the piece of the paper most **directly reusable** in a trading system, because it is the only fully specified, parameter-light way to say "this move is genuinely unusual *for this stock, right now*". The absolute leg alone would fire constantly on volatile names and never on quiet ones; the relative leg alone would fire on statistically loud but economically trivial wiggles. Their conjunction is what makes the 163 events comparable enough that averaging them produces clean curves at all — and, as Section 5 shows, the same conjunction silently **restricts the sample to slowly accumulated moves**, which is the single most important caveat for anyone reusing it.

### Power-law relaxation as the universal shape of post-event order flow

Every quantity measured — absolute return, volume, spread, imbalance, and all eight direction × aggressiveness volume and count series — decays as $x_{\rm ex}(t)\sim t^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha$ between roughly 0.23 and 0.85, over a fit range spanning more than two decades in time.

**Why it matters:** the functional form is the **verdict**. A power law with $\alpha<1$ has **no characteristic time scale** — no half-life, and a divergent time integral — so any risk model that assumes volatility, spread or volume mean-revert exponentially after a shock will **systematically underestimate how long elevated conditions persist**. The exponents are also a compact cross-market fingerprint: SZSE's $\alpha_{\rm AR}\approx0.50$ against NYSE's $0.34$ says the emerging market **digests shocks faster**, which is the paper's central comparative claim and a directly testable prediction for other emerging venues.

### Volume imbalance of market orders as a leading indicator

Defined narrowly on executed volume only, $I=V_b/(V_b+V_s)$, the imbalance is the only one of the four basic quantities whose peak **precedes** the price peak — $t_{\max}=-2$ for positive events and $t_{\max}=-7$ for negative ones — and its excursion is large: 79.9% buy-side against a 47% unconditional baseline.

**Why it matters:** a peak at negative $t$ is, in principle, **exploitable**, whereas peaks at $t=0$ or $t=+1$ are only diagnostic. This is also where the paper **departs from the LSE literature**, which found $t_{\max}>0$; the paper attributes the difference to its market-order-only definition, which makes the choice of imbalance definition a **first-order design decision** rather than a detail. The fact that the imbalance has the flattest relaxation exponent ($0.23$) additionally means order-flow direction stays skewed long after price and volume have normalized.

### Buy-sell lead-lag and the order-flow propagation chain

Around positive events buy market-order volume peaks earlier ($t=-1$) and higher (20×) than sell market-order volume ($t=+1$, 12×), and the roles invert around negative events. The paper's mechanism: an impulse starts the price moving, expectation of a new fair value pulls in same-direction market orders, then profit-taking orders arrive with a lag; the crossover of the two aggregate price impacts at $t=0$ is what produces the reversal.

**Why it matters:** this converts the observed reversal from a statistical curiosity into a **causal story with a measurable trigger** — the crossing point of two order-flow curves. It also explains *why* the impact is 80% permanent rather than fully transient: the profit-taking flow arrives large enough to cap and slightly reverse the move, but never large enough to unwind it. Any reversal-fade strategy therefore has a hard ceiling on its harvestable edge, set by the relative heights of the two peaks.

### Order aggressiveness classes, and the volume-versus-number divergence

Splitting each side into partially filled market orders, filled market orders, effective limit orders and cancellations exposes behaviour invisible in aggregate volume: **sell limit orders invert the lead-lag pattern** (peaking later and higher than buy limit orders), **canceled buy orders show the largest peak of all** (24.4×), and for filled buy orders and cancellations the **volume peak arrives before the count peak**, so average order size is falling while activity is still rising.

**Why it matters:** the volume/number divergence is a **size-based proxy for information** that needs no investor identifier — anyone with public trade-and-quote data can compute $V/N$. The paper's inference is that large orders lead and small orders follow, i.e. **the informed participants are already leaving while the crowd is still arriving**. That is the paper's most transferable microstructure signal, and it is also the claim most in tension with its own investor-type results (see below).

### Individual versus institutional order strategies

With direct investor-type labels, **volume dynamics are essentially identical** across the two groups, but the **composition of order flow is not**: institutions run a market-order share of 0.42 versus 0.26 for individuals, cancel less (0.09 vs 0.14), keep buy and sell rate deviations nearly cancelling around events, and reduce market-order intensity after the peak while individuals increase theirs.

**Why it matters:** the paper's headline conclusion — that **institutions are more aggressive and more informed** — rests entirely on this section, and it does so through *composition* rather than *timing*. That is an important methodological lesson: the informational asymmetry between investor classes shows up in **which order type they choose**, not in how much volume they push, so any attempt to detect institutional footprints from volume alone is looking in the wrong place. It is also the section that **undercuts** the size-based inference of the previous topic, since the labelled data show no qualitative volume-dynamics difference between the groups.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

### Algorithm A — Extreme-event detection by combined filtering, window shrinking and de-duplication

**Goal:** identify, from a year of order-book data, a set of intraday price moves that are simultaneously large in absolute terms and large relative to the stock's own contemporaneous volatility, and time-stamp each one to a single minute.

**Inputs:** for each stock, the best bid and best ask at the end of every minute of the continuous double auction; thresholds $\theta_{\rm abs}=4\%$, $\theta_{\rm rel}=6$, and maximum window $\Delta t_{\max}=60$ minutes. **Outputs:** a list of events, each a triple (stock, day, event minute $t=0$), signed positive or negative.

**Steps:**

1. **Build the minute mid-price series.** For each minute of the continuous auction, take the last best bid $b$ and last best ask $a$ and set $p=(a+b)/2$. Using the mid-price rather than the trade price avoids bid-ask bounce contaminating the return series, which matters because the filter squares returns.
2. **Compute one-minute log returns.** $r(\tau)=\ln p(\tau)-\ln p(\tau-1)$. The paper justifies mixing arithmetic and log returns by noting $R_{\Delta t}(t)=[p(t)-p(t-\Delta t)]/p(t-\Delta t)\approx\ln[p(t)/p(t-\Delta t)]$ for small windows.
3. **Trim the day.** Discard the first 5 minutes (opening effects) and the last 60 minutes (so that 60-minute windows and post-event observation are both possible).
4. **Apply the absolute filter over a rolling 60-minute window.** Flag minute $t$ if $|R_{60}(t)|=|\ln p(t)-\ln p(t-60)|\ge 4\%$. This screens out economically trivial moves.
5. **Apply the relative filter to the same window.** Compute the window's realized volatility $v(t,\Delta t)=\sqrt{\sum_{\tau=t-\Delta t}^{t}r^{2}(\tau)}$ and require $|R_{\Delta t}(t)|\ge 6\,v(t,\Delta t)$. This screens out moves that are unremarkable given how noisy the stock has been. Note the sum runs *inclusive of* the end minute $t$, so the event's own return contributes to its own threshold.
6. **Shrink the window.** With the event confirmed at $\Delta t=60$, repeatedly reduce $\Delta t$ and re-test both filters; keep going while both still pass. The **smallest** $\Delta t$ that still passes defines the event, and its window **end** is declared $t=0$. This sharpens the alignment: without it, a slow 90-minute drift and a fast 10-minute jump would both be stamped at the end of an arbitrary 60-minute box.
7. **Sign and de-duplicate.** Label the event positive if $R>0$, negative otherwise. Because a single episode makes many consecutive minutes pass the filters, retain only the **first** event per stock per day.

**Worked example (with intermediate values):** take stock 000001 on some day, with mid-prices $p(t-60)=10.00$ and $p(t)=10.42$ yuan.
- Step 4: $R_{60}=\ln(10.42/10.00)=\ln 1.042=0.04114=\mathbf{4.114\%}\ge 4\%$ → **absolute filter passes**.
- Step 5: suppose the 60-minute window contains 40 minutes at $+0.1029\%$ and 21 flat minutes. Then $\sum r^2=40\times(0.001029)^2=4.235\times10^{-5}$, so $v=\sqrt{4.235\times10^{-5}}=0.006508=\mathbf{0.651\%}$ and $6v=\mathbf{3.905\%}$. Since $4.114\%\ge3.905\%$ → **relative filter passes**.
- Step 6: try $\Delta t=45$. If the last 45 minutes contain 30 active minutes, $R_{45}=30\times0.1029\%=3.087\%<4\%$ → the absolute filter fails, so 45 is too small. Try $\Delta t=55$ with 37 active minutes: $R_{55}=3.807\%<4\%$ → still fails. So the smallest qualifying window here is **$\Delta t=60$**, and $t=0$ is the 60th minute.
- Step 7: $R>0$ → **positive event**. If minute $t$ is not the first qualifying minute of that stock-day, drop it. Repeating over 23 stocks × 2003 gives **5450 raw flags → 163 retained events (131 positive, 32 negative)**.

**Structural note verifiable from the numbers above:** the ratio $|R|/v=\left|\sum r_i\right|/\sqrt{\sum r_i^{2}}$ obeys, by Cauchy–Schwarz, $|R|/v\le\sqrt{n}$ where $n$ is the number of non-zero return minutes in the window. Requiring $|R|/v\ge 6$ therefore forces $n\ge 36$. In the example above $|R|/v=4.114/0.651=6.32\le\sqrt{40}=6.32$ — the bound is exactly attained because all 40 active returns were equal. This means the filter, read literally, **admits only moves accumulated over at least ~36 minutes** and structurally excludes single-minute crashes.

### Algorithm B — Intraday-pattern removal and event-aligned aggregation

**Goal:** make trajectories from different stocks, different days and different times of day directly comparable and averageable, so that a mean event curve is meaningful.

**Inputs:** a raw minute series $X(d,t')$ for one quantity (volume, absolute return, spread, imbalance, order counts by class, …) across all days $d$ and intraday minutes $t'=1,\dots,240$; the event list from Algorithm A. **Outputs:** the group-average trajectory $x_K(t)$ for $t=-100,\dots,+200$, expressed in multiples of the normal level.

**Steps:**

1. **Estimate the intraday pattern.** For the quantity in question, compute the reference profile $P(t')$ across all trading days — the typical U-shaped level at each intraday minute. Every quantity gets its own $P(t')$.
2. **Normalize.** $x(d,t')=X(d,t')/P(t')$ (Eq. 2). After this step the average level of $x$ in normal conditions is $1$, so any plotted value reads as "times normal". This is what makes the later definition $x_{\rm ex}=x-1$ legitimate.
3. **Cut event windows.** For each event $k$ at $(d_k,t'_k)$, extract $\{x_k(t)\}$ for $t=-100$ to $+200$ where $t=t'-t'_k$. If fewer than 200 minutes remain in the day, continue into the next trading day — permissible precisely because the seasonal component has already been divided out.
4. **Average with equal weights.** $x_K(t)=\frac{1}{\lVert K\rVert}\sum_{k\in K}x_k(t)$ (Eq. 3), separately for the positive group ($\lVert K\rVert=131$) and the negative group ($\lVert K\rVert=32$).
5. **Read off the peak.** Locate $t_{\max}=\arg\max_t x_K(t)$ and $x_K(t_{\max})$; these are the $(t_{\max},V_{\max})$ pairs tabulated throughout the paper.

**Worked example (with intermediate values):** three positive events for the quantity "buy market-order volume".
- Step 1–2: event 1 occurs at intraday minute 130, where $P(130)=25{,}000$ shares. Raw volume in the minute before is $X=480{,}000$ → $x_1(-1)=480{,}000/25{,}000=\mathbf{19.2}$.
- Event 2 occurs at minute 35, where the pattern is higher, $P(34)=40{,}000$; raw volume $X=840{,}000$ → $x_2(-1)=840{,}000/40{,}000=\mathbf{21.0}$. Without normalization these two would have been averaged as 480k and 840k and the morning event would have dominated purely because mornings are busy.
- Event 3: $P=30{,}000$, $X=600{,}000$ → $x_3(-1)=\mathbf{20.0}$.
- Step 4: $x_K(-1)=(19.2+21.0+20.0)/3=\mathbf{20.07}$ — matching the paper's "about 20 times its average value in regular moments" at $t_{\max}=-1$.
- Step 5: if $x_K(-2)=18.0$, $x_K(-1)=20.07$, $x_K(0)=19.5$, then $t_{\max}=\mathbf{-1}$ and $V_{\max}=\mathbf{20.07}$.

### Algorithm C — Construction of the four basic quantities, including the market-order volume imbalance

**Goal:** produce, minute by minute, the four state variables whose event dynamics the paper reports: volatility proxy, activity proxy, liquidity-cost proxy, and order-flow-direction proxy.

**Inputs:** the minute mid-price series; the trade/execution tape with each execution's side and share volume; the best bid and best ask at each minute end. **Outputs:** four raw minute series ready for Algorithm B — $|r(t)|$, $V(t)$, $s(t)$, $I(t)$.

**Steps:**

1. **Absolute return.** $|r(t)|=|\ln p(t)-\ln p(t-1)|$, the 1-minute volatility proxy.
2. **Market-order volume.** $V(t)=V_b(t)+V_s(t)$, the total shares executed via marketable orders in the minute, **adjusted for stock splits** so that 2003's split events do not create spurious volume jumps.
3. **Bid-ask spread.** $s(t)=a(t)-b(t)$, best ask minus best bid at the **end** of the minute — a snapshot, not a time-weighted average.
4. **Volume imbalance.** For positive events, the buy share of executed volume $I=V_b/(V_b+V_s)$ (Eq. 4); for negative events, the analogous sell share $I=V_s/(V_b+V_s)$. Only market orders enter — limit orders are deliberately excluded, unlike Tóth et al.
5. **Hand off to Algorithm B.** Each of the four gets its own intraday pattern $P(t')$ and is normalized and averaged. For the imbalance the normalization means the plotted "relative imbalance" is $I/I_0$, with the unconditional levels $I_0=0.47$ (buy) and $I_0=0.53$ (sell).

**Worked example (with intermediate values):** one minute just before a positive event, on a stock quoted 10.41 bid / 10.43 ask, previous mid 10.38, current mid 10.42.
- Step 1: $|r|=|\ln(10.42/10.38)|=|\ln 1.003854|=0.003847=\mathbf{0.385\%}$. If the intraday pattern for absolute return at that minute is $0.055\%$, the relative absolute return is $0.385/0.055=\mathbf{7.0}$.
- Step 2: $V_b=800{,}000$, $V_s=200{,}000$ → $V=\mathbf{1{,}000{,}000}$ shares. With pattern $P=70{,}000$, relative volume $=1{,}000{,}000/70{,}000=\mathbf{14.3}$ — the order of magnitude of the paper's Fig. 2b peak.
- Step 3: $s=10.43-10.41=\mathbf{0.02}$ yuan. With a normal spread of $0.0125$ yuan, relative spread $=0.02/0.0125=\mathbf{1.60}$, matching Fig. 2c's peak region.
- Step 4: $I=800{,}000/1{,}000{,}000=\mathbf{0.800}$.
- Step 5: relative imbalance $=0.800/0.47=\mathbf{1.702}$ — reproducing the paper's reported peak of "about 1.70" and its 79.9% statement.

### Algorithm D — Power-law relaxation fitting

**Goal:** quantify how fast each quantity returns to normal after the event with a single number, and compare that number across quantities, order types and markets.

**Inputs:** a group-average trajectory $x_K(t)$ from Algorithm B, for $t\ge1$; a fit range $[t_1,t_2]$. **Outputs:** the exponent $\alpha$, its standard error, and the fit range actually used.

**Steps:**

1. **Form the excess variable.** $x_{\rm ex}(t)=x_K(t)-1$ (Eq. 5). The subtraction of exactly 1 is only valid because Algorithm B set the normal level to 1; skipping the normalization would leave an unknown asymptote and force a three-parameter fit.
2. **Posit the power law.** $x_{\rm ex}(t)\sim t^{-\alpha}$ (Eq. 6), the same form found for mature markets and the analogue of the Omori law for aftershocks.
3. **Linearize.** $\ln x_{\rm ex}(t)=c-\alpha\ln t$, so the exponent is minus the slope in log-log coordinates.
4. **Choose the fit range.** The paper uses $[1,300]$ minutes for the four basic quantities and for most order-type series, but shifts the start to $t_1=2$ for sell limit orders and $t_1=4$ for canceled sell orders — precisely the series whose peaks occur at $t_{\max}=+2$ and $+4$, since a power law cannot be fitted through a still-rising segment.
5. **Estimate by OLS.** Run a linear least-squares regression of $\ln x_{\rm ex}$ on $\ln t$ and report $\alpha$ with its regression standard error.
6. **Interpret and compare.** Larger $\alpha$ means faster relaxation. Tabulate against other quantities (Table 1) and other markets (Table 2).

**Worked example (with intermediate values):** excess absolute return around positive events, with $x_K(1)=8.0$.
- Step 1: $x_{\rm ex}(1)=8.0-1=\mathbf{7.0}$.
- Step 2–3: assume the paper's $\alpha_{\rm AR}=0.50$, so $x_{\rm ex}(t)=7.0\,t^{-0.50}$.
- Predicted values: $x_{\rm ex}(10)=7.0/\sqrt{10}=\mathbf{2.214}$ → $x_K(10)=3.21$; $x_{\rm ex}(100)=7.0/10=\mathbf{0.700}$ → $x_K(100)=1.70$; $x_{\rm ex}(300)=7.0/17.32=\mathbf{0.404}$ → $x_K(300)=1.404$.
- Step 5 check with the two endpoints of the fit range: $\hat\alpha=-\dfrac{\ln0.404-\ln7.0}{\ln300-\ln1}=-\dfrac{(-0.906)-(1.946)}{5.704}=\dfrac{2.852}{5.704}=\mathbf{0.500}$ ✓.
- Step 6, decay horizon: the time for the excess to fall to a fraction $q$ of its $t=1$ value is $t=q^{-1/\alpha}$. For $q=0.1$ and $\alpha=0.50$, $t=10^{2.00}=\mathbf{100}$ minutes. For volume, $\alpha=0.47$ → $t=10^{1/0.47}=10^{2.128}=\mathbf{134}$ minutes. For canceled buy orders, $\alpha=0.85$ → $t=10^{1/0.85}=10^{1.176}=\mathbf{15.0}$ minutes. The imbalance, $\alpha=0.23$ → $t=10^{4.35}\approx\mathbf{22{,}400}$ minutes ($\approx$ 93 trading days), i.e. effectively **no relaxation within the observable window**.

### Algorithm E — Order taxonomy: aggressiveness class, direction, investor type, and relative rates

**Goal:** decompose the raw order stream into the eight (and later sixteen) sub-streams whose separate dynamics reveal who is doing what around the event, and compute the composition statistics that distinguish investor classes.

**Inputs:** every order submission and cancellation record (time, price, size, side, investor-type flag); the contemporaneous opposite-side best quote and outstanding queue volume. **Outputs:** per-minute volume $V$ and count $N$ series for each (class × side × investor-type) cell, plus the relative rates $\rho_j$ and the marketable share $\phi$.

**Steps:**

1. **Classify by price aggressiveness.** A buy order priced above the best ask (or a sell priced below the best bid) is an **effective market order** (marketable); every other order is an **effective limit order**. In 2003 SZSE no true market orders existed, so this reclassification is mandatory rather than cosmetic.
2. **Split marketable orders by fill completeness.** Compare the order size against the total outstanding volume in the opposite book at prices no less aggressive than the order's limit. If the order size exceeds it, the order is **split into two records**: a **partially filled order** for the executed shares and an **effective limit order** for the residual that joins the book. If fully executable, it is a **filled order**. The paper justifies keeping PFO and FO separate on the grounds that they have very different price-impact behaviour.
3. **Add cancellations.** Every cancellation record becomes a **canceled order** with its remaining size as its volume. This yields the four classes PFO, FO, LO, CO.
4. **Split by side and by investor type.** Cross the four classes with buy/sell (8 cells) and, where needed, with individual/institution (16 cells).
5. **Aggregate to the minute, twice.** For each cell compute both the **volume** $V(t)=\sum$ shares and the **number** $N(t)=\#$ orders. Running both is what exposes the $t_{\max}$ vs $t'_{\max}$ divergence.
6. **De-seasonalize and average.** Push every cell series through Algorithm B; read off $(t_{\max},V_{\max})$ and $(t'_{\max},N_{\max})$.
7. **Compute relative rates.** Per investor class and minute, $\rho_j=N_j/(N_{\rm MO}+N_{\rm LO}+N_{\rm CO})$ for $j\in\{{\rm MO,LO,CO}\}$, and the marketable share $\phi=N_{\rm MO}/(N_{\rm MO}+N_{\rm LO})$. Compare event-window values to normal-period averages.
8. **Fit relaxations.** Push every excess volume and excess count series through Algorithm D.

**Worked example (with intermediate values):** one minute of an individual investor's flow around a positive event.
- Step 1–3: the minute contains 100 records. Best ask is 10.43. 26 buy/sell orders are priced through the opposite best quote → marketable. Of those, 11 exceed the available opposite depth → **11 PFO records plus 11 residual LO records**; the other 15 are **FO**. There are 49 further orders priced passively → **LO**. There are 14 cancellations → **CO**.
- Step 4–5: counts become $N_{\rm PFO}=11$, $N_{\rm FO}=15$, $N_{\rm LO}=49+11=60$, $N_{\rm CO}=14$. Marketable count $N_{\rm MO}=11+15=\mathbf{26}$.
- Step 7: total $=26+60+14=100$, so $\rho_{\rm MO}=\mathbf{0.26}$, $\rho_{\rm LO}=\mathbf{0.60}$, $\rho_{\rm CO}=\mathbf{0.14}$ — exactly the paper's individual-investor baseline. Marketable share $\phi=26/(26+60)=26/86=\mathbf{0.302}$, matching the paper's reported SZSE figure of 0.30. For an institutional minute with $N=(42,49,9)$: $\rho=(\mathbf{0.42},\mathbf{0.49},\mathbf{0.09})$ and $\phi=42/91=\mathbf{0.462}$.
- Step 6 divergence check for filled buy orders: with relative $V(-1)=19.4$ and relative $N(-1)\approx12$, the relative mean size is $S(-1)=19.4/12=\mathbf{1.62}$; at $t=+1$ with $V\approx18$ and $N=12.9$, $S(+1)=\mathbf{1.40}$. The **13.6% drop** in average order size between $t=-1$ and $t=+1$ is the quantitative content of the paper's "large orders lead, small orders follow" claim.
- Step 8: the excess volume of canceled buy orders is fitted over $[1,300]$ giving $\alpha=\mathbf{0.85\pm0.09}$, the fastest decay in the paper; the excess count of buy limit orders gives $\alpha=\mathbf{0.30\pm0.01}$, the slowest.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **Imbalance-lead early-warning signal** — take the market-order-only imbalance of Algorithm C step 4 and the relative-value normalization of Algorithm B, and run them live as a streaming indicator rather than as an event-study output. The tradable content is that the imbalance peaks at $t_{\max}=-2$ (positive events) and $t_{\max}=-7$ (negative events), i.e. **before** the price peak, whereas volume and volatility peak at $t=0$ and the spread at $t=+1$.
  - **Strong:** on liquid large-cap emerging-market names in a bearish regime, the excursion is enormous — the buy share reaches **0.799 against a 0.47 unconditional baseline** — and it is the only measured quantity with a negative $t_{\max}$ (Sec. 4.1–4.2, Figs. 2d and 3d, Table of $t_{\max}$ values) *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** the signal is statistically detectable from a **single minute** of data in active names. Treating the buy volume share as approximately binomial with per-order success probability $p=I_0=0.47$ over $n$ effectively independent order arrivals, $\mathrm{SD}(I)\approx\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}=\sqrt{0.2491/n}$. With $n=100$ orders in the minute, $\mathrm{SD}(I)=0.0499$, so the observed shift to $0.799$ is $z=(0.799-0.47)/0.0499=\mathbf{6.6\sigma}$ *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** unreliable on the downside. Only **32 negative events** survive filtering, the negative-event imbalance curve is described as "very noisy", Fig. 3's caption says no proper maximum can be identified at all (contradicting the body text's $t_{\max}=-7$), and the negative-event imbalance power-law fit was **too vague to report** (Sec. 4.2–4.3, Table 1) *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** in thin minutes the indicator is almost pure noise. With the same binomial model, $\mathrm{SD}(I)=\sqrt{0.2491/n}$, so at $n=4$ orders per minute $\mathrm{SD}(I)=0.250$, and a spurious reading of $I=1.0$ occurs with probability $p^4=0.47^4=\mathbf{4.9\%}$ per minute — roughly once every 20 minutes by chance alone. Any live threshold must therefore be volume-conditioned, not fixed *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** the paper averages the **ratio** across events, $x_K=\frac1{\lVert K\rVert}\sum V_b^{(k)}/(V_b^{(k)}+V_s^{(k)})$ (Algorithm B step 4), whereas a portfolio-level live signal naturally computes the **ratio of sums**. By Jensen's inequality these differ whenever the per-event volumes differ, and for a concave-in-$V_s$ ratio the event-average exceeds the pooled ratio, so the published 0.799 is an **upper-biased target** for a pooled implementation *(From AI)*

- **Aggressiveness-spectrum reversal fade** — reuse Algorithm E's four-class taxonomy (especially step 2's PFO/FO split and step 7's relative rates) as the feature set for a short-horizon mean-reversion trade triggered at the crossover point identified in Algorithm A. The distinctive features are the **sell-limit-order inversion** (sell LO peaks at $t=+2$ with $V_{\max}=19.0$ versus buy LO at $t=0$ with $16.4$) and the **canceled-buy spike** (24.4×), both of which mark the ask side being rebuilt as the move exhausts.
  - **Strong:** the reversal itself is a robust, repeatable feature — the price rises ~5% over 100 minutes, then **retraces about 1% within 10–15 minutes** and settles, across 131 independent positive events (Sec. 3, Fig. 1a; restated in Sec. 8) *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** the trade clears its cost hurdle by a workable margin under the paper's own spread numbers. Net edge $=|{\rm overreaction}|-(s_{\rm event}+2c)$ where $s_{\rm event}$ is the round-trip half-spread cost at event-time spreads and $c$ the one-way commission. With a 0.01-yuan tick on a ~10-yuan stock the normal relative spread is ~0.10%, and Fig. 2c puts the event spread at **1.6×** normal, i.e. ~0.16%; taking $c\approx0.12\%$ (2003 China commission plus stamp duty) gives net $=1.00\%-(0.16\%+0.24\%)=\mathbf{+0.60\%}$ per trade over ~15 minutes *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** unusable in the negative-event direction, where the paper explicitly reports that "the price after large negative events **does not seem to relax to a constant level**" (Sec. 8) — there is no stable retracement target to exit against *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** the harvestable edge is hard-capped by the permanence of the impact. Only $1\%$ of a $5\%$ move is given back, so the maximum retracement fraction is $1/5=\mathbf{20\%}$; a strategy sized on the assumption of full mean reversion would be wrong by $4\%$ of notional on the average event, i.e. **6.7× the 0.60% net edge computed above**. Position sizing must therefore target the 1% transient component only *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** the edge is fragile to the spread assumption, and the spread is the quantity that relaxes *slowest relative to its accumulation*. The paper notes the post-event spread relaxation is **slower than its accumulation** (unlike return, volume and imbalance) with $\alpha_{\rm BAS}=0.60\pm0.09$ from a $t=+1$ peak; if the realized spread over the 15-minute holding window averages 1.4× rather than 1.0× normal on **both** legs, the cost term rises to $0.28\%+0.24\%=0.52\%$ and the net edge halves to $\mathbf{+0.48\%}$ *(From AI)*

- **Relaxation-exponent clock for holding periods and risk horizons** — take Algorithm D wholesale and fit $\alpha$ per instrument, per quantity and per order class, then use the closed-form decay horizon $t_q=q^{-1/\alpha}$ (derived in Algorithm D step 6) to set stop-loss windows, volatility-forecast horizons and post-event position-holding times instead of using a fixed clock.
  - **Strong:** the power law is empirically solid where it matters most — the fit spans **more than two decades** of time ($[1,300]$ minutes), and the two headline exponents have tight errors: $\alpha_{\rm AR}=0.50\pm0.03$ (6% relative) and $\alpha_V=0.47\pm0.02$ (4% relative), with the paper noting the fits are "more significant with less fluctuations and longer scaling ranges" for return and volume (Sec. 4.3, Table 1) *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** the exponents differentiate horizons by an order of magnitude, so the clock actually carries information. Using $t_{0.1}=10^{1/\alpha}$: canceled buy orders ($\alpha=0.85$) clear 90% of their excess in $10^{1.176}=\mathbf{15}$ minutes, filled buy orders ($\alpha=0.57$) in $10^{1.754}=\mathbf{57}$ minutes, aggregate volume ($\alpha=0.47$) in $10^{2.128}=\mathbf{134}$ minutes, and buy limit-order counts ($\alpha=0.30$) in $10^{3.33}\approx\mathbf{2100}$ minutes — nearly nine trading days *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** the clock is unusable for the two quantities a risk manager most wants it for. The spread exponent carries a **15% relative error** ($0.60\pm0.09$, and $0.54\pm0.11$ for negative events, i.e. 20%) and the imbalance exponent a **26% relative error** ($0.23\pm0.06$), with the negative-event imbalance fit abandoned entirely (Sec. 4.3, Table 1) *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** every reported exponent satisfies $\alpha<1$, and $\int_{1}^{T}t^{-\alpha}\,dt=\dfrac{T^{1-\alpha}-1}{1-\alpha}\to\infty$ as $T\to\infty$. There is therefore **no finite mean relaxation time** and no half-life: cumulative excess volatility keeps growing with the horizon, so any "the shock has passed after $k$ minutes" rule is arbitrary, and the fitted $\alpha$ becomes sensitive to the choice of $t_2$. Concretely, for $\alpha=0.47$ the cumulative excess over $[1,300]$ is $(300^{0.53}-1)/0.53=\mathbf{37.0}$ units·minutes while over $[1,3000]$ it is $(3000^{0.53}-1)/0.53=\mathbf{124.6}$ — a **3.4× increase from a 10× longer window** *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** log-log OLS on a decaying noisy series biases $\alpha$ upward. The late-tail points have $x_{\rm ex}$ small and noise comparable to the level, and $\mathbb{E}[\ln X]\le\ln\mathbb{E}[X]$ (Jensen), so the fitted line is pulled **down** at large $t$ where the relative noise is largest, **steepening** the slope. This is a plausible partial explanation of why the SZSE exponents ($0.47$–$0.64$) exceed the NYSE/LSE values ($0.25$–$0.44$) from studies with far larger event samples, and should be checked with maximum-likelihood or weighted fits before treating the emerging-versus-mature contrast as physical *(From AI)*

- **Volume-versus-count divergence as a label-free large-order detector** — implement Algorithm E steps 5–6 (compute both $V(t)$ and $N(t)$ for every cell) and monitor the derived mean order size $S(t)=V(t)/N(t)$. The exploitable pattern is $t_{\max}<t'_{\max}$ for filled buy orders ($-1$ vs $+1$) and canceled orders, meaning average order size peaks and turns down while activity is still climbing. This needs **no investor identifier** and so is portable to any market with public trade-and-quote data.
  - **Strong:** the paper measures the divergence directly and cleanly — Table 3 gives $t_{\max}=-1,\ V_{\max}=19.4$ but $t'_{\max}=+1,\ N_{\max}=12.9$ for filled buy orders, and $t_{\max}=-1,\ V_{\max}=24.4$ but $t'_{\max}=0,\ N_{\max}=18.4$ for canceled buy orders, with $t_{\max}=t'_{\max}$ for the other six cells — so the divergence is specific, not a generic artifact (Sec. 6, Table 3) *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** $S=V/N$ is a strictly monotone transformation of the two series, so the peak-time gap translates directly into a sign condition on $dS/dt$. Numerically, $S(-1)=19.4/12=1.62$ and $S(+1)=18/12.9=1.40$, a **13.6% decline in average order size across the 2-minute gap** while order count rose ~7.5% — the divergence is large enough to survive realistic measurement noise *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** the paper's own labelled data contradict the inference. Section 8 states flatly that "**no qualitative difference has been observed in the volume dynamics around extreme events between individuals and institutions**", and Sec. 7.1 finds the investor type has "negligible effect" (peaks of 19.4 for individuals vs 17.5 for institutions on the buy side). So the size-based proxy for "institutional" is **not validated by the ground truth available in the same dataset** *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** the $V/N$ statistic is mechanically asymmetric between sides on this exchange. Section 2 records that **buy orders must be integer multiples of 100 shares while sell orders may be any size**, so buy-side sizes are quantized with a floor of 100 shares and sell-side sizes are continuous down to 1 share. $\mathbb{E}[S_{\rm buy}]$ is therefore inflated relative to $\mathbb{E}[S_{\rm sell}]$ by construction, and any cross-side comparison of mean order size — or of the buy/sell $V$ peaks (20× vs 12×) as evidence of buyer dominance — is confounded by the lot rule *(From AI)*

- **Path-efficiency ratio as a slow-drift versus jump classifier** — extract just the relative filter of Algorithm A step 5 and use $\eta(t,\Delta t)=|R_{\Delta t}(t)|/v(t,\Delta t)$ as a standalone, scale-free intraday trend-quality statistic, applying different playbooks above and below a threshold. It is the $L_2$ analogue of Kaufman's efficiency ratio, and it is dimensionless, so a single threshold transfers across instruments and price levels without recalibration.
  - **Strong:** the statistic demonstrably isolates a coherent, homogeneous population of moves — the 163 selected events produce averaged curves with a clean 40–50-minute accumulation ramp and a peak the paper describes as **sharper** than the corresponding NYSE/NASDAQ result, despite pooling 23 stocks across a full year (Sec. 3, Fig. 1; Sec. 6 on the 40–50-minute ramp) *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** the statistic has an exact null distribution and a clean interpretation. Under iid zero-drift returns with per-minute variance $\sigma^2$, $\mathbb{E}[R^2]=n\sigma^2$ and $\mathbb{E}[v^2]=n\sigma^2$, so $\mathbb{E}[\eta^2]\approx1$ — the statistic is **centred on 1 for pure noise regardless of $n$, $\sigma$ or price level**. Under a drift $\mu$ per minute plus noise $\sigma$, $R=n\mu$ and $v\approx\sqrt{n(\mu^2+\sigma^2)}$, giving $\eta=\sqrt{n}\,\mu/\sqrt{\mu^2+\sigma^2}$ — a per-window $t$-statistic *(From AI)*
  - **Strong:** the threshold has a calculable rarity, which is what makes it usable as an alarm. Setting $\eta\ge6$ with $n=60$ requires $\mu/\sqrt{\mu^2+\sigma^2}\ge 6/\sqrt{60}=0.7746$, i.e. $\mu^2\ge1.5\sigma^2$, i.e. a **per-minute signal-to-noise ratio $|\mu|/\sigma\ge1.22$ sustained for an hour**. That predicts extreme rarity, and the data agree: with 23 stocks × ~240 days × ~175 eligible minutes $\approx$ **966,000** candidate windows, the 5450 raw triggers are **0.56%** of windows, collapsing to 163 distinct episodes after de-duplication *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** by Cauchy–Schwarz $\eta\le\sqrt{n}$, so requiring $\eta\ge6$ forces $n\ge\mathbf{36}$ contributing minutes. The filter therefore **structurally cannot fire on a fast jump**: a move completed in a single minute has $\eta=1$, and one completed in 5 equal minutes has $\eta=\sqrt5=2.24$, both far below 6. Anything trained on this event set will be blind to flash-crash-type events, and the paper's "slow accumulation before the peak" finding is **partly a selection artifact of its own filter** *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** the threshold is not window-size invariant, which corrupts the window-shrinking step. Since $\eta$ scales as $\sqrt{n_{\rm eff}}$, comparing a 36-minute and a 60-minute window against the *same* constant 6 systematically favours the longer window, so the shrink loop of Algorithm A step 6 stops for reasons driven by $\sqrt{n}$ arithmetic rather than by market structure — and the resulting $t=0$ alignment inherits that bias *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** the paper itself documents that each filter leg is individually defective — the absolute leg "just finds usual trades" because of the intraday volatility pattern and cannot compare volatile with quiet stocks, while the relative leg "maybe only finds out the events happened in the period of low average volatility, but actual price changes are very small" (Sec. 3). Neither leg is safe to deploy alone *(From paper)*

### Models & Algorithms

| Model/Algorithm | Role |
|---|---|
| **Combined absolute + relative event filter** | Event definition: $\lvert R_{\Delta t}\rvert\ge4\%$ over $\Delta t=60$ min **and** $\lvert R_{\Delta t}\rvert\ge6\,v(t,\Delta t)$, following the design of Zawadowski et al. and Tóth et al. |
| **Window-shrinking event localization** | Reduces $\Delta t$ while both filters still pass, setting $t=0$ at the end of the smallest qualifying window |
| **Realized volatility as sum of squared returns** (Eq. 1) | $v(t,\Delta t)=\sqrt{\sum_{\tau=t-\Delta t}^{t}r^2(\tau)}$ — the scale for the relative filter, taken from the realized-volatility literature (Bollen & Inder; Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold and co-authors) |
| **Intraday-pattern (seasonality) removal** (Eq. 2) | $x=X/P(t')$, applied to every quantity individually; sets the normal level to 1 and licenses cross-day/cross-stock pooling and the next-day continuation of event windows |
| **Equal-weight event-aligned averaging** (Eq. 3) | $x_K(t)=\frac1{\lVert K\rVert}\sum_k x_k(t)$ over $t=-100,\dots,200$ |
| **Market-order volume imbalance** (Eq. 4) | $I=V_b/(V_b+V_s)$ on executed volume only, following Plerou, Gopikrishnan, Gabaix & Stanley; deliberately narrower than the Tóth et al. definition |
| **Excess-variable construction** (Eq. 5) | $x_{\rm ex}(t)=x(t)-1$, the object that is fitted |
| **Power-law (Omori-type) relaxation model** (Eq. 6) | $x_{\rm ex}(t)\sim t^{-\alpha}$, the functional form applied uniformly to all quantities; conceptual lineage from Lillo & Mantegna's Omori-law analogy |
| **Linear least-squares regression of $\ln x_{\rm ex}$ on $\ln t$** | The estimator for every reported $\alpha$ and its standard error, over fit ranges $[1,300]$, $[2,300]$ or $[4,300]$ minutes |
| **Effective market / effective limit order classification** | Reclassifies 2003 SZSE limit orders as marketable or passive by comparing the limit price to the opposite best quote (Smith, Farmer, Gillemot & Krishnamurthy) |
| **Order-splitting rule for partial fills** | A limit order that exceeds the available opposite-side depth is recorded as **two** orders — a partially filled order plus a residual effective limit order |
| **Four-class order-aggressiveness taxonomy** | PFO / FO / LO / CO per side, in the spirit of Biais, Hillion & Spatt; the PFO/FO split is justified by their different price-impact behaviour (Zhou 2007) |
| **Relative order rates** | $\rho_j=N_j/(N_{\rm MO}+N_{\rm LO}+N_{\rm CO})$ by investor class and by side, plus the marketable share $\phi=N_{\rm MO}/(N_{\rm MO}+N_{\rm LO})$; methodology adopted from Tóth et al. |
| **Dual volume/count aggregation** | Computing both $V(t)$ and $N(t)$ per cell, which is what surfaces the $t_{\max}$ vs $t'_{\max}$ divergence and the implied mean-order-size dynamics |
