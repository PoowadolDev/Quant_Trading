## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation and the gap — option flow split into "positive" and "negative" signals, not just total volume.** Equity options are attractive to informed traders because of **high leverage**, the **absence of the uptick rule**, and the **limited downside of a long put** versus a short futures position; the global notional outstanding for equity-linked options was **USD 7 tn** out of USD 582 tn of all derivatives in H2 2020 (BIS), against a world GDP of **USD 88 tn**. Prior work is split: [Anthony, 1988] found call volume leads stock volume, [Stephan and Whaley, 1990] found the stock market leads options by **at least 15 minutes**, and [Easley et al., 1998] reconciled the two by showing the conclusion flips once volumes are **aggregated into directional buckets** rather than used raw. The paper adopts exactly that decomposition: a trader expecting a price *rise* buys calls or sells puts; a trader expecting a *fall* sells calls or buys puts. Formally, for asset $i$, contract $j$, day $d$, MPC $m$ and filter $F$:
   $$V^{\text{Up},F}_{i,j,d,m}=V^{\text{Call Buy},F}_{i,j,d,m}+V^{\text{Put Sell},F}_{i,j,d,m},\qquad V^{\text{Down},F}_{i,j,d,m}=V^{\text{Call Sell},F}_{i,j,d,m}+V^{\text{Put Buy},F}_{i,j,d,m}$$
   $$T^{\text{Up},F}_{i,j,d,m}=T^{\text{Call Buy},F}_{i,j,d,m}+T^{\text{Put Sell},F}_{i,j,d,m},\qquad T^{\text{Down},F}_{i,j,d,m}=T^{\text{Call Sell},F}_{i,j,d,m}+T^{\text{Put Buy},F}_{i,j,d,m}$$
   where $V^{\cdot}$ are contract volumes and $T^{\cdot}$ are transaction counts (a count regardless of size).
   *Worked example.* One contract, one day, one MPC: 800 contracts of call buys and 200 of put sells give $V^{\text{Up}}=800+200=\mathbf{1000}$; 300 call sells and 100 put buys give $V^{\text{Down}}=300+100=\mathbf{400}$. If those came from 24 call-buy trades and 16 put-sell trades against 30 call-sell and 30 put-buy trades, then $T^{\text{Up}}=24+16=\mathbf{40}$ and $T^{\text{Down}}=30+30=\mathbf{60}$ — note the volume view is bullish while the trade-count view is bearish, which is exactly why the paper carries three separate OVI variants (point 2).

2. **The Option Volume Imbalance (OVI) — the paper's central metric.** OVI aggregates the directional volumes across *all* contracts of an asset and normalises by total flow:
   $$\text{OVI}^{[F]}_{i,d,m}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}\left(X^{\text{Up},F}_{i,j,d,m}-X^{\text{Down},F}_{i,j,d,m}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}\left(X^{\text{Up},F}_{i,j,d,m}+X^{\text{Down},F}_{i,j,d,m}\right)}\tag{1}$$
   where $X$ is a generic flow variable, and $0/0$ is *defined* as $0$ (so an asset with no option flow gets OVI $=0$). Three instantiations are used: **volume-based** OVI ($X=V$), **trade-based** OVI ($X=T$), and **nominal-volume-based** OVI ($X^{\text{Up}}_{i,j,d,m}=P^{\text{Option}}_{i,j,d}V^{\text{Up}}_{i,j,d,m}$, likewise for Down), the last of which deliberately stops cheap deep-OTM contracts from dominating. The normalisation forces $\text{OVI}\in[-1,1]$, so the metric is **comparable across assets** regardless of liquidity or market cap. Each transaction is **double-counted** — a Customer buying a call from a Market Maker is logged both as a Customer-Buy and a Market-Maker-Sell — which is intentional: it lets the same trade be read from both sides. The sign convention is arbitrary and OVI can be positively *or* negatively correlated with future returns depending on the MPC. Unlike the put/call ratio of [Pan and Poteshman, 2006] (restricted to Open-Buy transactions), OVI uses *all* transaction types and makes **no linearity assumption**: only $\text{sign}(\text{OVI})$ is used as the directional prediction.
   *Worked example.* Asset $i$ has two contracts. Contract 1: $V^{\text{Up}}=1000$, $V^{\text{Down}}=400$, average option price $P^{\text{Option}}_1=\$2.50$. Contract 2: $V^{\text{Up}}=150$, $V^{\text{Down}}=450$, $P^{\text{Option}}_2=\$0.40$. Volume-based: numerator $=(1000-400)+(150-450)=600-300=300$; denominator $=1400+600=2000$; so $\text{OVI}=300/2000=\mathbf{+0.15}$. Nominal-volume-based: Up $=2.50\times1000+0.40\times150=2500+60=2560$; Down $=2.50\times400+0.40\times450=1000+180=1180$; $\text{OVI}^{\text{nom}}=(2560-1180)/(2560+1180)=1380/3740=\mathbf{+0.369}$ — the bearish cheap contract is de-weighted, more than doubling the bullish reading. Trade-based, using $T^{\text{Up}}=40$, $T^{\text{Down}}=60$ from point 1: $\text{OVI}^{T}=(40-60)/100=\mathbf{-0.20}$, the opposite sign.

3. **Data: two NASDAQ option data sets, five market participant classes, 10-minute granularity.** The study uses **NOTO** (Nasdaq Option Market / NOM exchange) and **PHOTO** (Nasdaq PHLX exchange) for **02 Jan 2015 – 31 Dec 2019**. Each day carries **39 intraday cumulative volume reports** at 10-minute intervals (first at 9:40 ET, last at 16:00 ET) plus a daily summary with low/high/open/close prices, **open interest**, electronic volume and total exchange volume. Volumes are broken into five MPCs — **Firm (proprietary trades), Brokers, Market Makers, Customers, Professional Customers** — and, for every MPC *except* Market Makers, into **Opening Buy, Opening Sell, Closing Buy, Closing Sell**. Descriptive findings: calls have higher median volume than puts; **Market Makers and Customers dominate** while Firms and Professional Customers have very small median volumes (so their OVI is noisier); Firms have by far the highest **nominal value per trade** on PHOTO. Correlations: PHOTO-vs-NOTO total volume per asset ranges **0.00–0.93**; total volume across MPCs ranges **0.8–1.0**; Customers' OVI correlates about **−0.6** with Market Makers and Brokers and about **−0.20** with Firms (Spearman, median across days, per-cell standard deviation ≤ 0.05), so the OVI feature set is **strongly multicollinear** — the reason a regularisation term is later needed. PCA on PHOTO shows total volume has very low intrinsic dimensionality (**PC1 explains 59%**, 12 components exceed 90%), whereas **every OVI needs more than 100 components to reach 50%** of variance (Professional Customers 107, Customers 211) — evidence that OVI carries far more asset-specific information than raw volume. Because full transaction records are unavailable, inter-MPC flow is *approximated* from the 10-minute reports by treating buy and sell volumes as the two sides of a bipartite graph (Chung–Lu style, [Benson et al., 2020]), assuming flow is proportional to the product of buyer and seller volume:
   $$NF_{d,m_1,m_2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}\sum_{t=1}^{39}P_{i,j,d}\,V^{\text{Buy},t}_{i,j,d,m_1}\times\frac{V^{\text{Sell},t}_{i,j,d,m_2}}{\sum_{m'}V^{\text{Sell},t}_{i,j,d,m'}}\tag{19}$$
   $$\widetilde{NF}_{m_1,m_2}=\text{median}\left(\frac{NF_{d,m_1,m_2}}{\sum_{m'_1,m'_2}NF_{d,m'_1,m'_2}};\ d=1,\dots,D\right)\tag{20}$$
   where $V^{\text{Buy},t}_{i,j,d,m}$ / $V^{\text{Sell},t}_{i,j,d,m}$ are volumes bought/sold in 10-minute window $t\in\{1,\dots,39\}$ by MPC $m$, and $P_{i,j,d}$ is the contract price. Findings: call and put flow structures are near-identical; whenever $\widetilde{NF}_{m_1,m_2}$ is high the reverse flow is high too; a very large share of flow involves a Customer and/or a Market Maker; Firms and Professional Customers trade mostly with Market Makers; Brokers trade more with Customers than with Market Makers (much more so on PHOTO).
   *Worked example of (19)–(20).* One contract, one 10-minute window, $P=\$2.50$. Buys: Market Maker 300, Customer 700. Sells: Market Maker 600, Broker 400, so $\sum_{m'}V^{\text{Sell}}=1000$. Then $NF_{\text{MMbuy},\text{MMsell}}=2.50\times300\times600/1000=\mathbf{450}$; $NF_{\text{MMbuy},\text{Brokersell}}=2.50\times300\times400/1000=\mathbf{300}$; $NF_{\text{Custbuy},\text{MMsell}}=2.50\times700\times600/1000=\mathbf{1050}$; $NF_{\text{Custbuy},\text{Brokersell}}=2.50\times700\times400/1000=\mathbf{700}$. Total $=2500=P\times\sum V^{\text{Buy}}$, as it must be. Normalising by (20): $\mathbf{0.18},\mathbf{0.12},\mathbf{0.42},\mathbf{0.28}$, summing to 1.

4. **Return definitions and the market-excess adjustment.** Three return types are defined (all adjusted for splits and dividends):
   $$f^{CL\_tmCL}_{i,d}=\frac{P^{\text{close}}_{i,d+1}-P^{\text{close}}_{i,d}}{P^{\text{close}}_{i,d}},\qquad
   f^{tmOP\_tmCL}_{i,d}=\frac{P^{\text{close}}_{i,d+1}-P^{\text{open}}_{i,d+1}}{P^{\text{open}}_{i,d+1}},\qquad
   f^{CL\_tmOP}_{i,d}=\frac{P^{\text{open}}_{i,d+1}-P^{\text{close}}_{i,d}}{P^{\text{close}}_{i,d}}$$
   i.e. **close-to-close**, next-day **open-to-close** (intraday) and **overnight** returns. Unless stated otherwise the paper uses the **overnight** $CL\_tmOP$ return and drops the superscript. The **excess market return** (EMR) is
   $$\tilde f_{i,d}=f_{i,d}-f_{\text{SPY},d}$$
   where $f_{\text{SPY},d}$ is the *future* return of the S&P-500 (via the SPY ETF), making the study market-neutral; raw returns are labelled **RR**. A footnote states that using **Fama–French 3-factor** risk-adjusted returns does not significantly change the results.
   *Worked example.* $P^{\text{close}}_{i,d}=100.00$, $P^{\text{open}}_{i,d+1}=100.80$, $P^{\text{close}}_{i,d+1}=100.20$. Then $f^{CL\_tmOP}=(100.80-100.00)/100.00=\mathbf{0.0080}$ (80 bp overnight); $f^{CL\_tmCL}=(100.20-100.00)/100.00=\mathbf{0.0020}$; $f^{tmOP\_tmCL}=(100.20-100.80)/100.80=\mathbf{-0.005952}$ (the gap partly gives back intraday). If SPY gapped $f_{\text{SPY},d}=0.0030$, the overnight EMR is $\tilde f_{i,d}=0.0080-0.0030=\mathbf{0.0050}$.

5. **Evaluation metrics: P&L, Sharpe Ratio, Profit-Per-Dollar, and a non-normality-robust SR test.** Only the *sign* of the signal is traded ($+$ = buy, $-$ = sell), sized by a bet $b_{i,d}$:
   $$\text{P\&L}_d=\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}b_{i,d}\times\tilde f_{i,d}\times\text{sign}(s_{i,d})\tag{2}$$
   where $s_{i,d}\in\mathbb{R}$ is the signal (a single OVI, or the linear combination of point 7). The paper is explicit that (2) is **not necessarily executable** (no transaction costs, possible liquidity/timing constraints) — it is a *measure of predictability*, not a live return. Over the horizon this gives a vector $\text{P\&L}=(\text{P\&L}_1,\dots,\text{P\&L}_{D-1})$, summarised by the **annualised Sharpe Ratio** [Sharpe, 1994] and the **Profit Per Dollar**:
   $$SR=\frac{\text{mean}(\text{P\&L})\times\sqrt{252}}{\text{sd}(\text{P\&L})}\tag{3}\qquad
   PPD=\frac{\sum_{d=1}^{D-1}\text{P\&L}_d}{\sum_{d=1}^{D-1}B_d},\qquad B_d=\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}b_{i,d}\tag{4}$$
   Significance of a strategy is tested against $H_0: SR=0$ following [Bailey and Lopez de Prado, 2014], with the Sharpe-Ratio variance estimate of [Bao, 2008] that corrects for non-normal returns:
   $$\text{stat}=\frac{SR(\text{P\&L})}{\sqrt{\dfrac{1-\text{skewness}(\text{P\&L})\cdot SR(\text{P\&L})+\left(\text{kurtosis}(\text{P\&L})-1\right)\cdot SR(\text{P\&L})^{2}/4}{T-1}}}$$
   ($T$ = number of P&L observations; the PDF renders $SR^2$ as "$SR*2$"). To compare two strategies ($H_0: SR_1=SR_2$) the paper uses the [Ledoit and Wolf, 2008] **studentised bootstrap** test on the residuals of a VAR model with a circular block scheme.
   *Worked example of (2).* Four assets, uniform bets $b=0.25$ each; signals $+0.15,-0.30,+0.05,-0.10$ (signs $+,-,+,-$); overnight EMRs $+0.005,-0.004,-0.002,+0.006$. Then $\text{P\&L}_d=0.25\times\big[(0.005)(+1)+(-0.004)(-1)+(-0.002)(+1)+(0.006)(-1)\big]=0.25\times(0.005+0.004-0.002-0.006)=0.25\times0.001=\mathbf{0.00025}=\mathbf{2.5\ \text{bp}}$.
   *Worked example of (3)–(4) reproducing the paper's headline cell.* Because every scheme normalises to $B_d=1$ per day, $PPD$ is just average daily P&L. For the **Q3 Market Maker** strategy the paper reports $PPD=-1.9$ bp over $D-1\approx1257$ days, which implies a cumulative P&L of $1257\times(-1.9\times10^{-4})=\mathbf{-0.239}$, i.e. **−23.9%** — matching Figure 6's plotted curve. Inverting (3) with the reported $SR=-4.42$: $\text{sd}=\text{mean}\times\sqrt{252}/SR=(-1.9\times10^{-4})(15.8745)/(-4.42)=\mathbf{6.82\times10^{-4}}$ daily. *Worked example of the SR test.* Take the per-day (non-annualised) $SR=-4.42/\sqrt{252}=-0.2784$, $T=1257$, skewness $=0.2$, kurtosis $=8$: numerator of the variance $=1-0.2(-0.2784)+(8-1)(0.2784)^2/4=1+0.0557+0.1356=1.1913$; divided by $T-1=1256$ gives $9.485\times10^{-4}$, so the standard error is $0.0308$ and $\text{stat}=-0.2784/0.0308=\mathbf{-9.04}$ — a two-sided $p$ of order $10^{-19}$, consistent with the paper's reported $p$-value of **0.00**.

6. **Signal-magnitude portfolios and six bet-sizing schemes.** Because (2) throws away the magnitude of OVI, assets are ranked each day on $|s_{i,d}|$ into five quintiles **QB1** (weakest 20%) to **QB5** (strongest 20%), and then into *cumulative* **quantile-ranked (QR) groups** $Q1=QB1\cup\dots\cup QB5$ (all signals), $Q2=QB1\cup\dots\cup QB4$, …, $Q5=QB5$ only — so $Qk$ is the top $20k\%$ of signals by magnitude. Independently, the bet size is built from a raw size $b'_{i,d}$ and normalised so that exactly **one dollar per day** is deployed, making cumulative P&L read as a cumulative percentage return:
   $$b_{i,d}=\frac{b'_{i,d}}{\sum_j b'_{j,d}}$$
   with the schemes: **Uniform** $b'_{i,d}=1$ for all $i,d$ with $s_{i,d}\neq0$; **Imbalance** $b'_{i,d}=|s_{i,d}|$ (equivalent to trading the signal itself rather than its sign); **Volume** $b'_{i,d}=\log\!\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}V_{i,j,d,m}\right)$ and **Nominal Volume** $b'_{i,t}=\log\!\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}P_{i,j,d}V_{i,j,d,m}\right)$ (volume as a liquidity proxy, log-transformed to soften cross-contract volume differences); **Relative Volume** $b'_{i,d}=\log\!\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}\frac{V_{i,j,d,m}}{OI_{i,j,d}}\right)$ with $OI_{i,j,d}$ the open interest, which prevents small-volume-but-liquid names from being penalised; and, once implied volatility is identified as the strongest conditioning feature, the **IV-Volume** scheme
   $$b'_{i,d}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}\sum_m \log\left(1+\min\{\sigma_{i,j,d},2\}\right)V_{i,j,d,m}$$
   where $\sigma_{i,j,d}$ is the implied volatility of contract $j$, capped at 2. **Results:** Uniform is the default and is *not* beaten on Sharpe by any volume-based scheme — Volume, Nominal Volume and Relative Volume are all significantly outperformed by Uniform for Market Makers (e.g. Volume-vs-Uniform difference $-0.51^*$ at Q3, Nominal Volume $-0.85^{**}$, Relative Volume $-0.52^*$), because volume weighting injects extra variance. The **Imbalance** scheme is never significantly different from Uniform after multiple-testing correction, but is slightly *higher* in SR for all QR groups of Brokers and Customers and for Q2/Q4/Q5 of Market Makers, so the paper recommends it as the better way to embed OVI magnitude. **IV-Volume** gives the best PPD of all schemes (Q5 $PPD=-3.9$ bp versus $-1.7$ bp for Uniform) but a lower SR ($-2.95$ vs $-3.66$) — more profitable, less consistent. Raw (un-logged) volume, sqrt-volume and underlying-liquidity weightings also fail to beat Uniform.
   *Worked example.* Three assets with signals $|s|=0.15,0.30,0.05$; MPC volumes $50{,}000$, $5{,}000$, $500$; open interest $200{,}000$, $2{,}000$, $100$; implied vols $0.25$, $0.90$, $2.40$. **Uniform:** $b=1/3,1/3,1/3$. **Imbalance:** $\sum|s|=0.50\Rightarrow b=\mathbf{0.30},\mathbf{0.60},\mathbf{0.10}$. **Volume:** $\ln(50001)=10.8198$, $\ln(5001)=8.5174$, $\ln(501)=6.2166$, sum $25.5538\Rightarrow b=\mathbf{0.4234},\mathbf{0.3333},\mathbf{0.2433}$ — the log compresses a 100× volume span into a 1.7× bet span. **Relative Volume:** $V/OI=0.25,2.5,5.0$, so $\ln(1.25)=0.2231$, $\ln(3.5)=1.2528$, $\ln(6)=1.7918$, sum $3.2677\Rightarrow b=\mathbf{0.0683},\mathbf{0.3833},\mathbf{0.5484}$ — the ranking *inverts* relative to the Volume scheme. **IV-Volume** (one contract each, third asset's $\sigma$ capped at 2): $\ln(1.25)\times50000=11{,}157$; $\ln(1.9)\times5000=3{,}209$; $\ln(3)\times500=549$; sum $14{,}915\Rightarrow b=\mathbf{0.748},\mathbf{0.215},\mathbf{0.037}$.

7. **P&L regression — a novel objective that maximises cumulative P&L directly.** OLS/LASSO with $t$-, $p$- or $R^2$-based comparison is argued to be the wrong tool here for three reasons: (a) the Euclidean loss is misaligned with *directional* prediction, so a predictor that gets every sign right can still have tiny $R^2$ (the paper confirms OLS finds all OVIs significant but with very low $R^2$ and indistinguishable explanatory power); (b) OVI–return relations need not be linear; (c) OVI is **zero on many days** (sparse) and linear methods cannot ignore zero-valued features. With $K$ OVI variants $A_{i,d,k}$, the signal is
   $$s_{i,d}(\beta;A)=\beta_0+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_k A_{i,d,k}\tag{5}$$
   and the quantity to maximise is total P&L
   $$PL(\beta;A,f)=\sum_{d=1}^{D-1}\text{P\&L}_d=\sum_{d=1}^{D-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i,d}(\beta;A)\,f_{i,d}\,\text{sign}\!\left(s_{i,d}(\beta;A)\right)\right)\tag{6}$$
   Gradient methods need smoothness, so $\text{sign}$ is replaced by a scaled logistic activation and the bet size by its smoothed uniform analogue:
   $$g(x;\alpha_1)=2\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha_1 x}}-\frac{1}{2}\right),\ \alpha_1>0\tag{7}\qquad
   b_{i,d}(\beta;A)=\frac{\left|g\!\left(s_{i,d}(\beta;A)\right)\right|}{\sum_j\left|g\!\left(s_{j,d}(\beta;A)\right)\right|}\tag{8}$$
   ($g$ equals $\tanh$ when $\alpha_1=2$ and converges pointwise to $\text{sign}$ as $\alpha_1\to\infty$), giving
   $$PL(\beta;A,f)=\sum_{d=1}^{D-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sum_j\left|g\!\left(s_{j,d}(\beta;A)\right)\right|}f_{i,d}\,g\!\left(s_{i,d}(\beta;A)\right)\right)\tag{9}$$
   The remaining non-differentiability (the absolute values in (8) and in the $\ell_1$ penalty) is removed with a smooth proxy
   $$h(x;\alpha_2)=\sqrt{x^2+\alpha_2},\ \alpha_2>0\tag{10}$$
   yielding the **objective to be minimised** (LASSO-style [Tibshirani, 1996] penalty with strength $\lambda$):
   $$L(\beta;A,f,q,\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\lambda)=-\sum_{d=1}^{D-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sum_j h\!\left(g\!\left(s_{j,d}(\beta;A)\right)\right)}f_{i,d}\,g\!\left(s_{i,d}(\beta;A)\right)\right)+\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(\beta_k;\alpha_2)\tag{11}$$
   minimised with **ADAM** [Kingma and Ba, 2014] (moment parameters at the recommended 0.9 and 0.999), using the analytic gradient
   $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\beta_k}=-\sum_{d=1}^{D-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i,d}\left(\frac{1}{\sum_j h\!\left(g(s_{j,d})\right)}\frac{\partial g(s_{i,d}(\beta))}{\partial\beta_k}-\frac{g(s_{i,d}(\beta))}{\left(\sum_j h\!\left(g(s_{j,d})\right)\right)^2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}h'\!\left(g(s_{j,d}(\beta))\right)\frac{\partial g(s_{j,d}(\beta))}{\partial\beta_k}\right)+\lambda\left(1-\mathbb{1}(k=0)\right)h'(\beta_k)$$
   $$\frac{\partial g(s_{i,d}(\beta))}{\partial\beta_k}=g'\!\left(\beta_0+\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{i,d,k}\beta_k\right)A_{i,d,k}\tag{12}$$
   and finally the fitted vector is rescaled so the non-intercept coefficients have unit $\ell_1$ norm, $\tilde\beta=\hat\beta/\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\hat\beta_k|$ (exactly neutral under $\text{sign}$, very slightly non-neutral under the $\tanh$ proxy). Stated trade-off: no closed form, non-convex, and $\lambda$ must be chosen; in exchange, $|\beta_k|$ reads directly as a buy/sell/neutral weighting factor. Training uses a **sliding window** of $l=500$ trading days (~2 years) with a **$T=100$-day out-of-sample test window**; the paper states results are insensitive to $l$ and $T$. Hyperparameters reported in the figures: $\eta=0.01$, $\lambda=0.01$, $\alpha_1=10$, $\alpha_2=10^{-5}$, step size 50.
   *Worked example (one day, $K=2$, $\alpha_1=10$, $\alpha_2=10^{-5}$, $\lambda=0.01$).* Take $\beta=(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0,0.6,-0.4)$ and three assets with features $A_1=(0.20,0.10)$, $A_2=(-0.30,0.20)$, $A_3=(0.05,-0.50)$. Signals from (5): $s_1=0.6(0.20)-0.4(0.10)=\mathbf{0.08}$; $s_2=0.6(-0.30)-0.4(0.20)=\mathbf{-0.26}$; $s_3=0.6(0.05)-0.4(-0.50)=\mathbf{0.23}$. Activations from (7), noting $g(x;10)=\tanh(5x)$: $g_1=\tanh(0.40)=\mathbf{0.37995}$, $g_2=\tanh(-1.30)=\mathbf{-0.86172}$, $g_3=\tanh(1.15)=\mathbf{0.81775}$. Smoothed magnitudes from (10): $h(g_1)=\sqrt{0.144362+10^{-5}}=0.379963$, $h(g_2)=0.861726$, $h(g_3)=0.817756$, so $\sum_j h=\mathbf{2.059445}$ (essentially $\sum|g|$). With returns $f=(0.005,-0.004,0.006)$, the day's contribution to (9) is $\frac{1}{2.059445}\left[0.005(0.37995)+(-0.004)(-0.86172)+0.006(0.81775)\right]=\frac{0.0102531}{2.059445}=\mathbf{0.004979}$ (≈ 49.8 bp). Penalty: $h(0.6)=0.600008$, $h(-0.4)=0.400013$, so $\lambda\sum h(\beta_k)=0.01(1.000021)=\mathbf{0.0100002}$, giving $L=-0.004979+0.010000=\mathbf{+0.005021}$. Gradient w.r.t. $\beta_1$ using $g'(x)=\tfrac{\alpha_1}{2}\left(1-g(x)^2\right)$: $g'(s_1)=5(1-0.14436)=4.27819$, $g'(s_2)=5(1-0.74256)=1.28720$, $g'(s_3)=5(1-0.66872)=1.65643$; via (12), $\partial g_i/\partial\beta_1=g'(s_i)A_{i,1}=0.855638,\ -0.386159,\ 0.082821$. First bracket term $=\frac{1}{2.059445}\left[0.005(0.855638)+(-0.004)(-0.386159)+0.006(0.082821)\right]=0.0063198/2.059445=0.0030687$. Second: $\sum_j h'(g_j)\partial g_j/\partial\beta_1$ with $h'(x)=x/\sqrt{x^2+\alpha_2}\approx\text{sign}(x)$ gives $0.855609+0.386156+0.082820=1.324585$, and $\sum_i f_i g_i=0.0102531$, $(\sum h)^{-2}=0.235777$, so the term is $0.0102531\times0.235777\times1.324585=0.0032021$. Hence $\partial L/\partial\beta_1=-(0.0030687-0.0032021)+0.01(0.999986)=+0.00013+0.01000=\mathbf{+0.010133}$. ADAM's first step is $\approx-\eta\,\text{sign}(\text{grad})$, so $\beta_1\leftarrow0.6-0.01=\mathbf{0.59}$. If the run converged at $\hat\beta=(0.02,0.75,-0.45)$, rescaling by $\sum_{k\ge1}|\hat\beta_k|=1.20$ gives $\tilde\beta=(\mathbf{0.0167},\mathbf{0.625},\mathbf{-0.375})$, with non-intercept $\ell_1$ norm exactly 1.

8. **Main empirical results — Market Makers dominate, three of five MPCs are significant, mid-strength quantiles win.** Using $s_{i,d}=\text{OVI}_{i,d,m}$ on PHOTO against overnight excess-market returns (EMR $CL\_tmOP$), the paper reports for the five QR groups: **Market Makers** $SR$ between **−3.55 and −4.42** ($PPD$ −1.4 to −1.9 bp, cumulative 17–25%, described elsewhere as ~30% over five years); **Customers** $SR$ **+2.66 to +3.46** ($PPD$ +1.2 to +1.5 bp); **Brokers** $SR$ **−2.13 to −2.54**; **Professional Customers** $SR$ **+0.98 to +1.44**; **Firms** $SR$ **−0.24 to +0.24**, with no clear trend. Significance tests on the cumulative QR groups reject $H_0:SR=0$ with **$p=0.00$ (2 dp)** for Brokers, Market Makers and Customers at every quantile rank, while Firms and Professional Customers sit near $p\approx0.34$–$0.46$ — and those two are exactly the lowest-volume MPCs. On the *distinct* QB buckets, **QB1 is almost uninformative** while QB2–QB5 are the significant ones for Market Makers and Customers, confirming that larger $|\text{OVI}|$ means stronger predictability. Across QR groups, **Q3 is the best performer** (both PPD and SR) for all MPCs except Firms; Q1 (all signals) is worst on PPD and either Q1 or Q5 is worst on SR — so taking *everything* or only the *extreme tail* both lose to the top 60–80%. Across return modes, **overnight ($CL\_tmOP$) is clearly the most predictable**, then close-to-close, then open-to-close (Market Maker $SR$: $-4.42$, $-3.08$, $-1.16$ at Q3 for EMR); EMR beats RR. Across OVI types, **trade-based OVI is the weakest** (significantly worse for Brokers, Market Makers, Customers) and **volume-based OVI has the highest-magnitude SR** for Market Makers and Brokers. Two combined signals are then fitted with the P&L regression:
   $$s^1_{i,d}=\text{OVI}^{\text{PHOTO}}_{i,d,\text{M.Maker}}\tag{13}$$
   $$s^2_{i,d}(\beta)=\beta_0+\beta_1\text{OVI}^{\text{PHOTO}}_{i,d,\text{Firm}}+\beta_2\text{OVI}^{\text{PHOTO}}_{i,d,\text{Broker}}+\beta_3\text{OVI}^{\text{PHOTO}}_{i,d,\text{MarketMaker}}+\beta_4\text{OVI}^{\text{PHOTO}}_{i,d,\text{Customer}}+\beta_5\text{OVI}^{\text{PHOTO}}_{i,d,\text{P.Customer}}\tag{14}$$
   The Market Maker coefficient **dominates at every window with magnitude 0.3–0.75**, rising in later years as Broker and Customer coefficients shrink; Professional Customers keep a consistently negative and relatively large coefficient (harmless because their OVI is mostly zero, and useful because it is uncorrelated with the rest). Crucially, $s^2$ **overfits**: in-sample mean PPD is 1.57 bp vs 1.50 bp for $s^1$, but out-of-sample $s^1$ wins with **1.33 bp vs 1.25 bp**. A broader 45-feature model (5 MPCs × {Buy, Sell, Both} × {NOTO, PHOTO, Both}) does better:
   $$M3:\ s^4_{i,d}=g\left(\beta_0+\sum_{M\in\text{MPCs}}\ \sum_{S\in\{\text{Buy},\text{Sell},\text{Both}\}}\ \sum_{\mathcal{D}\in\{\text{NOTO},\text{PHOTO},\text{Both}\}}\beta_{M,S,\mathcal{D}}\,\text{OVI}^{S,\mathcal{D}}_{i,d,M}\right)\tag{18}$$
   raising PPD to **2.48 bp in-sample and 1.68 bp out-of-sample**. Two supplementary results round out the picture. First, contemporaneous versus predictive explanatory power is compared by fitting
   $$\tilde f^{CL\_tmCL}_{i,d-1}=\alpha+\beta_{i,d}\text{OVI}_{i,d,m}+\epsilon_{i,d}\tag{21}\qquad
   \tilde f^{CL\_tmCL}_{i,d}=\alpha+\beta_{i,d}\text{OVI}_{i,d,m}+\epsilon_{i,d}\tag{22}$$
   in a 200-day sliding window stepped every 50 days on non-zero OVIs: OVI is significant for **all** MPCs in both directions, but contemporaneous $p$-values are far smaller and $R^2$ far larger — e.g. Market Maker $R^2=\mathbf{5.55\times10^{-3}}$ ($p=0$) contemporaneously versus $\mathbf{1.65\times10^{-4}}$ ($p=8.45\times10^{-5}$) for future overnight returns, a **34× gap**. Second, hit-rate is measured as $\max\{\pi,1-\pi\}$ where $\pi$ is the fraction of positive daily P&Ls: Firms and Professional Customers sit near **0.50** (uninformative), Customers and Brokers reach **0.55–0.60**, and Market Makers exceed **0.60**.
   *Worked example tying $R^2$ to the reported $p$-value.* $R^2=1.65\times10^{-4}$ implies $|r|=0.01285$. With a 200-day window over roughly 500 non-zero-OVI assets ($n\approx10^5$ observations), $t\approx r\sqrt{n-2}=0.01285\times316=\mathbf{4.06}$, giving $p\approx5\times10^{-5}$ — the same order as the paper's $8.45\times10^{-5}$, so a *vanishing* $R^2$ can still be overwhelmingly significant, which is precisely the paper's argument for abandoning $R^2$ as the yardstick. *Worked example of the hit-rate metric.* If $\pi=0.39$ then the reported accuracy is $\max\{0.39,0.61\}=\mathbf{0.61}$; against a fair coin over $T=1257$ days the standard error is $\sqrt{0.25/1257}=0.0141$, so $z=(0.61-0.50)/0.0141=\mathbf{7.80}$, $p\approx3\times10^{-15}$.

9. **Which options carry the information: implied volatility and puts, not maturity.** Options are bucketed each day into four quartiles of a chosen feature, and the whole OVI pipeline is re-run per bucket, for Brokers, Market Makers and Customers only (the three significant MPCs), with pairwise Ledoit–Wolf SR difference tests at the 5% level. Features come from a **Black–Scholes model for European options without dividends** [Black and Scholes, 1973]:
   $$P^{\text{Call}}(S,K,\tau,r,\sigma)=Se^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_1)-Ke^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2)\tag{15}$$
   $$P^{\text{Put}}(S,K,\tau,r,\sigma)=Ke^{-r\tau}\Phi(-d_2)-Se^{-r\tau}\Phi(-d_1)\tag{16}$$
   $$d_1(S,K,\tau,r,\sigma)=\frac{\log(S/K)+\left(r+\sigma^2/2\right)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}},\qquad d_2(S,K,\tau,r,\sigma)=d_1(K,S,\tau,r,\sigma)-\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$$
   where $S$ is the underlying price (taken as the average of open and close), $r$ the risk-free rate, $\tau$ time to maturity, $K$ the strike, $\sigma$ the unobserved volatility and $\Phi$ the standard normal CDF; $P^{\text{Call}}/P^{\text{Put}}$ are the average of the contract's opening and closing prices. **Implied volatility** $\sigma_I$ is the $\sigma$ solving (15)/(16) for the observed average price, obtained by a simple iterative method. All quantities are buyer-perspective (so every call has positive $\Delta$). Standardised moneyness is
   $$\text{Mon}=\begin{cases}+\log\left(Se^{r\tau}/K\right)/(\sigma\sqrt\tau), & \text{for call options}\\[2pt] -\log\left(Se^{r\tau}/K\right)/(\sigma\sqrt\tau), & \text{for put options}\end{cases}$$
   and the five Greeks are $\Delta=\partial P/\partial S$, $\Gamma=\partial^2P/\partial S^2$, $\Theta=\partial P/\partial t$ (Appendix Table 2: $\Theta=-\partial P/\partial\tau$), $\rho=\partial P/\partial r$, $\mathcal{V}=\partial P/\partial\sigma$. **Results:** (i) **Implied volatility is the single most consistent conditioning feature** — the 4th (highest-$\sigma_I$) bucket is best for all three MPCs, significantly beating all others except the 3rd for Brokers, and even beating the *complete* data set on PPD for Brokers and Market Makers; for Market Makers $\sigma_1$ vs $\sigma_4$ is $-2.88^{***}$ and $\sigma_1$ vs $\sigma_3$ is $-1.75^{**}$. (ii) **Puts beat calls**: the 1st $\Delta$ bucket (mostly ITM puts) beats all others across MPCs, significantly for Market Makers and Customers ($\Delta_1$ vs $\Delta_2=1.55^{**}$), and the first two $\rho$ buckets (the puts) are best with significance for all MPCs ($\rho_1$ vs $\rho_3=2.12^{**}$). (iii) **ATM options carry the signal**: high $\Gamma$ (Market Makers' 4th bucket, $\gamma_4$ vs $\gamma_1=2.02^{**}$) and high $\Theta$ (3rd/4th buckets, $\theta_3$ vs $\theta_1=1.99^{**}$) win — contrary to the deep-OTM finding of [Pan and Poteshman, 2006] and [Ge et al., 2016], which the paper attributes to its shorter (next-day) horizon where ATM options are more geared to small moves. Moneyness itself gives no clean ordering beyond bucket 1 (deep OTM) being worst ($\text{mon}_1$ vs $\text{mon}_2=-2.47^{***}$), because the moneyness distribution is skewed toward OTM. (iv) **Maturity has no effect at all** — no consistent bucket pattern for any MPC — so, contrary to common practice, there is *no need to filter* long- or short-dated contracts. (v) **Vega shows no consistent pattern**: sensitivity to volatility does not matter even though the volatility level does. Underlying-side features also fail: no significant differences across **market-cap** buckets, a weak (insignificant) positive relation with **turnover**, and no significant **sector** differences (ANOVA not rejected); only **148 of 1792** individual instruments reject $SR=0$ at 5%, and just **one** survives Bonferroni correction — the signal is a portfolio-level effect.
   *Worked example.* $S=100$, $K=100$, $\tau=30/365=0.082192$, $r=0.02$, $\sigma=0.30$. Then $\sigma\sqrt\tau=0.30\times0.286691=0.0860073$ and $d_1=\left[0+(0.02+0.045)(0.082192)\right]/0.0860073=0.00534249/0.0860073=\mathbf{0.062117}$; $d_2=0.062117-0.086007=\mathbf{-0.023890}$; $\Phi(d_1)=0.524759$, $\Phi(d_2)=0.490469$, $\Phi(-d_1)=0.475241$, $\Phi(-d_2)=0.509531$, $e^{-r\tau}=0.998358$. Formula (15) *as printed* gives $P^{\text{Call}}=100(0.998358)(0.524759)-100(0.998358)(0.490469)=52.3897-48.9664=\mathbf{\$3.42}$, and (16) gives $P^{\text{Put}}=50.8694-47.4461=\mathbf{\$3.42}$. Standardised moneyness for this ATM call: $\log(100e^{0.0016438}/100)/0.0860073=0.0016438/0.0860073=\mathbf{+0.019}$ — essentially zero, i.e. ATM. For a $K=110$ call: $\log(100.16438/110)=-0.093646$, so $\text{Mon}=-0.093646/0.0860073=\mathbf{-1.089}$ (OTM, lands in bucket 1); for a $K=110$ *put* the sign flips to $\mathbf{+1.089}$ (ITM) — so under this convention negative Mon means OTM and positive means ITM for both option types, which is why "bucket 1" is read as deep OTM. *Implied-volatility inversion.* If the observed average price of the ATM call were $\$3.75$, then with $\mathcal{V}\approx S\phi(d_1)\sqrt\tau=100(0.398942)(0.286691)=11.44$ per unit of $\sigma$, one Newton step gives $\sigma_I\approx0.30+(3.75-3.51)/11.44=\mathbf{0.321}$ — that contract would fall into a higher $\sigma$ bucket and, per the paper's finding, contribute more predictive OVI.

10. **Extensions: persistence, buys vs sells, the second exchange, and cross-impact.** *(a) Holding-period persistence.* The multi-day P&L is
    $$\text{P\&L}^h_d=\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}b_{i,d}\times\left(\sum_{k=1}^{h}\tilde f^{CL\_tmCL}_{i,d+(k-1)}\right)\times\text{sign}(s_{i,d})\tag{17}$$
    for holding periods $h=1,\dots,10$ days. For **Market Makers**, day-2 non-cumulative returns are *also* significant ($p=0.00$) and the cumulative return **does not revert** — evidence for informed trading rather than transient price pressure, aligning with [Pan and Poteshman, 2006] and against [Goncalves-Pinto et al., 2020]. Customers' Q4/Q5 show no mean reversion either; **Brokers reverse sign from about day 5 onward**; Firms and Professional Customers are insignificant even on day 1. *(b) Buys vs sells.* Restricting (1) to buy-only or sell-only transactions: $\text{OVI}^{\text{Buy}}_{\text{Customer}}$ is almost uninformative and significantly worse than the full $\text{OVI}_{\text{Customer}}$ (differences $-1.40^{***}$ to $-2.00^{***}$), so **Customers' predictability comes from their selling (option-writing) activity**; the reverse holds for Brokers, where sell-only is significantly worse ($-0.42^{***}$ to $-1.11^{***}$) and buy-only slightly better than the full metric; for Market Makers both restrictions are significantly worse at every QR group. Splitting into **opening vs closing** transactions changes nothing material (closing volumes are much smaller and perform poorly). *(c) NOTO vs PHOTO.* Same qualitative picture, weaker: Market Makers keep a significant annualised $SR$ of about **2.5–3**, overnight returns still dominate, and Q2–Q4 still beat Q1/Q5. But NOTO shows a **price reversal** between overnight and next-day intraday returns (signs of $SR$ flip; no $CL\_tmCL$ result is significant for NOTO), suggesting NOM predictability is more short-term price pressure while PHLX has proportionally more informed flow. Significance uses Bonferroni across $5\times5\times3\times3\times3$ tests, i.e. $p=0.05/675\approx7.4\times10^{-5}$. *(d) Cross-impact network.* With $N=1792$ NYSE assets continuously listed in CRSP over 2015–2019, all $N^2$ ordered pairs are tested: asset $i$'s Market Maker OVI is used to trade asset $j$'s overnight excess return on days when $i$'s OVI is in quantile group $q$,
    $$\text{PnL}_{i,j,d}=\mathbb{1}_{Q(i,d,q)}\,\text{sign}(s_{i,d})\,\tilde f_{j,d}$$
    where $Q(i,d,q)=1$ if asset $i$ is in the $q$-th quantile group on day $d$. An edge $G_{i,j}=1$ is added when the [Bailey and Lopez de Prado, 2014] SR test on $\{\text{PnL}_{i,j,d}\}_{d=1}^{D}$ gives a $p$-value below the threshold — following the **Bonferroni-network** approach of [Tumminello et al., 2011] and [Curme et al., 2014] with FWER $\le p$ at three levels: $0.05$ (no correction), $0.05/N=2.8\times10^{-5}$ (row-level) and $0.05/N^2=1.6\times10^{-8}$ (full). Fully corrected networks still hold **209 to 1207 edges** (Q1 to Q5), decisively rejecting "no cross-impact". Strikingly, in the Q5 network only **2 of 1207 edges are self-loops** (an asset's own OVI predicting itself) — cross-impact vastly outnumbers direct impact — though 2 still exceeds the Erdős–Rényi expectation of 0.67, and the 2 bi-directed edges exceed their expectation of 0.23. The degree distributions fit neither a power law ([Clauset et al., 2009] rejects in-degrees for five networks and out-degrees for six) nor Erdős–Rényi, tested with a two-sample binomial statistic on the empirical densities $\hat p^{\text{IN}}_j=\sum_l G_{l,j}/N$, $\hat p^{\text{OUT}}_j=\sum_l G_{j,l}/N$ under $d^{\text{IN}}_j\sim\text{Binomial}(N,p^{\text{IN}}_j)$:
    $$\text{Test}_{i,j}=\frac{\hat p_i-\hat p_j}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{2N}}}\ \sim\ N(0,1)\ \text{under } H_0$$
    with Bonferroni $p=0.05/N^2$. Rejection rates: **0%** for the fully corrected (small) networks, **5–12%** row-level, **41–58%** uncorrected. The Q3 row-level network has one giant component containing all but two instruments weakly and 942 of 1792 strongly; **Transportation** has the highest average in-degree and lowest out-degree, while **Communications and ETFs** have far above-average out-degrees; a Chung–Lu bipartite null finds **no significant sector-sector cluster structure**. Top-centrality names include **GLD** (out-degree 34, in-degree 0), **INN** (out-degree 42), **CL** (out-degree 38, betweenness 69,515), **TKC** (in-degree 20, Katz 2.946).
    *Worked example of the cross-impact P&L and degree test.* On a day when asset $i$ is in Q3 ($\mathbb{1}_{Q}=1$) with $s_{i,d}=-0.4$ (so $\text{sign}=-1$) and asset $j$'s overnight EMR is $\tilde f_{j,d}=-0.006$, the pair's return is $\text{PnL}_{i,j,d}=1\times(-1)\times(-0.006)=\mathbf{+0.006}$; the series over $D$ days is then fed to the SR test. For the degree test, compare a node with $d^{\text{IN}}=20$ against one with $d^{\text{IN}}=5$ in the $N=1792$ Q5 network: $\hat p_i=20/1792=0.011161$, $\hat p_j=5/1792=0.002790$, pooled $\hat p=25/3584=0.006975$, $\hat p(1-\hat p)=0.0069264$. As printed, the denominator is $\sqrt{0.0069264/3584}=1.390\times10^{-3}$ and $\text{Test}=0.008371/0.001390=\mathbf{6.02}$; the Bonferroni threshold $p=0.05/1792^2=1.56\times10^{-8}$ corresponds to $|z|\approx5.54$, so this pair **rejects**.

11. **Stated limitations and caveats.** The paper is explicit that (i) **transaction costs are ignored** and the P&L may not be executable — liquidity and timing constraints are unmodelled, so the metric measures predictability rather than realised profit; (ii) hedging customers cannot be identified, so hedge-motivated option trades pollute the "directional signal" interpretation (straddle-type strategies are argued to cancel out, but hedges do not); (iii) **each transaction is double-counted** by construction across MPCs; (iv) **Black–Scholes is used on American-style equity options**, which the authors acknowledge may systematically undervalue them, dismissed as a "typically 1–2%" error to avoid large-scale computation; (v) the multi-factor model $s^2$ **overfits** relative to the single Market Maker signal out-of-sample (1.25 bp vs 1.33 bp); (vi) OVI's explanatory power on *contemporaneous* returns is much stronger than on future returns ($R^2$ 34× larger for Market Makers), i.e. most of the OVI–return relationship is same-day, not predictive; (vii) the **intraday analysis is not quantifiable** with the data at hand — the universe is smaller and restricted to 2018–2019, and after fully Bonferroni-correcting across 3 QR buckets × 10 return modes × 5 MPCs × 39 times, **no** intraday SR is significant (Benjamini–Hochberg also yields no rejection), so those results are reported only qualitatively: option volume follows the familiar **U-shape** intraday, Market Maker open-to-$t$ OVI peaks around **13:10**, and a seasonal-like oscillation in SR versus $t$ appears across MPCs (e.g. Firm 90-minute EMR flips sign after 10:30–11:00 and again at 12:30, 13:20, 15:10); (viii) a **trend shift after 2018** is visible for Firms and other MPCs, and Broker/Customer coefficients shrink in later years, so the relationship is not stationary; (ix) the P&L regression objective is **non-convex with no closed form** and requires tuning $\lambda$.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### Decomposing option flow by market participant class

The paper's most distinctive move is to compute a separate OVI for **Firm, Broker, Market Maker, Customer and Professional Customer** volumes, where earlier work used only customer or generic non-market-maker data. Because every transaction is recorded from both sides, the same trade appears with opposite direction for the two counterparties, and the paper argues this is a feature rather than a bug: a class that profits from *speculative accuracy* (Customers) should generate a positive signal, whereas Market Makers — who profit from spreads, not direction — should generate a **negative** one that mirrors the speculative ability of everyone trading against them. The empirical result matches that prediction with unusual sharpness: Market Maker OVI is the **strongest** signal at $|SR|$ up to **4.42–4.5**, Customers is next at **+2.66 to +3.46**, Brokers is significant and negative, and the two thinnest-volume classes (Firms, Professional Customers) are statistically indistinguishable from noise ($p\approx0.34$–$0.46$).

**Why it matters:** it turns "option flow predicts stocks" into a **testable microstructure claim about who is informed**, and it identifies the most useful column of the data — Market Maker volume, the one class the exchange does not even break into opening/closing intents — as the **best single predictor**, delivering a higher out-of-sample PPD (1.33 bp) than a fitted five-factor combination of all classes (1.25 bp). For anyone building option-flow features, this says the participant tag is a **first-order design choice**, not a detail.

### Signal magnitude, quantile-rank groups, and the failure of the extremes

OVI is used through its sign, so magnitude has to enter somewhere else. The paper enters it twice — through **cumulative quantile-rank groups** $Q1$ (all signals) to $Q5$ (strongest 20%), and through the **Imbalance** bet-sizing scheme $b'_{i,d}=|s_{i,d}|$ — and finds a non-monotone answer. Distinct-bucket tests show **QB1 is almost uninformative** while QB2–QB5 carry the signal, confirming magnitude matters; but cumulative-group tests show **Q3 (top 60%) is the best performer** on both PPD and SR for every MPC except Firms, with Q1 worst on PPD and Q1 or Q5 worst on SR. Meanwhile every **volume-based** weighting scheme (Volume, Nominal Volume, Relative Volume) is significantly *beaten* by the plain **Uniform** scheme on Sharpe, because volume weighting adds variance faster than it adds edge.

**Why it matters:** it is a **concrete, transferable portfolio-construction rule**: filter out the weakest signals, but do not chase the extreme tail, and do not let liquidity proxies drive sizing. The mechanism is diversification — Q5 holds only ~313 assets versus ~886 for Q1, so the incremental per-name edge of the top quintile is **outweighed by the loss of averaging**, and the paper's numbers let you see the trade-off directly (Q3 $SR=-4.42$ with $n=532$ versus Q5 $SR=-3.55$ with $n=313$).

### Implied volatility as the dominant conditioning feature, and puts over calls

Segmenting contracts into quartiles of each option feature and re-running the whole pipeline, the paper finds that **implied volatility has the most significant and consistent effect** on OVI's predictive power: the highest-$\sigma_I$ bucket is best for Brokers, Market Makers *and* Customers, significantly beating all other buckets (for Market Makers, $\sigma_1$ vs $\sigma_4=-2.88^{***}$), and even beating the full data set on PPD. **Put options carry more information than calls** — the lowest $\Delta$ bucket (ITM puts) and the two lowest $\rho$ buckets (all puts) are the best performers, significantly so. **ATM options** (high $\Gamma$, high $\Theta$) beat OTM ones, reversing the deep-OTM result of [Pan and Poteshman, 2006] and [Ge et al., 2016] — the paper attributes this to its **next-day** horizon, where ATM contracts give more gearing per small move. And **maturity has no effect whatsoever**, nor does Vega.

**Why it matters:** it is an actionable **filter specification** with a stated null result attached. The IV finding is strong enough that the paper builds a bet-sizing scheme around it (IV-Volume, best PPD of all schemes at −3.9 bp), and the maturity null **saves work** — it explicitly licenses skipping the maturity filters that comparable studies apply. The horizon-dependence of the moneyness result is the sharpest methodological warning in the paper: **the informative slice of the option chain depends on the prediction horizon**, so filters cannot be copied across horizons.

### P&L regression: matching the objective to the decision

Standard practice regresses returns on candidate features and reads off $t$-, $p$- or $R^2$ values, but the paper shows why that fails here. Its own OLS run finds *all* OVIs significant with $R^2$ so low they cannot be ranked; the digest's arithmetic in point 8 shows why — an $R^2$ of $1.65\times10^{-4}$ over ~$10^5$ observations still gives $t\approx4$. The proposed alternative optimises the thing you actually care about: it makes the **cumulative P&L itself the objective** (equation 11), smooths $\text{sign}$ into a logistic activation $g$ and $|\cdot|$ into $h(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\alpha_2}$ so ADAM can be used, and adds an $\ell_1$ penalty to cope with the strong multicollinearity among OVIs (Customer–Market Maker Spearman correlation about **−0.6**) and with feature **sparsity** (OVI is exactly zero on many asset-days, a case linear methods cannot ignore).

**Why it matters:** this is the paper's most **portable** contribution, independent of options entirely. Any directional-forecasting problem where a predictor gets signs right but magnitudes wrong, or where features are correlated and sparse, inherits the same misalignment; the fitted $\beta_k$ also read directly as **decision weights** rather than as conditional-mean slopes. Its practical value is demonstrated, not just asserted: the method surfaces a useful Professional-Customer coefficient with a large magnitude precisely *because* that feature is mostly zero — something the authors note "would not have been possible" under linear regression.

### Overnight-only predictability, persistence, and the informed-trading verdict

Across return modes, predictability concentrates overwhelmingly in the **overnight ($CL\_tmOP$) window** — Market Maker Q3 gives $SR=-4.42$ overnight, $-3.08$ close-to-close, and only $-1.16$ open-to-close — and close-to-close predictability is shown to be inherited from the overnight leg rather than from the next day's session. The multi-day test (equation 17) then separates the two competing explanations in the literature: for **Market Makers the cumulative return does not revert** over 10 days and day-2 returns are themselves significant, which points to **informed trading** rather than transient **price pressure**; for **Brokers the signal reverses from around day 5**, and NOTO shows an overnight-to-intraday sign flip absent from PHOTO, suggesting the NOM exchange's predictability is more pressure-driven than PHLX's.

**Why it matters:** it converts a return-prediction result into an **economic diagnosis**, and it does so with a test anyone can run — reversal versus persistence over increasing holding periods. It also localises the tradable window: the edge lives in the **close-to-open gap**, which is exactly the window with no intraday execution and the hardest one in which to trade size. Finally, the exchange-level contrast means the *same* feature can be an informed-trading signal on one venue and a pressure signal on another, so venue is a variable, not a nuisance.

### Cross-impact: one asset's option flow predicting another's returns

Testing all $N^2$ ordered pairs among **1792** continuously-listed NYSE assets and adding an edge whenever the pair's SR is significant, the paper builds 15 **Bonferroni networks** at three correction levels. Even under full correction ($p=0.05/N^2=1.6\times10^{-8}$) the networks retain **209–1207 edges**, and — the headline — of Q5's 1207 edges only **two are self-loops**. Cross-impact therefore **outnumbers direct impact by roughly 600:1** under the strictest threshold. The degree structure fits neither a power law nor Erdős–Rényi; ETFs and Communications are net "senders" (high out-degree), Transportation is a net "receiver" (highest in-degree, lowest out-degree), and there is no significant sector-sector clustering.

**Why it matters:** it says the standard framing — *asset $i$'s option flow predicts asset $i$'s stock* — is looking in the **wrong place**. The overwhelming majority of statistically validated relationships are **lead-lag across names**, which reframes OVI as a **network signal** rather than a per-asset feature and points to a much larger hypothesis space than single-asset tests can see. The absence of sector clustering is equally informative: the routing is **not** explained by the obvious grouping, so the network has to be estimated, not assumed.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

### Algorithm A — Constructing the Option Volume Imbalance (OVI)

**Goal:** Turn a day's raw, participant-tagged option volume reports for one underlying asset into a single number in $[-1,1]$ that says how lopsided that day's directional option flow was.

**Inputs:** For asset $i$ on day $d$, for every option contract $j=1,\dots,C_{i,d}$ and every market participant class $m$: the volumes (or trade counts) split by transaction type — call buys, call sells, put buys, put sells; the contract's average of opening and closing price $P^{\text{Option}}_{i,j,d}$ (only for the nominal variant); optionally a filter $F$ (a subset of contracts, e.g. one implied-volatility quartile, or buys only). **Outputs:** one scalar $\text{OVI}^{[F]}_{i,d,m}\in[-1,1]$ per asset, per day, per participant class, per variant.

**Steps:**

1. **Choose the flow variable.** Decide what "amount of flow" means. $X=V$ counts **contracts** (volume-based OVI); $X=T$ counts **transactions** regardless of size (trade-based OVI); $X=P^{\text{Option}}V$ counts **dollars** (nominal-volume-based OVI). The reason to offer all three: volume-based OVI over-weights cheap deep-OTM contracts that trade in huge size, and the nominal variant fixes that by pricing each contract in, while the trade-based variant ignores size entirely.
2. **Label each transaction bullish or bearish.** A trader expecting the underlying to rise either buys a call or sells a put; one expecting a fall either sells a call or buys a put. So, per contract, sum the two bullish legs and the two bearish legs separately:
   $$X^{\text{Up},F}_{i,j,d,m}=X^{\text{Call Buy},F}_{i,j,d,m}+X^{\text{Put Sell},F}_{i,j,d,m},\qquad X^{\text{Down},F}_{i,j,d,m}=X^{\text{Call Sell},F}_{i,j,d,m}+X^{\text{Put Buy},F}_{i,j,d,m}$$
   Note this is done **from the perspective of each participant class separately**, so a single trade contributes an "Up" to the buyer's class and a "Down" to the seller's class — deliberate double-counting that lets the same trade be read from both sides.
3. **Aggregate across the whole option chain.** Add the Up legs over all $C_{i,d}$ contracts of that asset, and likewise the Down legs. This collapses strikes and maturities into one bullish total and one bearish total. (The paper's evidence in Section 6 justifies not filtering by maturity, since maturity has no measurable effect on predictability.)
4. **Take the normalised difference.** Divide the net directional flow by the gross flow:
   $$\text{OVI}^{[F]}_{i,d,m}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}\left(X^{\text{Up},F}_{i,j,d,m}-X^{\text{Down},F}_{i,j,d,m}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}\left(X^{\text{Up},F}_{i,j,d,m}+X^{\text{Down},F}_{i,j,d,m}\right)}$$
   The denominator is what makes the metric **comparable across assets**: a mega-cap with a million contracts of flow and a small-cap with a thousand both land on the same $[-1,1]$ scale, so liquidity and market cap drop out.
5. **Handle the empty case.** If an asset had **no** option flow that day for that class, both numerator and denominator are zero; the paper *defines* $0/0=0$, so the asset simply gets a neutral signal and is excluded from trading (the Uniform scheme only allocates to assets with $s_{i,d}\neq0$). This is why the feature is **sparse** and why linear regression handles it badly.
6. **Repeat per class and per variant.** Loop steps 1–5 over the five MPCs and the three flow variables (and, for the extensions, over buys-only / sells-only and over the two exchanges), producing the feature matrix $A_{i,d,k}$ used later.

**Worked example (end-to-end, with intermediate values).** Asset $i$, day $d$, Market Maker class, two contracts.

| | Contract 1 | Contract 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Call buys (vol) | 800 | 100 |
| Put sells (vol) | 200 | 50 |
| Call sells (vol) | 300 | 400 |
| Put buys (vol) | 100 | 50 |
| Avg option price | \$2.50 | \$0.40 |

- *Step 1:* choose $X=V$ first.
- *Step 2:* Contract 1: $V^{\text{Up}}=800+200=\mathbf{1000}$, $V^{\text{Down}}=300+100=\mathbf{400}$. Contract 2: $V^{\text{Up}}=100+50=\mathbf{150}$, $V^{\text{Down}}=400+50=\mathbf{450}$.
- *Step 3:* $\sum_j V^{\text{Up}}=1000+150=\mathbf{1150}$; $\sum_j V^{\text{Down}}=400+450=\mathbf{850}$.
- *Step 4:* numerator $=1150-850=\mathbf{300}$; denominator $=1150+850=\mathbf{2000}$; $\text{OVI}=300/2000=\mathbf{+0.15}$ — a mildly bullish Market Maker imbalance.
- *Step 1 repeated with $X=P^{\text{Option}}V$ (nominal):* Up $=2.50(1000)+0.40(150)=2500+60=\mathbf{2560}$; Down $=2.50(400)+0.40(450)=1000+180=\mathbf{1180}$; $\text{OVI}^{\text{nom}}=(2560-1180)/(2560+1180)=1380/3740=\mathbf{+0.369}$. The cheap contract 2 was carrying most of the bearish weight; pricing it in **more than doubles** the bullish reading.
- *Step 5 check:* if a third contract existed with zero volume, it contributes $0$ to both sums and changes nothing; if the asset had *no* flow at all, $\text{OVI}=0$ by definition and the asset is skipped.
- *Interpretation:* because this is a Market Maker OVI and the paper finds Market Maker OVI is **negatively** related to next-day returns, a reading of $+0.15$ is a **short** signal on asset $i$ for the overnight window.

### Algorithm B — Evaluating an OVI signal: quantile-rank portfolios, bet sizing, and significance

**Goal:** Convert a daily cross-section of signals into a hypothetical market-neutral daily P&L series, and decide whether the resulting Sharpe Ratio is real or noise.

**Inputs:** Daily signals $s_{i,d}$ for $N_d$ assets (a single OVI, or the combination from Algorithm C); the underlying's opening and closing prices, split- and dividend-adjusted; the SPY return for the same window; per-asset option volume and open interest (only if a volume-based bet scheme is used); a choice of quantile group $q\in\{1,\dots,5\}$ and of bet-sizing scheme. **Outputs:** a P&L vector $(\text{P\&L}_1,\dots,\text{P\&L}_{D-1})$; the annualised Sharpe Ratio $SR$; the Profit-Per-Dollar $PPD$ in basis points; a $p$-value for $H_0:SR=0$; optionally a $p$-value for $H_0:SR_1=SR_2$ against another strategy.

**Steps:**

1. **Compute the target return.** For each asset pick the return type. Overnight is the default and the most predictable: $f^{CL\_tmOP}_{i,d}=\left(P^{\text{open}}_{i,d+1}-P^{\text{close}}_{i,d}\right)/P^{\text{close}}_{i,d}$. Then strip out the market so the study is direction-of-market-neutral: $\tilde f_{i,d}=f_{i,d}-f_{\text{SPY},d}$. Using excess returns is what allows the result to be read as *stock-specific* predictability rather than a hidden beta bet.
2. **Rank by signal strength and select the universe.** Sort the day's assets by $|s_{i,d}|$ into quintiles $QB1$ (weakest) to $QB5$ (strongest), then form the **cumulative** group $Qq=QB(6-q)\cup\dots\cup QB5$, i.e. the top $20q\%$ of signals by magnitude — $Q1$ is everything, $Q5$ is the strongest fifth only. Assets with $s_{i,d}=0$ are never traded. Rationale: magnitude proxies confidence, but as step 6 shows, the very top of the distribution is too small a portfolio to be reliable.
3. **Size the bets and normalise to one dollar.** Compute a raw size $b'_{i,d}$ per the chosen scheme — $b'=1$ (Uniform), $b'=|s_{i,d}|$ (Imbalance), $b'=\log\!\left(1+\sum_j V_{i,j,d,m}\right)$ (Volume), $b'=\log\!\left(1+\sum_j P_{i,j,d}V_{i,j,d,m}\right)$ (Nominal Volume), $b'=\log\!\left(1+\sum_j V_{i,j,d,m}/OI_{i,j,d}\right)$ (Relative Volume), or the IV-weighted variant — then set
   $$b_{i,d}=\frac{b'_{i,d}}{\sum_j b'_{j,d}}$$
   so that $B_d=\sum_i b_{i,d}=1$ every day. This normalisation is what makes cumulative P&L readable as a **cumulative percentage return** and makes different schemes comparable.
4. **Trade the sign and accumulate the day's P&L.**
   $$\text{P\&L}_d=\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}b_{i,d}\times\tilde f_{i,d}\times\text{sign}(s_{i,d})$$
   Only the sign enters — a positive signal is a long, a negative one a short. Magnitude has already been used twice (in the universe selection of step 2 and possibly in the sizing of step 3), which is deliberate: the paper makes **no linearity assumption** between OVI and returns.
5. **Summarise the series.** Annualise with $SR=\text{mean}(\text{P\&L})\sqrt{252}/\text{sd}(\text{P\&L})$ and compute $PPD=\sum_d\text{P\&L}_d/\sum_d B_d$, reported in basis points. Because $B_d=1$, $PPD$ is simply the **average daily percentage return**.
6. **Test significance, correcting for non-normal returns.** Daily P&L is skewed and fat-tailed, so a naive $t$-test overstates confidence. Use the [Bailey and Lopez de Prado, 2014] test with the [Bao, 2008] variance:
   $$\text{stat}=\frac{SR}{\sqrt{\dfrac{1-\text{skewness}\cdot SR+\left(\text{kurtosis}-1\right)SR^2/4}{T-1}}}$$
   and compare $|\text{stat}|$ to a normal quantile. To compare two schemes or two buckets, use the [Ledoit and Wolf, 2008] studentised bootstrap on VAR residuals with circular blocks, which handles both non-normality and serial dependence. Apply **Bonferroni** correction across the full grid of tests being run (the paper uses $0.05/675\approx7.4\times10^{-5}$ for its 675-cell exchange comparison).
7. **Read the sign convention carefully.** A significantly *negative* $SR$ is just as much a discovery as a positive one — it means the signal predicts with the **opposite** sign, which is exactly the Market Maker case. Comparisons between buckets are therefore comparisons of $|SR|$.

**Worked example (end-to-end, with intermediate values).** Four assets on day $d$, Uniform scheme, $Q1$ (all non-zero signals).

- *Step 1:* prices give overnight raw returns $0.008,-0.001,0.001,0.009$; SPY gapped $+0.003$; excess returns are $\tilde f=(\mathbf{+0.005},\mathbf{-0.004},\mathbf{-0.002},\mathbf{+0.006})$.
- *Step 2:* signals $s=(+0.15,-0.30,+0.05,-0.10)$, magnitudes $(0.15,0.30,0.05,0.10)$. All four are non-zero, so $Q1$ keeps all four. (Under $Q3$ — top 60%, i.e. the strongest 2.4 → 2 names here — only assets 2 and 1 would survive.)
- *Step 3:* Uniform gives $b'=1$ each, so $b=1/4=\mathbf{0.25}$ each and $B_d=1$. ✓
- *Step 4:* signs are $(+,-,+,-)$, so $\text{P\&L}_d=0.25\left[(+0.005)(+1)+(-0.004)(-1)+(-0.002)(+1)+(+0.006)(-1)\right]=0.25\left[0.005+0.004-0.002-0.006\right]=0.25(0.001)=\mathbf{0.00025}=\mathbf{2.5\ bp}$. Three of four names paid; asset 4's short lost.
- *Step 5, scaled to the paper's actual Q3 Market Maker cell:* suppose the daily series has $\text{mean}=-1.9\times10^{-4}$ and $\text{sd}=6.82\times10^{-4}$ over $T=1257$ days. Then $PPD=\mathbf{-1.9\ bp}$, cumulative P&L $=1257(-1.9\times10^{-4})=\mathbf{-0.239}$ (**−23.9%**), and $SR=(-1.9\times10^{-4})(15.8745)/(6.82\times10^{-4})=\mathbf{-4.42}$ — reproducing the paper's headline figure.
- *Step 6:* per-day $SR=-4.42/15.8745=-0.2784$; with skewness $0.2$ and kurtosis $8$, the variance numerator is $1-0.2(-0.2784)+7(0.2784)^2/4=1+0.0557+0.1356=1.1913$, so the standard error is $\sqrt{1.1913/1256}=\mathbf{0.0308}$ and $\text{stat}=-0.2784/0.0308=\mathbf{-9.04}$ ($p\sim10^{-19}$) — the paper's reported $p=0.00$.
- *Step 7:* the negative $SR$ means the executable version of this strategy is to **flip the signal**: short when Market Maker OVI is positive. Flipped, the same series earns **+23.9%** cumulative over five years, before costs.

### Algorithm C — P&L Regression (combining many OVI features)

**Goal:** Find the linear combination of many correlated, sparse OVI features whose *sign* maximises cumulative trading profit — rather than the combination that minimises squared return error.

**Inputs:** Feature tensor $A_{i,d,k}$ for $K$ OVI variants, $N$ assets and $D$ days; realised returns $f_{i,d}$; hyperparameters $\alpha_1$ (activation sharpness), $\alpha_2$ (absolute-value smoothing), $\lambda$ ($\ell_1$ strength), learning rate $\eta$, window length $l$, test length $T$. **Outputs:** a coefficient vector $\tilde\beta$ with unit $\ell_1$ norm on its non-intercept entries, interpretable directly as per-feature buy/sell decision weights; plus an out-of-sample PPD for each test window.

**Steps:**

1. **Form the composite signal.** $s_{i,d}(\beta;A)=\beta_0+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_k A_{i,d,k}$. Each $\beta_k$ says how much feature $k$ pushes the decision toward long or short; the intercept allows a standing directional tilt.
2. **Write the thing you actually want to maximise.** Total P&L over the window, $PL(\beta;A,f)=\sum_{d}\sum_i b_{i,d}(\beta;A)f_{i,d}\,\text{sign}(s_{i,d}(\beta;A))$. This objective has no closed form and, critically, is **piecewise constant** in $\beta$ — the $\text{sign}$ function makes its gradient zero almost everywhere — so it cannot be optimised directly.
3. **Smooth the sign function.** Replace $\text{sign}$ with a steepened logistic:
   $$g(x;\alpha_1)=2\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha_1x}}-\frac12\right)=\tanh\!\left(\frac{\alpha_1x}{2}\right),\qquad\alpha_1>0$$
   which equals $\tanh$ at $\alpha_1=2$ and converges pointwise to $\text{sign}$ as $\alpha_1\to\infty$. Now the objective has a non-trivial gradient.
4. **Absorb the sign into the bet size.** Define $b_{i,d}(\beta;A)=\left|g(s_{i,d})\right|/\sum_j\left|g(s_{j,d})\right|$, which both keeps the daily budget at one dollar and removes the need for a separate $\text{sign}$ factor, since $b\cdot\text{sign}(s)$ becomes just $g(s)/\sum_j|g(s_j)|$. The objective becomes $PL=\sum_d\sum_i\frac{f_{i,d}\,g(s_{i,d})}{\sum_j|g(s_{j,d})|}$.
5. **Smooth the remaining absolute values.** Both the denominator of step 4 and the $\ell_1$ penalty are non-differentiable at zero, so replace $|x|$ by
   $$h(x;\alpha_2)=\sqrt{x^2+\alpha_2},\qquad\alpha_2>0\ \text{small}$$
   which is $C^\infty$ and satisfies $h(x)\to|x|$ as $\alpha_2\to0$.
6. **Assemble the loss (negated, plus a LASSO penalty).**
   $$L=-\sum_{d=1}^{D-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{f_{i,d}\,g(s_{i,d}(\beta;A))}{\sum_j h\!\left(g(s_{j,d}(\beta;A))\right)}\right)+\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{K}h(\beta_k;\alpha_2)$$
   The penalty is what makes the method usable at all here: the OVI features are strongly multicollinear (Customer–Market Maker Spearman $\approx-0.6$) so an unpenalised fit would be unstable, and the penalty lets genuinely uninformative features be shrunk toward zero so the surviving $|\beta_k|$ can be **ranked**.
7. **Descend with ADAM.** Use the analytic gradient (the quotient rule on step 6, with $\partial g(s_{i,d})/\partial\beta_k=g'(s_{i,d})A_{i,d,k}$ and $h'(x)=x/\sqrt{x^2+\alpha_2}$), and run **ADAM** with moment parameters 0.9 / 0.999. The intercept is excluded from the penalty via the indicator $\left(1-\mathbb{1}(k=0)\right)$. The problem is **non-convex**, so this finds a local optimum.
8. **Rescale for interpretability.** Set $\tilde\beta=\hat\beta/\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\hat\beta_k|$ so the non-intercept coefficients sum to 1 in absolute value. Under a true $\text{sign}$ function this is exactly neutral (scaling $\beta$ doesn't change any sign); under the $\tanh$ proxy it changes the objective slightly, because it changes how saturated the activations are.
9. **Validate out-of-sample with a sliding window.** Fit on days $\{d-l+1,\dots,d\}$ with $l=500$ (~2 years), then evaluate the frozen $\hat\beta$ on $\text{Test}_d=\{d+1,\dots,d+T\}$ with $T=100$, and step the window forward (step size 50). Report the out-of-sample PPD, which reads directly as the profit from 100 days of trading. This is the step that exposed the overfitting of the five-MPC model $s^2$ versus the single-feature $s^1$.

**Worked example (end-to-end, with intermediate values).** $K=2$ features, 3 assets, one day, $\alpha_1=10$, $\alpha_2=10^{-5}$, $\lambda=0.01$, $\eta=0.01$, starting from $\beta=(0,0.6,-0.4)$. Features: $A_1=(0.20,0.10)$, $A_2=(-0.30,0.20)$, $A_3=(0.05,-0.50)$. Returns $f=(0.005,-0.004,0.006)$.

- *Step 1:* $s_1=0.6(0.20)-0.4(0.10)=\mathbf{0.08}$; $s_2=0.6(-0.30)-0.4(0.20)=\mathbf{-0.26}$; $s_3=0.6(0.05)-0.4(-0.50)=\mathbf{0.23}$.
- *Step 3:* $g(x)=\tanh(5x)$, so $g_1=\tanh(0.40)=\mathbf{0.37995}$, $g_2=\tanh(-1.30)=\mathbf{-0.86172}$, $g_3=\tanh(1.15)=\mathbf{0.81775}$. Note all three are already well away from $\pm1$ but asset 2 and 3 are heading toward saturation.
- *Step 5:* $h(g_1)=\sqrt{0.144362+10^{-5}}=0.379963$; $h(g_2)=0.861726$; $h(g_3)=0.817756$; $\sum_j h=\mathbf{2.059445}$ (versus $\sum|g|=2.059442$ — the smoothing costs 1.5 parts per million).
- *Step 4:* implied bet sizes $b=(0.379963,0.861726,0.817756)/2.059445=(\mathbf{0.1845},\mathbf{0.4184},\mathbf{0.3971})$, summing to 1. ✓
- *Step 6, data term:* $\sum_i f_i g_i=0.005(0.37995)+(-0.004)(-0.86172)+0.006(0.81775)=0.00189975+0.00344688+0.00490650=\mathbf{0.01025313}$; divided by $2.059445$ gives $PL=\mathbf{0.004979}$ (≈ **49.8 bp** for the day). *Penalty:* $h(0.6)=0.600008$, $h(-0.4)=0.400013$, so $\lambda\sum h=0.01(1.000021)=\mathbf{0.0100002}$. Total $L=-0.004979+0.010000=\mathbf{+0.005021}$.
- *Step 7, gradient w.r.t. $\beta_1$:* $g'(x)=5\left(1-g(x)^2\right)$ gives $g'(s_1)=5(1-0.14436)=4.27819$, $g'(s_2)=5(1-0.74256)=1.28720$, $g'(s_3)=5(1-0.66872)=1.65643$; so $\partial g_i/\partial\beta_1=g'(s_i)A_{i,1}=\mathbf{0.855638},\ \mathbf{-0.386159},\ \mathbf{0.082821}$. First term: $\frac{1}{2.059445}\left[0.005(0.855638)-0.004(-0.386159)+0.006(0.082821)\right]=\frac{0.0063198}{2.059445}=\mathbf{0.0030687}$. Second term: $\sum_j h'(g_j)\partial g_j/\partial\beta_1=0.855609+0.386156+0.082820=1.324585$, and $0.01025313\times(2.059445)^{-2}\times1.324585=0.01025313\times0.235777\times1.324585=\mathbf{0.0032021}$. Hence $\partial L/\partial\beta_1=-(0.0030687-0.0032021)+0.01(0.999986)=0.00013340+0.00999986=\mathbf{+0.0101333}$ — dominated by the penalty, telling the optimiser to shrink $\beta_1$.
- *Step 7, ADAM's first update:* with bias correction, the first step is $\approx-\eta\,\text{sign}(\text{grad})$, so $\beta_1\leftarrow0.6-0.01=\mathbf{0.59}$.
- *Step 8:* suppose the run converges at $\hat\beta=(0.02,0.75,-0.45)$. Then $\sum_{k\ge1}|\hat\beta_k|=1.20$ and $\tilde\beta=(\mathbf{0.0167},\mathbf{0.625},\mathbf{-0.375})$. Reading: feature 1 carries **62.5%** of the decision weight positively, feature 2 **37.5%** negatively.
- *Step 9:* freeze $\tilde\beta$, run Algorithm B on the next 100 days, and record the PPD. In the paper's actual five-MPC run this produced **1.57 bp in-sample but only 1.25 bp out-of-sample**, versus **1.50 / 1.33 bp** for the single Market Maker feature — a textbook overfitting signature, since the richer model wins in-sample and loses out.

### Algorithm D — Option-feature bucketing via Black–Scholes implied volatility and Greeks

**Goal:** Find out *which* option contracts carry the predictive information, by re-running the entire OVI pipeline separately on quartiles of each option characteristic.

**Inputs:** Every traded contract's strike $K$, time to maturity $\tau$, type (call/put), and average of opening and closing price; the underlying price $S$ (average of open and close); a risk-free rate $r$. **Outputs:** for each feature (moneyness, maturity, implied volatility, $\Delta$, $\Gamma$, $\Theta$, $\rho$, $\mathcal{V}$) and each of four buckets, a full set of $SR$/$PPD$/instrument-count figures, plus pairwise SR-difference test results identifying the best-performing bucket.

**Steps:**

1. **Price the contract under Black–Scholes.** Use the European, no-dividend model as a computational convenience:
   $$P^{\text{Call}}=Se^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_1)-Ke^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2),\qquad P^{\text{Put}}=Ke^{-r\tau}\Phi(-d_2)-Se^{-r\tau}\Phi(-d_1)$$
   $$d_1=\frac{\log(S/K)+\left(r+\sigma^2/2\right)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt\tau},\qquad d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt\tau$$
   The paper acknowledges equity options are mostly **American**, so BS may systematically undervalue them, and accepts the error as "typically 1–2%" to avoid large-scale computation.
2. **Invert for implied volatility.** $\sigma_I$ is the $\sigma$ that makes the model price equal the observed average of the contract's opening and closing prices; solve with a simple iterative method (Newton on vega, or bisection). This is the only feature that must be *backed out* rather than read off.
3. **Compute the Greeks and standardised moneyness.** $\Delta=\partial P/\partial S$ (positive for all calls, negative for all puts, so the $\Delta$ buckets effectively sort puts before calls); $\Gamma=\partial^2P/\partial S^2$ (peaks ATM, so high-$\Gamma$ buckets ≈ ATM); $\Theta=\partial P/\partial t$ (also largest ATM); $\rho=\partial P/\partial r$ (positive for calls, negative for puts, so the low-$\rho$ buckets are the puts); $\mathcal{V}=\partial P/\partial\sigma$. Moneyness is standardised by the volatility-scaled log forward:
   $$\text{Mon}=\begin{cases}+\log\left(Se^{r\tau}/K\right)/(\sigma\sqrt\tau) & \text{calls}\\ -\log\left(Se^{r\tau}/K\right)/(\sigma\sqrt\tau) & \text{puts}\end{cases}$$
   so that **positive = ITM and negative = OTM for both option types**. Note the Greeks are not in one-to-one correspondence with moneyness — $\Gamma$ tracks $|{\text{moneyness}}|$ while $\Delta$ is monotone in moneyness — which is precisely why several Greeks are worth bucketing separately: each slices the option universe differently.
4. **Bucket by daily quartile.** For each day and each feature, split all traded contracts into four disjoint buckets: bucket 1 = lowest 25% of the feature's values, bucket 4 = highest 25%. Quartiles are recomputed daily, so buckets are relative to that day's chain, not to fixed thresholds.
5. **Re-run the whole pipeline per bucket.** For each bucket, treat it as the filter $F$ in Algorithm A, recompute OVI from only those contracts, then run Algorithm B end-to-end (quantile groups, uniform sizing, overnight excess returns). Restrict to **Brokers, Market Makers and Customers** — the only three MPCs whose OVI was significant, so the others would only add noise.
6. **Compare buckets with pairwise tests.** Run the Ledoit–Wolf SR difference test between all $\binom42=6$ bucket pairs at the 5% level, and read the resulting matrix. Because the Market Maker SR is negative throughout, "outperforms" means **larger in magnitude**.
7. **Act on the winner.** If one feature dominates, fold it into the bet sizing. Implied volatility did, so the paper adds the IV-Volume scheme $b'_{i,d}=\sum_j\sum_m\log\left(1+\min\{\sigma_{i,j,d},2\}\right)V_{i,j,d,m}$, with the cap at $\sigma=2$ stopping absurd implied vols from a mispriced illiquid contract from dominating the whole book.

**Worked example (end-to-end, with intermediate values).** One ATM call: $S=100$, $K=100$, $\tau=30/365=0.082192$, $r=0.02$; observed average contract price \$3.75.

- *Step 1, trial $\sigma=0.30$:* $\sigma\sqrt\tau=0.30(0.286691)=\mathbf{0.0860073}$; $d_1=\left[\log(1)+(0.02+0.045)(0.082192)\right]/0.0860073=0.00534249/0.0860073=\mathbf{0.062117}$; $d_2=0.062117-0.086007=\mathbf{-0.023890}$; $\Phi(d_1)=\mathbf{0.524759}$, $\Phi(d_2)=\mathbf{0.490469}$, $e^{-r\tau}=0.998358$. The formula as printed gives $P^{\text{Call}}=100(0.998358)(0.524759)-100(0.998358)(0.490469)=52.3897-48.9664=\mathbf{\$3.42}$.
- *Step 2:* the model says \$3.42, the market says \$3.75, a gap of $+\$0.33$. Vega is $\mathcal{V}\approx S\phi(d_1)\sqrt\tau=100(0.398942)(0.286691)=\mathbf{11.44}$ per unit $\sigma$. One Newton step: $\sigma_I\approx0.30+0.33/11.44=\mathbf{0.329}$. A second iteration would refine it slightly; call it $\sigma_I\approx0.33$.
- *Step 3:* with $\sigma_I=0.33$, $\sigma\sqrt\tau=0.0946$, $\Delta=\Phi(d_1)\approx\mathbf{+0.525}$ (positive ⇒ a call ⇒ lands in a high-$\Delta$ bucket, which the paper finds **less** informative); $\Gamma=\phi(d_1)/(S\sigma\sqrt\tau)=0.398942/(100\times0.0946)=\mathbf{0.0422}$ (high — this is an ATM contract, the informative kind); $\rho>0$ (a call ⇒ a high-$\rho$ bucket, again the less informative side). Moneyness: $\log\left(100e^{0.0016438}/100\right)/0.0946=0.0016438/0.0946=\mathbf{+0.017}$ ⇒ ATM. Compare a $K=110$ call: $\log(100.16438/110)=-0.093646$, so $\text{Mon}=-0.093646/0.0946=\mathbf{-0.990}$ ⇒ OTM, bucket 1, the **worst-performing** moneyness bucket. Compare a $K=110$ *put*: the sign flips to $\mathbf{+0.990}$ ⇒ ITM, and being a put it has $\Delta<0$ and $\rho<0$, landing it in bucket 1 for both — the **best-performing** bucket for both Greeks.
- *Steps 4–6:* if this contract's $\sigma_I=0.33$ sits in the day's top quartile of implied volatility, it joins bucket $s4$. Running Algorithm B on $s4$-only OVI for Market Makers gives, per the paper, the highest-magnitude SR of the four; the pairwise test against bucket $s1$ returns a difference of $\mathbf{-2.88^{***}}$, meaning $s1$'s $|SR|$ is 2.88 lower than $s4$'s — the strongest single result in the paper's whole feature study. Against $s3$ the difference is $-1.75^{**}$; $s4$ versus $s3$ is $-1.13$, not significant.
- *Step 7:* this contract's IV-Volume weight contribution is $\log\left(1+\min\{0.33,2\}\right)\times V=\log(1.33)\times V=\mathbf{0.2852}V$; a $\sigma_I=2.4$ contract would be capped and contribute $\log(3)V=\mathbf{1.0986}V$, i.e. **3.85×** the weight per contract traded.

### Algorithm E — Cross-impact Bonferroni network construction

**Goal:** Discover, without assuming any structure, which assets' option imbalance predicts which *other* assets' equity returns, and test whether the resulting graph has any recognisable topology.

**Inputs:** Market Maker OVI for all $N$ assets over $D$ days; overnight excess returns $\tilde f_{j,d}$ for all $N$ assets; a quantile group $q$; a family-wise significance target. **Outputs:** an $N\times N$ unweighted directed adjacency matrix $G$ (edge $i\to j$ means $i$'s OVI predicts $j$'s returns); edge/density/degree statistics; power-law and Erdős–Rényi test results; centrality rankings.

**Steps:**

1. **Fix the universe.** Keep only assets listed in CRSP for the *entire* window 01 Jan 2015 – 31 Dec 2019, giving $N=\mathbf{1792}$ NYSE assets. Requiring full listing avoids survivorship artefacts inside the window at the cost of imposing one across it.
2. **Enumerate every ordered pair.** There are $N^2=\mathbf{3{,}211{,}264}$ hypotheses, including the $N$ self-loops $(i,i)$ which correspond to the conventional direct-impact test.
3. **Build each pair's return series.** For each pair $(i,j)$, trade asset $j$ on the days when asset $i$'s OVI is in quantile group $q$, going long $j$ when $i$'s OVI is positive and short when negative:
   $$\text{PnL}_{i,j,d}=\mathbb{1}_{Q(i,d,q)}\,\text{sign}(s_{i,d})\,\tilde f_{j,d}$$
   where $Q(i,d,q)=1$ if asset $i$ is in the $q$-th quantile group on day $d$, else 0. Days when $i$'s signal is too weak or absent contribute exactly zero.
4. **Test each pair.** Apply the [Bailey and Lopez de Prado, 2014] SR significance test to $\{\text{PnL}_{i,j,d}\}_{d=1}^{D}$ and set $G_{i,j}=1$ if the $p$-value falls below the threshold, else 0. This is the **Bonferroni-network** / statistically-validated-network idea of [Tumminello et al., 2011], as applied to intraday lead-lag by [Curme et al., 2014], but with cumulative P&L in place of correlation.
5. **Control the family-wise error rate.** With three million tests, an uncorrected 5% threshold would produce ~160,000 false edges. Use a uniform level $\alpha_1=p/(\text{number of tests})$ so that $\text{FWER}\le p$: the paper reports all three of $p=0.05$ (no correction), $p=0.05/N=2.8\times10^{-5}$ (row-level), and $p=0.05/N^2=1.6\times10^{-8}$ (full), and repeats the whole build for $q=1,\dots,5$, yielding **15 networks**.
6. **Compare against null topologies.** Count **self-loops** and **bi-directed edges** and compare to their Erdős–Rényi expectations at the same density (for a density-$\delta$ ER graph, expected self-loops $=N\delta$ and expected bi-directed edges $=\binom{N}{2}\delta^2$). Test whether degrees follow a **power law** with [Clauset et al., 2009]. Test whether degrees are homogeneous (the ER hypothesis) via a two-sample binomial statistic on $\hat p^{\text{IN}}_j=\sum_lG_{l,j}/N$ and $\hat p^{\text{OUT}}_j=\sum_lG_{j,l}/N$, under $d^{\text{IN}}_j\sim\text{Binomial}(N,p^{\text{IN}}_j)$, testing $H_0:p^{\text{IN}}_j=p^{\text{IN}}_i\ \forall i,j$:
   $$\text{Test}_{i,j}=\frac{\hat p_i-\hat p_j}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/(2N)}}\ \sim\ N(0,1)$$
   with Bonferroni $p=0.05/N^2$.
7. **Characterise the surviving structure.** Measure connected components (weak and strong), average in/out degree by sector, and node centralities — in-degree, out-degree, eigenvector, Katz, PageRank, betweenness — and test for sector-sector clustering against a Chung–Lu bipartite null where edge weights are proportional to the product of source out-degree and target in-degree.

**Worked example (end-to-end, with intermediate values).**

- *Step 1:* $N=1792$. *Step 2:* $1792^2=3{,}211{,}264$ pairs.
- *Step 3:* for pair $(i,j)$, on a day when $i$ is in $Q3$ so $\mathbb{1}_Q=1$, with $s_{i,d}=-0.4$ (so $\text{sign}=-1$) and $\tilde f_{j,d}=-0.006$: $\text{PnL}_{i,j,d}=1\times(-1)\times(-0.006)=\mathbf{+0.006}$. On a day when $i$ is outside $Q3$, $\text{PnL}_{i,j,d}=\mathbf{0}$ regardless of $j$'s return. Over 1257 days with ~60% inclusion, roughly **754 non-zero observations** enter the test.
- *Steps 4–5:* the full-Bonferroni threshold is $p=0.05/3{,}211{,}264=\mathbf{1.56\times10^{-8}}$, i.e. $|z|\gtrsim5.54$. At that level the $q=5$ network retains **1207 edges** out of 3.21 M candidate pairs — a density of $1207/3{,}211{,}264=\mathbf{0.038\%}$, which the paper reports as 0.00 to 2 dp. At $q=1$ only **209** edges survive; at $p=0.05$ uncorrected the count explodes to **231,413** edges (density **7%**), and average degree rises from $1207/1792=\mathbf{0.67}$ to $\mathbf{129.14}$.
- *Step 6:* of Q5's 1207 edges, **2 are self-loops** versus an ER expectation of $N\delta=1792\times(1207/1792^2)=1207/1792=\mathbf{0.67}$ — so self-impact is 3× over-represented but is still only **0.17%** of all edges, meaning cross-impact outnumbers direct impact by about **600:1**. Bi-directed edges: **2 observed** versus $\binom{1792}{2}\delta^2=1{,}605{,}336\times(3.759\times10^{-4})^2=\mathbf{0.23}$ expected, about 9× over-represented but again vanishingly rare in absolute terms. *Degree homogeneity:* comparing a node with $d^{\text{IN}}=20$ against one with $d^{\text{IN}}=5$: $\hat p_i=20/1792=0.011161$, $\hat p_j=5/1792=0.002790$, pooled $\hat p=25/3584=0.006975$, $\hat p(1-\hat p)=0.0069264$; as printed the denominator is $\sqrt{0.0069264/(2\times1792)}=\sqrt{1.9326\times10^{-6}}=\mathbf{1.390\times10^{-3}}$ and $\text{Test}=0.008371/0.001390=\mathbf{6.02}>5.54$, so the pair **rejects** homogeneity. Aggregated, the rejection rate is **0%** for the fully corrected networks, **5–12%** at row level, **41–58%** uncorrected — so degrees are demonstrably heterogeneous in the larger networks. *Power law:* rejected for in-degrees in 5 of 15 networks and for out-degrees in 6 of 15; the Q3 row-level network's in-degrees give $p=0$, out-degrees give a fitted exponent of **3.75** without rejection.
- *Step 7:* the Q3 row-level network has **one giant component** — weakly connected it covers all but 2 of 1792 instruments; strongly connected it covers **942**. **Transportation** has the highest average in-degree and lowest out-degree (a pure receiver); **Communications** and **ETFs** have far above-average out-degrees (pure senders). Concrete nodes: **GLD** has out-degree 34 and in-degree 0 (broadcasts, never receives); **INN** out-degree 42; **CL** out-degree 38 with betweenness 69,515; **TKC** in-degree 20 with Katz centrality 2.946. The Chung–Lu sector null finds **no sector pair** significantly different from observed, so there is **no sector-level cluster structure**.

### Algorithm F — Approximating trade flow between market participant classes

**Goal:** Estimate who traded with whom, given only per-class aggregate buy and sell volumes in 10-minute buckets rather than matched transaction records.

**Inputs:** For every asset $i$, contract $j$, day $d$, and 10-minute window $t\in\{1,\dots,39\}$: the volume bought $V^{\text{Buy},t}_{i,j,d,m}$ and sold $V^{\text{Sell},t}_{i,j,d,m}$ by each MPC $m$; the contract price $P_{i,j,d}$. **Outputs:** a $5\times5$ (or $9\times9$ once opening/closing intents are split out) matrix $\widetilde{NF}_{m_1,m_2}$ of median nominal flow proportions, one per exchange and per option type.

**Steps:**

1. **Exploit the finest available time slice.** Work window-by-window rather than day-by-day: within a 10-minute window the set of participants is small, so the matching ambiguity is much smaller than over a full day.
2. **Resolve the unambiguous windows exactly.** If in a given window exactly one MPC is buying and/or exactly one is selling a particular contract, the counterparty is determined and the flow can be recorded directly with no modelling.
3. **Model the ambiguous windows as a bipartite graph.** When several MPCs trade the same contract in the same window, place their buy volumes as left nodes and their sell volumes as right nodes; an edge's weight is the volume transacted between that buyer class and that seller class. Following the **Chung–Lu** generative model for bipartite graphs [Benson et al., 2020], assume flow is **proportional to the product of buyer and seller volume** — i.e. each buyer's volume is split across sellers in proportion to those sellers' share of total selling.
4. **Accumulate nominal flow across the whole data set.** Weight by contract price to get dollar flow, and sum over contracts, assets and windows:
   $$NF_{d,m_1,m_2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}\sum_{j=1}^{C_{i,d}}\sum_{t=1}^{39}P_{i,j,d}\,V^{\text{Buy},t}_{i,j,d,m_1}\times\frac{V^{\text{Sell},t}_{i,j,d,m_2}}{\sum_{m'}V^{\text{Sell},t}_{i,j,d,m'}}$$
5. **Normalise each day to a probability distribution** so that $\sum_{m_1}\sum_{m_2}NF_{d,m_1,m_2}=1$ for every day, removing the effect of day-to-day volume swings.
6. **Take the median across days** for robustness against outlier sessions:
   $$\widetilde{NF}_{m_1,m_2}=\text{median}\left(\frac{NF_{d,m_1,m_2}}{\sum_{m'_1,m'_2}NF_{d,m'_1,m'_2}};\ d=1,\dots,D\right)$$
7. **Read the matrix.** Compare cells across exchanges and between calls and puts; check symmetry ($\widetilde{NF}_{m_1,m_2}$ versus $\widetilde{NF}_{m_2,m_1}$) to see whether relationships are reciprocal.

**Worked example (end-to-end, with intermediate values).** One contract, one 10-minute window, $P_{i,j,d}=\$2.50$. Buys: Market Maker 300, Customer 700. Sells: Market Maker 600, Broker 400.

- *Step 2:* two buyers and two sellers, so the window is **ambiguous** — proceed to step 3.
- *Step 3:* total selling $\sum_{m'}V^{\text{Sell}}=600+400=\mathbf{1000}$, so the seller shares are Market Maker $600/1000=\mathbf{0.60}$ and Broker $400/1000=\mathbf{0.40}$. Each buyer's volume is split in those proportions.
- *Step 4:* $NF_{\text{MM buy},\text{MM sell}}=2.50(300)(0.60)=\mathbf{450}$; $NF_{\text{MM buy},\text{Broker sell}}=2.50(300)(0.40)=\mathbf{300}$; $NF_{\text{Cust buy},\text{MM sell}}=2.50(700)(0.60)=\mathbf{1050}$; $NF_{\text{Cust buy},\text{Broker sell}}=2.50(700)(0.40)=\mathbf{700}$. Sum $=\mathbf{2500}$, which equals $P\times\sum_mV^{\text{Buy}}_m=2.50\times1000$ — a useful internal check, since the seller shares sum to 1 by construction.
- *Step 5:* normalised proportions are $\mathbf{0.18}$, $\mathbf{0.12}$, $\mathbf{0.42}$, $\mathbf{0.28}$, summing to 1. ✓
- *Step 6:* repeating over all $D=1258$ days and taking the median per cell yields $\widetilde{NF}$. In the real data the largest NOTO call cells are around **0.18** and **0.14** in the Market Maker / Customer-Opening region, with PHOTO's corresponding cells at **0.13** and **0.07**.
- *Step 7:* reading the real matrix — call and put structures are near-identical; high $\widetilde{NF}_{m_1,m_2}$ implies high $\widetilde{NF}_{m_2,m_1}$ (relationships are reciprocal); almost all flow involves a Customer and/or a Market Maker; Firms and Professional Customers trade almost exclusively with Market Makers; Brokers trade more with Customers (PHOTO 0.08) than with Market Makers (0.02); and the Firms share is much higher on PHOTO than NOTO while the Market Maker share is higher on NOTO.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **Overnight-gap tilt from participant-tagged option flow** — Take Algorithm A (steps 2 and 4, the Up/Down split and the normalised difference) restricted to the **Market Maker** class, and feed its sign, *inverted*, into Algorithm B (steps 1 and 4) against close-to-open excess returns. The whole idea needs nothing but a participant-tagged daily volume feed and an overnight holding window, so it can be dropped into any equity book that already holds positions across the close. The natural extension is to other venues and asset classes with participant-tagged derivative volume (index futures, FX options) where the same "market makers are the mirror of everyone else" logic should hold.
  - **Strong:** *Close-to-open (overnight) horizon, market-excess returns.* — The paper's return-mode comparison gives Market Maker Q3 an $|SR|$ of **4.42** overnight versus **3.08** close-to-close and only **1.16** open-to-close, and shows close-to-close predictability is inherited from the overnight leg. *(From paper — Section 5, Figure 8)*
  - **Strong:** *Wide cross-sections of several hundred names per day.* — For uniform bets $b=1/n$ the daily P&L is $\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i$ with variance $\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\left(1+(n-1)\bar\rho\right)$, so $SR\propto\sqrt{n/(1+(n-1)\bar\rho)}$: breadth alone predicts a Q3-over-Q5 Sharpe ratio of $\sqrt{532/313}=\mathbf{1.30}$ for near-zero residual correlation, and the paper observes $4.42/3.55=\mathbf{1.25}$. The idea therefore needs a universe of hundreds, not tens, of names. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Any horizon that includes the next intraday session.* — Open-to-close Market Maker $SR$ collapses to $-1.16$, and on the NOTO data set the overnight and intraday $SR$s carry **opposite signs**, indicating a reversal that eats the gap profit. *(From paper — Sections 5 and 7.3, Figure 18)*
  - **Weak:** *Any cost regime above roughly one basis point per side.* — Gross $PPD$ at Q3 is **1.9 bp per day** on a one-dollar book turned over daily, i.e. two round-trip legs. Break-even therefore requires an all-in effective half-spread plus fees below $1.9/2=\mathbf{0.95}$ bp per side; the paper states outright that costs are excluded. Small caps, where half-spreads routinely exceed 5 bp, are net-negative by construction. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Single-name deployment.* — Only **148 of 1792** instruments reject $SR=0$ at the 5% level individually, and just **one** survives Bonferroni. Against a pure-noise null, $1792\times0.05=\mathbf{89.6}$ rejections are expected with $\text{sd}=\sqrt{1792(0.05)(0.95)}=9.22$, so $z=(148-89.6)/9.22=\mathbf{6.3}$ — real, but only about **58 names** of genuine signal out of 1792. This is a portfolio effect, not a stock-picking one. *(From AI, using the paper's Appendix D count)*

- **Implied-volatility-conditioned feature weighting** — Reuse Algorithm D (step 2, the implied-volatility inversion, and step 7, the IV-Volume weight) as a *generic* quality filter on any order-flow feature built from a derivative chain: compute $\sigma_I$ per contract, keep only the top quartile, and pass the filtered flow into Algorithm A step 3. It stands alone — no other idea here is required — and transfers directly to crypto option chains or single-stock variance products where implied vol is observable.
  - **Strong:** *High-implied-volatility contracts, across every significant participant class.* — The top-$\sigma_I$ bucket is best for Brokers, Market Makers **and** Customers, with $\sigma_1$ versus $\sigma_4$ at $\mathbf{-2.88^{***}}$ and $\sigma_1$ versus $\sigma_3$ at $-1.75^{**}$; the top bucket even beats the *unfiltered* data set on $PPD$ for two of three classes. *(From paper — Section 6, Figures 13 and 15)*
  - **Strong:** *When the objective is profit-per-dollar rather than Sharpe.* — Expected P&L per name is $(2p-1)\,\mathbb{E}|\tilde f|$ where $p$ is the directional hit rate, and $\mathbb{E}|\tilde f|\propto\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$ for a roughly symmetric return. Tilting toward high-$\sigma$ names therefore scales the *mean* with $\sigma$ while leaving $p$ untouched — and scales the *standard deviation* by the same factor, so $SR$ does not improve. This predicts exactly the paper's result: IV-Volume gives the best $PPD$ of all six schemes ($-3.9$ bp at Q5, **2.3×** Uniform's $-1.7$ bp) but a *worse* $SR$ ($-2.95$ versus $-3.66$, i.e. **0.81×**). *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *When used as a bet-sizing weight rather than a filter.* — The log-and-cap transform $\log\left(1+\min\{\sigma,2\}\right)$ spans only $\log(1.15)=0.140$ to $\log(1.80)=0.588$ over a realistic $\sigma\in[0.15,0.80]$, a **4.2×** range, while the multiplying volume term spans three or more orders of magnitude across assets. The IV term is therefore a rounding error on top of the Volume scheme — and the Volume scheme is one the paper shows is significantly **beaten** by Uniform on Sharpe ($-0.51^*$ at Q3). Use the IV signal to *select* contracts, not to size bets. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Illiquid or near-expiry option chains.* — $\sigma_I$ is recovered by inverting a Black–Scholes price whose vega $\mathcal{V}=S\phi(d_1)\sqrt\tau\to0$ near expiry; at $\tau=2$ days and $S=100$, $\mathcal{V}=100(0.399)\sqrt{2/365}=\mathbf{2.95}$ per unit $\sigma$, so a single \$0.05 tick of pricing error moves $\sigma_I$ by $0.05/2.95=\mathbf{1.7}$ vol points, and at $\tau=1$ day by 2.4 points. The paper's $\min\{\sigma,2\}$ cap is an admission of this; short-dated illiquid contracts will be mis-bucketed. *(From AI)*

- **P&L regression as a general-purpose directional signal combiner** — Lift Algorithm C wholesale (steps 3 through 8: logistic activation, smoothed bet size, smoothed $\ell_1$, ADAM, unit-norm rescaling) and apply it to any problem where correlated, sparse features must be blended into a *sign* decision — order-book imbalance stacks, sentiment scores, cross-sectional momentum panels. Nothing about it is options-specific; the only requirement is a per-period cross-section and a realised return.
  - **Strong:** *Correlated and sparse feature sets where OLS cannot rank covariates.* — The paper's own OLS run finds every OVI significant but with $R^2$ so low the features cannot be ordered, while the P&L regression produces an interpretable ranking and lifts out-of-sample $PPD$ from **1.33 bp** (best single feature) to **1.68 bp** on the 45-feature model M3. *(From paper — Sections 4.1, 5.2 and 7.3, Figure 19)*
  - **Strong:** *When one hyperparameter should span several sizing conventions.* — Taking limits in equation (8): as $\alpha_1\to\infty$, $g\to\text{sign}$ so $h(g)\to1$ and $b_{i,d}\to1/n$, recovering the **Uniform** scheme exactly; as $\alpha_1\to0$, $g(x)\approx\alpha_1x/2$ so $b_{i,d}\to|s_{i,d}|/\sum_j|s_{j,d}|$, recovering the **Imbalance** scheme exactly. A single continuous knob therefore interpolates the two best-performing schemes in the paper, and can be tuned rather than chosen. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Any setting with strong signals, at the paper's own $\alpha_1=10$.* — Since $g'(x)=\frac{\alpha_1}{2}\left(1-g(x)^2\right)$, at $\alpha_1=10$ we get $g'(0)=5$, $g'(0.5)=5\left(1-\tanh^2 2.5\right)=\mathbf{0.133}$, and $g'(1.0)=5\left(1-\tanh^2 5\right)=\mathbf{9\times10^{-4}}$. A unit-magnitude signal contributes roughly **5,500× less gradient** than a near-zero one, so the fitted $\beta$ is driven almost entirely by the weakest signals — precisely the QB1 bucket the paper shows is *uninformative*. Either anneal $\alpha_1$ upward during training or standardise the features first. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Whenever the training window length changes without retuning $\lambda$.* — The objective sums the data term over $l$ days rather than averaging it, so it scales $\propto l$, while $\lambda\sum_k h(\beta_k)$ is $l$-independent. At $l=500$ and ~1.5 bp per day the data term is $500\times1.5\times10^{-4}=0.075$ against a penalty of $0.01\times1=0.01$, i.e. **13%**; halving to $l=250$ doubles the effective regularisation to **27%**, and doubling to $l=1000$ halves it to 6.5%. The paper's claim that different $l$ and $T$ do not significantly alter results therefore holds only over a range where the shrinkage happens not to bind. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Small feature sets where a simpler model already works.* — The paper's own five-MPC model $s^2$ beats the single-feature $s^1$ in-sample (1.57 versus 1.50 bp) and **loses** out-of-sample (1.25 versus 1.33 bp); the objective is non-convex with no closed form, and $\lambda$ must be chosen. *(From paper — Section 5.2, and the explicit disadvantages listed in Section 4.1)*

- **Mid-quantile signal gating as a portable universe rule** — Reuse Algorithm B step 2 on its own: rank the daily cross-section by $|\text{signal}|$, discard the weakest 40%, and trade the remaining top 60% with uniform weights, rather than either trading everything or chasing the extreme tail. This is a single rule that can be bolted onto any existing cross-sectional signal without touching how the signal is computed.
  - **Strong:** *Any signal whose per-name information coefficient rises with magnitude but whose tail is thin.* — Q3 is the best performer on both $PPD$ and $SR$ for **every** MPC except Firms, and the distinct-bucket tests show QB1 is almost uninformative while QB2–QB5 carry the signal. *(From paper — Section 5, Figures 6 and 7)*
  - **Strong:** *When the breadth-versus-edge trade-off has an interior optimum.* — Under the fundamental law of active management $SR\approx IC\sqrt{\text{breadth}}$, moving from Q3 ($n=532$) to Q5 ($n=313$) sacrifices a breadth factor of $\sqrt{532/313}=\mathbf{1.30}$, so the top quintile's per-name $IC$ would have to be more than **30% higher** to win. The observed Sharpe ratio $4.42/3.55=1.25$ says it is not — a directly checkable justification for stopping at the 60th percentile rather than the 80th or 100th. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Days when the entire cross-section is weak.* — Quantile ranking is **scale-invariant**: multiplying every $|s_{i,d}|$ by any $c>0$ leaves the Q-groups bit-identical, so the rule cannot tell a high-conviction day from a low-conviction one and will still deploy the full dollar across 60% of names on a day when every signal is noise. A rule keyed to an *absolute* threshold, or one scaling gross exposure by the cross-sectional dispersion of $|s|$, would not have this failure mode. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *When the optimal cut must itself be chosen honestly.* — Q5 is among the worst on $SR$ and Q1 is worst on $PPD$, so the result depends on landing on the right middle — but the winning cut was selected on the same 2015–2019 sample used to report the performance, with no held-out selection step. *(From paper — Section 5, "Quantile rank portfolios")*

- **Cross-impact routing: predicting other names from one name's option flow** — Build Algorithm E's network (steps 3 to 5) once, then use its out-edges as a *routing table*: when asset $i$'s OVI fires, trade its neighbours $j$ rather than (or alongside) $i$ itself. Because the network is estimated independently of the rest of the pipeline, this idea stands alone and generalises to any lead-lag discovery problem where the candidate relation count is quadratic in universe size.
  - **Strong:** *Universes large enough that cross-relations vastly outnumber self-relations.* — Under the strictest correction the Q5 network keeps **1207 edges** of which only **2 are self-loops**; ETFs and Communications are strong senders (GLD: out-degree 34, in-degree 0), Transportation a strong receiver. The conventional own-asset test is looking at 0.17% of the discoverable structure. *(From paper — Section 7.4, Figures 20 and 30)*
  - **Strong:** *At the fully Bonferroni-corrected threshold, the surviving edges are essentially all real.* — With $N^2=3{,}211{,}264$ tests at $\alpha=0.05/N^2=1.56\times10^{-8}$, the expected number of false edges is $3.21\times10^6\times1.56\times10^{-8}=\mathbf{0.05}$ by construction. So of 1207 edges, fewer than one is a false positive — an unusually clean discovery set to build on. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Trading the selected edges at their estimated strength.* — The edges are chosen as the maximum over 3.21 M candidate strategies, and family-wise error control bounds the *count* of false edges without correcting the **upward bias in the selected edges' estimated Sharpe**. Conditioning a normal estimate on $|z|>5.54$ gives $\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{SR}\mid\text{selected}\right]>SR_{\text{true}}$ by the truncated-normal mean, so realised out-of-sample performance is provably lower than the in-sample figures, and the paper reports no held-out period for the network. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Any attempt to regularise the network with a structural prior.* — The degrees fit **neither** a power law (rejected for in-degrees in 5 of 15 networks, out-degrees in 6 of 15) **nor** Erdős–Rényi (41–58% of pairwise degree tests reject in the uncorrected networks), and a Chung–Lu bipartite null finds **no** significant sector-sector clustering. With no parsimonious generative model available, every one of the 3.21 M candidate edges must be estimated independently from roughly 754 non-zero observations. *(From paper — Section 7.4 and Appendix D)*
