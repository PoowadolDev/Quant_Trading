## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation — the volume–volatility literature openly contradicts itself, and the paper's diagnosis is that everyone has been measuring the wrong object.** Econophysics and econometrics both agree that trading volume and volatility are related, yet the measured correlation is repeatedly reported as **weak** (Karpoff's survey, ref. [4]) and time-lagged analyses produce "a variety of contradictory results" (refs. [5]–[12]). The paper catalogues the disagreement explicitly: Brailsford finds significant cross-correlation between overnight return and volume [14]; Brooks finds only "modest improvements" in forecasting from lagged volume [15]; Clark finds a nearly parabolic volume–volatility relation and models volatility as a **subordinated random process** driven by trading times [16]; Koulakiotis reports a *negative* volatility–volume relation [17]; Lamoureux and Lastrapes find **ARCH effects disappear** once volume enters the variance equation [6]; Sharma finds NYSE returns are best described by **GARCH without** volume as a mixing variable [18]; and Gillemot, Farmer and Lillo show Clark's subordination explains at most a small fraction of observed clustering and leptokurtosis [19]. The paper's thesis is that the ordinary Pearson correlation between volume and volatility is the wrong statistic, and that the real structure lives in the **conditional distribution** of volatility given volume, and specifically in its **upper edge**.

2. **Data and normalization — 30 DJIA stocks, 17 years, 130,410 daily observations, with volume detrended and both quantities z-scored.** The dataset is the 30 constituents of the **Dow Jones Industrial Average** at daily frequency from **April 1990 to June 2007**, giving **130,410 data points** (4,347 trading days per stock). Data after June 2007 is deliberately excluded "due to the potential for high non-stationarity in the volume time series associated with the world financial crisis", though the authors state their results are unchanged when post-2007 data is included. For each stock $i$ the daily logarithmic **return** is

   $$R_i(t) \equiv \ln p_i(t) - \ln p_i(t-1) \tag{1}$$

   where $p_i(t)$ is the closing price of stock $i$ on day $t$. Volume is first **detrended in log space**:

   $$\tilde{Q}_i(t) \equiv \ln Q_i(t) - Y_i \tag{2}$$

   where $Q_i(t)$ is raw traded share volume on day $t$ and $Y_i$ is a **least-squares linear fit of $\ln Q_i$** over the whole 17-year window (methodology referenced to Chambers & Hastie, ref. [20]); subtracting it removes the global growth trend in market activity. *Worked example:* a day with $p_i(t-1)=85.00$ and $p_i(t)=86.70$ gives $R_i(t)=\ln 86.70-\ln 85.00=\ln 1.02=\mathbf{0.019803}$; a raw volume $Q_i(t)=6{,}000{,}000$ gives $\ln Q_i=15.60727$, and if the fitted trend value on that date is $Y_i=15.20$ then $\tilde{Q}_i(t)=15.60727-15.20=\mathbf{0.40727}$.

   Both quantities are then converted to **dimensionless units** — normalized volatility $g$ and normalized logarithmic volume $v$ — and these two definitions are used everywhere in the rest of the paper:

   $$g_i(t) \equiv \left| \frac{R_i(t) - \langle R_i(t)\rangle}{\sigma_R} \right| \tag{3}$$

   $$v_i(t) \equiv \frac{\tilde{Q}_i(t) - \langle \tilde{Q}_i(t)\rangle}{\sigma_{\tilde{Q}}} \tag{4}$$

   with $\langle\cdots\rangle$ a time average over the studied period and the standard deviations defined as $\sigma_R=\sqrt{\langle R^2\rangle-\langle R\rangle^2}$ and $\sigma_{\tilde{Q}}=\sqrt{\langle\tilde{Q}^2\rangle-\langle\tilde{Q}\rangle^2}$. The asymmetry is deliberate: **volatility carries an absolute value so $g\ge 0$, while volume $v$ is signed** and can be negative. Throughout the paper "volume" means $v$ and "volatility" means $g$. *Worked example, continuing point 2:* suppose for this stock $\langle R\rangle=0.0004$ and $\langle R^2\rangle=3.40\times10^{-4}$, so $\sigma_R=\sqrt{3.40\times10^{-4}-(4\times10^{-4})^2}=\sqrt{3.3984\times10^{-4}}=0.018435$; then $g_i(t)=|(0.019803-0.0004)/0.018435|=0.019403/0.018435=\mathbf{1.0525}$. For volume, with $\sigma_{\tilde{Q}}=0.35$, $v_i(t)=(0.40727-0)/0.35=\mathbf{1.1636}$. (The mean subtraction in Eq. 4 is in fact redundant: $\tilde{Q}$ is an OLS residual, so $\sum_t \tilde{Q}_i(t)=0$ exactly and $\langle\tilde{Q}\rangle=0$, making $\sigma_{\tilde{Q}}$ simply the RMS residual.)

3. **Marginal distributions — volume is Gaussian, volatility and returns are not.** Fig. 1(a) shows the pdf of $v$ across the 30 DJIA stocks is in "excellent agreement with a unit Gaussian", consistent with Wang et al.'s finding that a normal curve also fits trading *values* [21]. In contrast, the unconditioned pdf $P(g)$ (inset of Fig. 1(b), log–log) is strongly **leptokurtic** with a tail that "roughly matches a power-law distribution", as reported by Wang, Shieh, Havlin and Stanley [22]; and the return distribution itself is better fit by a **Laplace** than by a Gaussian, agreeing with Podobnik et al. on NYSE stocks [23]. This asymmetry — a well-behaved Gaussian conditioning variable and a heavy-tailed conditioned variable — is what makes the conditional analysis in points 5–6 both possible and necessary. *Worked example of the Gaussian claim:* under a unit Gaussian the fraction of days in a 0.2-wide volume bin centred at $v=0$ is $\varphi(0)\times0.2=0.39894\times0.2=\mathbf{7.98\%}$, whereas the bin centred at $v=2.9$ holds $\varphi(2.9)\times0.2=0.005953\times0.2=\mathbf{0.119\%}$ — a **67-fold** difference in bin population that becomes important in point 6.

4. **The central distributional discovery — the volume-conditional volatility distributions all collapse onto one curve when volatility is measured in units of shifted volume.** Fig. 1(b) plots $P(g|v)$ for eight volume bins ($v=-1.75\pm0.25$ through $v=+1.75\pm0.25$) and they look "very similar" but not identical. Taking inspiration from Yamasaki et al., who collapsed volatility **return-interval** distributions across thresholds $q$ by scaling with the mean return interval [25], the authors search for the volume analogue and find it in a shifted volume variable:

   $$v' \equiv v + 4.5, \qquad\text{collapse:}\quad v'\,P(g\,|\,v) = f\!\left(\frac{g}{v'}\right) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; P(g\,|\,v) = \frac{1}{v'}f\!\left(\frac{g}{v'}\right)$$

   where $f(\cdot)$ is a single universal function independent of $v$, and the offset $4.5$ is chosen purely "to avoid singularities and unphysical values, since normalized volume as defined in Eq. 4 can be a non-positive quantity". Fig. 1(c) shows every conditional distribution landing on the same curve, meaning **all conditional volatility distributions are identical up to a scale factor $v'$**. *Worked example:* at $v=-1$, $v'=3.5$; at $v=+2$, $v'=6.5$. The collapsed abscissa $x=g/v'=0.4$ therefore corresponds to $g=0.4\times3.5=\mathbf{1.4}$ on a low-volume day and to $g=0.4\times6.5=\mathbf{2.6}$ on a high-volume day, and the ordinates satisfy $3.5\,P(1.4|{-}1)=6.5\,P(2.6|2)$, i.e. $P(2.6|v{=}2)=(3.5/6.5)P(1.4|v{=}{-}1)=\mathbf{0.538}\,P(1.4|v{=}{-}1)$. In plain terms: **a $g=2.6$ day at $v=+2$ is the same distributional event as a $g=1.4$ day at $v=-1$** — the volatility scale is $6.5/3.5=\mathbf{1.857\times}$ larger.

5. **The unified functional form — a power law with an exponential cutoff whose two exponents are linear in volume.** After rejecting log-normal and stretched-exponential candidates, and noting in Fig. 1(d) that the tails "are too curved to fit power-laws", the authors settle on a power law with exponential cutoff:

   $$P(g\,|\,v) \sim g^{-\xi} e^{-\varsigma g} \tag{5}$$

   with $\xi$ the power-law exponent and $\varsigma$ the exponential-cutoff rate. Because Eq. 5 must reproduce the $v'$ collapse, they promote volume to a parameter of the distribution by setting $\xi=\alpha v+a$ and $\varsigma=\beta v+b$, giving

   $$P(g, v\,|\,v) \sim g^{-(\alpha v + a)} e^{-(\beta v + b) g} \tag{6}$$

   where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ control how fast the power-law exponent and the cutoff rate respond to volume, and $a,b$ are their values at $v=0$. **Maximum likelihood estimation** on the Fig. 1(b) data yields $\alpha=0.4$, $\beta=-1.23$, $a=-2.5$ and $b=3$ (the PDF prints this as "$a-2.5$", an obvious typo for $a=-2.5$). Fig. 2 draws the resulting contour plot over $g\in[1,10]$, $v\in[-3,3]$ and shows that **decreasing $g$ or increasing $v$ monotonically increases the probability density**, and crucially that "higher volatilities are far more localized in their range of volumes than low volatilities" — low volatility occurs at any volume, high volatility essentially only at high volume, so **high volatility is predictable from volume while low volatility is not**. *Worked example:* at $v=+1$, $\xi=0.4(1)-2.5=-2.1$ and $\varsigma=-1.23(1)+3=1.77$, so $P(g|1)\propto g^{2.1}e^{-1.77g}$; at $g=3$ this is $3^{2.1}e^{-5.31}=10.045\times0.0049409=\mathbf{0.04963}$. At $v=-1$, $\xi=-2.9$ and $\varsigma=4.23$, so $P(3|-1)\propto 3^{2.9}e^{-12.69}=24.192\times3.0817\times10^{-6}=\mathbf{7.455\times10^{-5}}$. The ratio is $0.04963/7.455\times10^{-5}\approx\mathbf{666\times}$: a $g=3$ day is roughly **666 times more likely (in relative density) at $v=+1$ than at $v=-1$**, which is exactly the localization Fig. 2 describes. Adding the normalizer the paper omits, $Z(v)=\int_0^\infty g^{-\xi}e^{-\varsigma g}dg=\Gamma(1-\xi)\varsigma^{\xi-1}$; at $v=0$ this is $\Gamma(3.5)\cdot3^{-3.5}=3.32335/46.7654=\mathbf{0.07106}$, so the proper density is $P(g|0)=14.072\,g^{2.5}e^{-3g}$, which evaluated at its mode $g^\*=-\xi/\varsigma=2.5/3=0.833$ gives $14.072\times0.63393\times0.082085=\mathbf{0.7324}$.

6. **The paper's headline new quantity — Local Maximum Volatility (LMV), the largest volatility ever seen at a given volume level.** Since only the high-volatility part of the distribution is predictable and "days of high volatility are the ones of greatest interest to traders and market researchers", the authors restrict attention to the upper edge of the volume–volatility scatterplot. Partitioning the observed volume range into bins $u_1,u_2,\dots,u_n$, LMV is

   $$g^{\,j}_{LM} \equiv \max(\{g_t\})\;\forall t \mid v_t \in u_j \tag{7}$$

   i.e. $g^{\,j}_{LM}$ is the maximum normalized volatility over all days $t$ whose normalized volume $v_t$ fell in bin $u_j$ — "the volatility of the most volatile day a given trading volume has co-occurred with". The bins are **30 equal-width bins spanning $v\in[-3,3]$** (width 0.2). The scatterplots of $g$ against $v$ (Fig. 3a) and against $v(t-1)$ (Fig. 3b) show a characteristic **triangular shape**, and the LMV points trace its hypotenuse. Measured with the ordinary Pearson correlation

   $$\rho_{XY}=\frac{\sum_j (X_j-\bar{X})(Y_j-\bar{Y})}{\sqrt{\sum_j (X_j-\bar{X})^2}\sqrt{\sum_j (Y_j-\bar{Y})^2}}$$

   the result for **The Boeing Company (BA)** is striking: same-day volume-vs-volatility correlation $\rho=\mathbf{0.50}$ but volume-vs-LMV correlation $\rho_0=\mathbf{0.93}$; using the *previous* day's volume, $\rho=\mathbf{0.20}$ versus $\rho_0=\mathbf{0.55}$. *Worked example of Eq. 7 plus the correlation:* take six volume bins centred at $v=-2.5,-1.5,-0.5,+0.5,+1.5,+2.5$ with observed LMVs $2.6, 2.2, 3.4, 3.3, 5.0, 5.3$. Then $\bar{v}=0$, $\sum v^2=17.5$, $\bar{g}_{LM}=21.8/6=3.6333$, $\sum v\,g_{LM}=10.90$, $\sum(g_{LM}-\bar{g}_{LM})^2=7.9333$, giving $\rho_0=10.90/\sqrt{17.5\times7.9333}=10.90/11.7828=\mathbf{0.925}$ — reproducing the paper's 0.93 to two figures. The authors also verify (Figs. 3c, 3d) that the LMV days are **spread across 1991–2007** rather than clustered in one volatile week, "which ensures the universal representativeness of LMV".

7. **LMV keeps its predictive correlation at a lag, while raw volatility loses it almost immediately.** Generalizing the same-day and one-day analysis to lags up to **16 days** and averaging over all 30 DJIA stocks, Fig. 4 shows the volume-vs-volatility correlation **collapses to roughly zero for almost any nonzero lag**, whereas the volume-vs-LMV correlation retains $\approx\mathbf{0.4}$ at a **one-day lag** and is still $\approx\mathbf{0.2}$ at a **four-day lag** (error bars are $\pm 1$ standard deviation across the 30 stocks). The authors read this as "significant potential for predicting days of potential largest volatility, and therefore largest risk, which is extremely important in protecting investments during a financial crisis" [27]. They pre-empt the objection that this is just volatility clustering in disguise, noting that Gillemot et al. already showed volatility clustering is *not* a simple volume effect from reduced average trading time [19], and promising the Fig. 5 evidence that volume adds non-redundant information. *Worked example of the lag decay:* if the LMV–volume correlation decays roughly geometrically from $\rho_0(0)\approx 0.9$, then $\rho_0(\tau)=0.9\,\lambda^\tau$ with $\rho_0(1)=0.4$ implies $\lambda=0.4/0.9=0.444$, predicting $\rho_0(4)=0.9\times0.444^4=0.9\times0.0389=\mathbf{0.035}$ — far below the observed $\approx0.2$, so the empirical decay is **much slower than geometric**, i.e. the memory is long-ranged rather than exponential.

8. **Single-variable conditioning: days before extreme volatility carry a visible volume signature, days before calm markets carry none.** Fig. 5(a) shows the quintile distribution $P(v(t)\,|\,g_0(t+1))$ and Fig. 5(b) shows $P(g(t)\,|\,g_0(t+1))$, where $g_0(t+1)$ is the subset of days whose *next* day falls in the **highest 1%** or **lowest 1%** of volatility, averaged over all 30 stocks. With no memory both would be flat at 20% per quintile. Instead, for the highest-1% case the **top 20% of volumes account for roughly 40% of the preceding days** (a $40\%/20\%=\mathbf{2.0\times}$ overrepresentation) while the **bottom 20% of volumes account for only about 10%** ($10\%/20\%=\mathbf{0.5\times}$), and the effect is **monotone across the quintiles**. For the lowest 1% of volatilities there is *no* such effect — the distribution of preceding volumes is statistically indistinguishable from flat, which is exactly what Fig. 2's contour plot predicted (low volatilities span the whole volume range and so cannot be inferred from it). The same asymmetry holds when conditioning on the *previous day's volatility* instead of volume. Summarized by the authors: "high volatility tends to follow high volatility, but also high volatility tends to follow high volume. No such significant effects can be observed for low volatility."

9. **Joint conditioning on both volume and volatility beats either alone — a factor-of-three lift versus a factor-of-two.** Figs. 5(c) and 5(d) plot the joint conditional probability

    $$P\big(g(t+1)\in g_0 \,\big|\, v_n(t),\, g_n(t)\big), \qquad n=1,\dots,5$$

    where $v_n(t)$ and $g_n(t)$ are the quintile of today's volume and today's volatility, $g_0$ is the set of smallest 1% (panel c) or largest 1% (panel d) next-day volatilities, and probabilities are expressed **in units of the unconditioned $P(g(t+1)=A)=1\%$**; bubble radius stands in for colour intensity in what is effectively a $5\times5$ heat map. The top-quintile cell reaches $P[g(t+1)=p\,|\,v_5(t),g_5(t)]\approx\mathbf{3\times}$ the unconditioned probability, while the bottom-quintile cell $P[g(t+1)=p\,|\,v_1(t),g_1(t)]$ is only $\approx\mathbf{0.5\times}$ — a **6:1 spread** between the most and least dangerous joint states. Compared with the $\approx2\times$ maximum from single-variable conditioning in point 9, this is a clear improvement, and the fact that bubble size varies **along both rows and columns** shows each variable carries information the other does not. The authors confirm this with "a simple multiregression model predicting next-day volatility from either volatility alone or volatility and volume together" and report an **average 6% increase in the $R^2$ value** when volume is included. Panel (c), for days preceding the *lowest* 1% volatility, shows markedly reduced variation and "no clear pronounced trends across row or column". *Worked example:* the top-1% target set has $0.01\times130{,}410=\mathbf{1{,}304}$ days; splitting into $5\times5=25$ cells the paper takes $1{,}304/25\approx\mathbf{52}$ days per cell as the no-memory baseline, i.e. $52.2/5{,}216=1.0\%$ conditional probability. A $3\times$ lift means $\approx\mathbf{157}$ of the 1,304 land in the $(v_5,g_5)$ cell and a $0.5\times$ deficit means $\approx\mathbf{26}$ land in $(v_1,g_1)$; under a Poisson null with mean 52.2 the standard deviation is $\sqrt{52.2}=7.22$, so $z=(157-52.2)/7.22=\mathbf{+14.5}$ and $z=(26-52.2)/7.22=\mathbf{-3.6}$ — both far outside sampling noise.

10. **Interpretation and stated limitations — LMV as a behavioural quantity, and the "risk of risk".** The conclusion frames LMV as the resolution of a psychological puzzle: investors believe high-volume days are dangerous, yet the measured volume–volatility correlation is "unable to explain the importance of trading volume" [37] and could be dismissed as **illusory correlation** [29]. The strong volume–LMV correlation says the investor's intuition is tracking a real quantity, just not the one economists measure. The authors connect this to **negativity bias** [30] — Baumeister et al.'s "bad is stronger than good" [31] — and to the documented **volatility asymmetry** in which negative price changes raise volatility more than positive ones [32]–[36]: because an investor "does not know the next-day volatility level in advance", the relevant object is the **worst case**, the "risk of risk", which is precisely what LMV measures. Limitations the paper itself states or implies: post-June-2007 crisis data is excluded on non-stationarity grounds; low volatility is explicitly *not* predictable from volume; all $\rho_0$ figures are computed on **binned maxima** rather than raw days; the multiregression is reported only as an average $R^2$ delta with no coefficients, standard errors, baseline $R^2$, or out-of-sample test; and no trading or risk-management backtest is attempted. *Worked example of the $R^2$ claim:* if $R^2$ increments decompose as $R^2_{\text{full}}=R^2_0+(1-R^2_0)\rho_p^2$ with $\rho_p$ the partial correlation of volume, then a **relative** 6% gain on a plausible AR-type baseline $R^2_0=0.2025$ (autocorrelation 0.45 in $|R|$) means $\Delta R^2=0.06\times0.2025=0.01215$ and $\rho_p=\sqrt{0.01215/(1-0.2025)}=\sqrt{0.015235}=\mathbf{0.123}$ — statistically overwhelming at $n=130{,}410$ ($t\approx0.123\times\sqrt{130{,}407}/\sqrt{1-0.0152}\approx\mathbf{44.8}$) but economically modest.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### The measurement problem: why the volume–volatility literature disagrees with itself

The paper's opening move is not a new model but a claim about **what has been measured wrong**. Two decades of studies computed a single Pearson correlation between volume and volatility (or ran volume into a GARCH variance equation) and got answers ranging from significantly positive [14] through negligible [15] to negative [17], with Lamoureux and Lastrapes even reporting that ARCH effects *vanish* when volume is added [6] while Sharma et al. report the opposite [18]. Zheng et al. argue this is what you should expect when the true relationship is **not about the centre of the volatility distribution at all**: since low volatility occurs at every volume level (Fig. 2), including calm days in the correlation dilutes a genuine high-volatility effect down to $\rho\approx0.2$–$0.5$.

**Why it matters:** It reframes a **null result as an aggregation artifact**. If the volume signal lives only in the upper tail of the conditional volatility distribution, then any statistic that averages over all days — correlation, OLS beta, GARCH variance coefficient — is **guaranteed** to understate it, and the literature's inconsistency becomes evidence for the paper's thesis rather than against it. The practical consequence is direct: a risk system should condition on volume when estimating **tail** volatility and should not bother when estimating **average** volatility.

### Scale invariance of the volume-conditional volatility distribution

The paper's cleanest empirical result is that $P(g|v)$ is not a family of different distributions but **one distribution stretched by one number**. Plotting $v'P(g|v)$ against $g/v'$ with $v'=v+4.5$ collapses eight volume-conditional distributions, spanning $v=-1.75$ to $+1.75$, onto a single curve (Fig. 1c). This is a direct structural analogue of Yamasaki et al.'s collapse of volatility return-interval distributions across thresholds [25], with shifted volume playing the role that mean return interval plays there.

**Why it matters:** A one-parameter scale family is **enormously more useful than a correlation**. It says the entire shape of tomorrow's volatility distribution — every quantile, every VaR level, every option-relevant moment — can be obtained by reading one number off today's volume and rescaling a single **universal** curve. It also means the curve can be estimated once, pooling all volume regimes and therefore all 130,410 observations, instead of separately per volume bin where the tail bins have only a handful of points. That is a **statistical-efficiency** gain, not just an aesthetic one.

### A conditional tail law whose exponents move with volume, and the resulting asymmetry of predictability

Eq. 6, $P \sim g^{-(\alpha v+a)}e^{-(\beta v+b)g}$ with $\alpha=0.4$, $\beta=-1.23$, $a=-2.5$, $b=3$, is the paper's parametric statement of the same fact. Because $\beta<0$, raising volume **shrinks the exponential cutoff rate**, pushing the tail out; because $\alpha>0$, raising volume also steepens the power-law prefactor slightly. The Fig. 2 contour plot makes the consequence visible: iso-probability contours are **wide in $v$ at low $g$ and narrow in $v$ at high $g$**. High volatility is confined to high volume; low volatility is everywhere.

**Why it matters:** This is the paper's key **asymmetry**, and it is what makes the whole enterprise falsifiable and useful. It predicts that volume-based forecasting should succeed for extremes and **fail** for calm periods — a prediction the paper then confirms independently in Figs. 5(a)–(d), where the high-1% panels show strong structure and the low-1% panels are flat. Any system built on this must therefore be a **one-sided** risk detector: it warns of storms, it never certifies calm.

### Local Maximum Volatility (LMV) as the object that actually tracks volume

LMV, $g^j_{LM}=\max\{g_t : v_t\in u_j\}$ (Eq. 7), replaces "the volatility on a given day" with "the worst volatility ever recorded at this volume level". For Boeing this single change lifts the measured correlation with same-day volume from **0.50 to 0.93** and with previous-day volume from **0.20 to 0.55**; across all 30 stocks it preserves $\approx0.4$ at one-day lag and $\approx0.2$ at four-day lag where the raw correlation is essentially zero (Fig. 4). The authors check the obvious confound — that the LMV days might all come from one volatile week — and show in Figs. 3(c),(d) that they are spread across the full 1991–2007 span.

**Why it matters:** It provides a **concrete, computable definition of "risk of risk"**, and it is the bridge between the statistics and the behavioural argument. It also changes the *unit of analysis* from a day to a volume bin, which is the source of both its power and its main statistical vulnerability (see the order-statistic caveats in Section 5). For a practitioner, LMV is directly interpretable: if $g^j_{LM}$ is the max over $n_j$ days in bin $j$, it is an empirical estimate of the $(1-1/n_j)$ quantile of volatility conditional on that volume level — a **conditional VaR envelope indexed by volume**.

### Joint conditioning: volume and volatility carry non-redundant information

The Fig. 5(c)/(d) bubble maps are the paper's answer to the strongest objection against it — that volume merely proxies for yesterday's volatility, which is already known to predict today's. Conditioning on **both** quintiles simultaneously yields a $3\times$ lift in the probability of a top-1% volatility day, against a maximum of $2\times$ for either variable alone, and the bubble sizes vary along **both** the volume axis and the volatility axis. The multiregression cross-check gives an average **+6% $R^2$** from adding volume.

**Why it matters:** Non-redundancy is what makes volume worth adding to an existing volatility model. If volume were a pure proxy, the joint lift would equal the larger of the two marginal lifts; the observed $3\times>2\times$ is **evidence of genuine incremental information**, and it is reinforced by the authors' invocation of Gillemot et al. [19], who independently showed volatility clustering is not reducible to a volume/trading-time effect. This is also the single most directly implementable result in the paper — a $5\times5$ lookup table is a deployable risk gate.

### The behavioural reading: negativity bias and why traders are right about volume

The conclusion argues that LMV, not volatility, is "a more accurate representation of an investor's memory". Humans exhibit **negativity bias** [30], [31] — disproportionate attention to threats — and markets exhibit the corresponding **volatility asymmetry** in which negative price moves raise volatility more than positive ones [32]–[36]. An investor who remembers the worst thing that happened at a given volume level, rather than the average thing, is computing something close to LMV, and since that quantity really does correlate with volume at $\rho_0\approx0.9$, the investor's conviction that high volume means danger is **justified** rather than an instance of illusory correlation [29].

**Why it matters:** It supplies a **testable mechanism** for a folk belief, which is rarer than it sounds — most behavioural-finance claims are either unfalsifiable or reduce to documenting a bias. Here the claim is: human perception encodes a max-statistic, max-statistics of volatility genuinely correlate with volume, therefore the belief is calibrated to a real regularity even though the standard statistic says otherwise. It also **reframes the target variable** for anyone building risk tooling for human users: the number that matches a trader's intuition and the number a statistician reports are different objects, and dashboards should probably show the former.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

### 4.1 Normalization pipeline — turning raw prices and volumes into $g$ and $v$

**Goal:** Convert raw closing prices and raw share volumes for 30 heterogeneous stocks into two dimensionless, cross-sectionally comparable, trend-free series so that data from all stocks can be pooled into one distribution.

**Inputs:** Daily closing price series $p_i(t)$ and daily raw share volume $Q_i(t)$ for each of the 30 DJIA stocks, April 1990 – June 2007. **Outputs:** Normalized volatility $g_i(t)\ge0$ and normalized logarithmic volume $v_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}$, both approximately unit-variance, for 130,410 stock-days.

**Steps:**

1. **Compute log returns.** For each stock take the daily logarithmic price change $R_i(t)\equiv\ln p_i(t)-\ln p_i(t-1)$ (Eq. 1). Logs are used so that returns are additive over time and roughly scale-free in the price level, and so that a 10% rise and a 10% fall are symmetric in magnitude.
2. **Log-transform and detrend volume.** Take $\ln Q_i(t)$, then subtract $Y_i$, the **least-squares straight-line fit of $\ln Q_i$ over the entire 17-year window**: $\tilde{Q}_i(t)\equiv\ln Q_i(t)-Y_i$ (Eq. 2). The log removes the multiplicative, right-skewed nature of raw volume; the linear detrend removes the secular growth in market activity over 17 years, which would otherwise make late-period days look like "high volume" purely because of the calendar.
3. **Standardize volatility.** Form $g_i(t)\equiv|(R_i(t)-\langle R_i\rangle)/\sigma_R|$ with $\sigma_R=\sqrt{\langle R^2\rangle-\langle R\rangle^2}$ (Eq. 3). Demeaning removes each stock's drift; dividing by $\sigma_R$ puts a volatile stock and a sleepy stock on the same scale; the absolute value converts a signed return into a **magnitude of movement**, which is what "volatility" means here.
4. **Standardize volume.** Form $v_i(t)\equiv(\tilde{Q}_i(t)-\langle\tilde{Q}_i\rangle)/\sigma_{\tilde{Q}}$ with $\sigma_{\tilde{Q}}=\sqrt{\langle\tilde{Q}^2\rangle-\langle\tilde{Q}\rangle^2}$ (Eq. 4). No absolute value is taken, so $v$ is **signed**: negative means quieter than this stock's typical day, positive means busier. Note $\langle\tilde{Q}_i\rangle=0$ identically because OLS residuals sum to zero, so this step is really just a division by the RMS residual.
5. **Pool across stocks.** Because both series are now $z$-scores, all 30 stocks' observations are stacked into one sample of 130,410 points for the distributional work. The paper verifies in Fig. 1(a) that the pooled $v$ is well fit by a **unit Gaussian**, which is the consistency check that the normalization worked.

**Worked example (one stock-day, end to end):**

| Step | Quantity | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Input | $p_i(t-1)$, $p_i(t)$ | 85.00, 86.70 |
| Input | $Q_i(t)$ | 6,000,000 shares |
| Input | fitted trend $Y_i$ at this date | 15.20 |
| Assumed stock stats | $\langle R\rangle$, $\langle R^2\rangle$, $\sigma_{\tilde{Q}}$ | $4.0\times10^{-4}$, $3.40\times10^{-4}$, 0.35 |
| 1 | $R_i(t)=\ln 86.70-\ln 85.00$ | $\ln 1.02 = 0.0198026$ |
| 2 | $\ln Q_i(t)$ | $15.607270$ |
| 2 | $\tilde{Q}_i(t)=15.607270-15.20$ | $0.407270$ |
| 3 | $\sigma_R=\sqrt{3.40\times10^{-4}-1.6\times10^{-7}}$ | $\sqrt{3.3984\times10^{-4}}=0.0184347$ |
| 3 | $g_i(t)=|0.0198026-0.0004|/0.0184347$ | $0.0194026/0.0184347 = \mathbf{1.0525}$ |
| 4 | $v_i(t)=0.407270/0.35$ | $\mathbf{1.1636}$ |

So this day is a **1.05-sigma volatility day at a +1.16-sigma volume level** — a moderately busy day with an entirely unremarkable price move, which is exactly the kind of observation that dilutes the raw correlation.

### 4.2 Conditional-distribution scaling and the power-law-with-cutoff fit

**Goal:** Establish that the distribution of volatility given volume is a **single universal shape rescaled by volume**, and produce a closed-form parametric law for it.

**Inputs:** The pooled $(g,v)$ sample from 4.1. **Outputs:** (i) the empirical collapse function $f(\cdot)$ and the scaling variable $v'=v+4.5$; (ii) fitted parameters $\alpha,\beta,a,b$ of Eq. 6; (iii) the qualitative conclusion that high volatility is volume-predictable and low volatility is not.

**Steps:**

1. **Estimate the marginals as a sanity baseline.** Histogram $v$ and confirm it matches a unit Gaussian (Fig. 1a); histogram $g$ on log–log axes and confirm a heavy, roughly power-law tail (Fig. 1b inset). This establishes that the conditioning variable is well behaved and the conditioned variable is not.
2. **Slice by volume and build conditional histograms.** Define volume windows of half-width 0.25 centred at $v=-1.75,-1.25,-0.75,-0.25,+0.25,+0.75,+1.25,+1.75$ and histogram $g$ within each, giving eight curves $P(g|v)$ (Fig. 1b). Visually they are similar but offset — the cue that a scaling relation exists.
3. **Search for the collapse variable.** By analogy with Yamasaki et al. [25], look for a $v$-dependent scale $s(v)$ such that $s(v)P(g|v)$ plotted against $g/s(v)$ is $v$-independent. The complication is that $v$ is a $z$-score and can be **negative or zero**, so $s(v)=v$ is unusable. The fix is a **shift**: $v'\equiv v+4.5$, chosen so that $v'>0$ throughout the data range, "to avoid singularities and unphysical values".
4. **Verify the collapse.** Replot all eight conditional pdfs as $v'P(g|v)$ versus $g/v'$; they land on one curve (Fig. 1c). The implied functional statement is $P(g|v)=(1/v')f(g/v')$ — one universal function $f$, one scale parameter per volume level.
5. **Identify the functional form of the tail.** Offset the eight curves vertically for legibility (Fig. 1d) and inspect the tails. Pure power laws are rejected because the tails "are too curved"; log-normal and stretched-exponential are also tried and rejected; the winner is a **power law with exponential cutoff**, $P(g|v)\sim g^{-\xi}e^{-\varsigma g}$ (Eq. 5).
6. **Make the parameters volume-dependent.** Since the collapse says volume enters the distribution, promote $\xi$ and $\varsigma$ to linear functions of volume, $\xi=\alpha v+a$ and $\varsigma=\beta v+b$, giving Eq. 6.
7. **Fit by maximum likelihood.** Estimate $(\alpha,\beta,a,b)$ from the Fig. 1(b) data by MLE, obtaining $\alpha=0.4$, $\beta=-1.23$, $a=-2.5$, $b=3$.
8. **Read the contour plot for the substantive conclusion.** Draw iso-density contours of Eq. 6 over $g\in[1,10]$, $v\in[-3,3]$ (Fig. 2). Contours at low $g$ span the full $v$ range; contours at high $g$ are squeezed into high $v$. Conclusion: **high volatility is localized in volume and therefore forecastable from it; low volatility is not.**

**Worked example (end to end, with intermediate values):**

Start from the day computed in 4.1: $v=1.1636$, $g=1.0525$.

- Step 3–4, collapse: $v'=1.1636+4.5=\mathbf{5.6636}$; collapsed abscissa $x=g/v'=1.0525/5.6636=\mathbf{0.1859}$. A day with the same collapsed coordinate at $v=-1$ ($v'=3.5$) would have $g=0.1859\times3.5=\mathbf{0.6506}$ — i.e. the *same* distributional rarity is reached at a much smaller raw volatility when volume is low.
- Step 6, parameters at this volume: $\xi=0.4(1.1636)-2.5=0.46544-2.5=\mathbf{-2.0346}$; $\varsigma=-1.23(1.1636)+3=-1.43123+3=\mathbf{1.5688}$.
- Step 7, unnormalized density at the observed $g$: $g^{-\xi}=1.0525^{2.0346}=e^{2.0346\times0.051166}=e^{0.104102}=1.10972$; $e^{-\varsigma g}=e^{-1.5688\times1.0525}=e^{-1.65117}=0.191835$; product $=\mathbf{0.21288}$.
- Same volume, high volatility $g=3$: $3^{2.0346}=e^{2.0346\times1.098612}=e^{2.23525}=9.34899$; $e^{-1.5688\times3}=e^{-4.7064}=0.0090327$; product $=\mathbf{0.08444}$. Ratio to the $g=1.05$ case $=0.08444/0.21288=\mathbf{0.397}$, so at this busy volume level a 3-sigma day is only about **2.5× rarer** than a 1-sigma day.
- Contrast at quiet volume $v=-1$: $\xi=-2.9$, $\varsigma=4.23$. Density at $g=1.05$: $1.05^{2.9}=e^{2.9\times0.048790}=e^{0.141491}=1.15199$; $e^{-4.4415}=0.011758$; product $=\mathbf{0.013545}$. Density at $g=3$: $3^{2.9}=e^{3.18598}=24.1920$; $e^{-12.69}=3.0817\times10^{-6}$; product $=\mathbf{7.455\times10^{-5}}$. Ratio $=7.455\times10^{-5}/0.013545=\mathbf{0.0055}$ — a 3-sigma day is about **180× rarer** than a 1-sigma day when volume is quiet.
- **Interpretation of the intermediate numbers:** the rarity penalty for a 3-sigma day drops from **180×** at $v=-1$ to **2.5×** at $v=+1.16$. That 70-fold change in relative tail weight is the entire content of "volume predicts extreme volatility", expressed in one arithmetic comparison.
- Step 8, normalization for absolute probabilities (not given in the paper): $Z(v)=\Gamma(1-\xi)\varsigma^{\xi-1}$. At $v=0$: $\Gamma(3.5)=3.32335$, $3^{-3.5}=1/46.7654=0.021384$, so $Z(0)=\mathbf{0.071064}$ and $P(g|0)=14.072\,g^{2.5}e^{-3g}$; at the mode $g^\*=-\xi/\varsigma=2.5/3=0.8333$ this gives $14.072\times0.633934\times0.082085=\mathbf{0.7324}$.

### 4.3 LMV construction and lagged correlation analysis

**Goal:** Extract the **upper envelope** of the volume–volatility scatterplot as a function of volume, and measure how strongly and how far into the future volume predicts it.

**Inputs:** Pooled or per-stock $(v_i(t), g_i(t))$ series; a bin partition of the volume axis; a set of time lags $\tau=0,1,\dots,16$ days. **Outputs:** an LMV curve $g^j_{LM}$ over volume bins $j$; the correlation $\rho_0$ between bin-centre volume and LMV; the lag profile $\rho_0(\tau)$ and its raw-correlation counterpart $\rho(\tau)$ averaged over the 30 stocks with $\pm1$ SD error bars.

**Steps:**

1. **Partition the volume axis.** Divide $v\in[-3,3]$ into **30 equal-width bins** $u_1,\dots,u_{30}$ of width $0.2$. The range $[-3,3]$ covers $\approx99.7\%$ of a unit-Gaussian $v$, consistent with Fig. 1(a).
2. **Take the maximum volatility in each bin.** For each bin $j$, collect every day $t$ whose volume $v_t$ fell in $u_j$ and record the single largest volatility observed: $g^j_{LM}\equiv\max(\{g_t\})\;\forall t\mid v_t\in u_j$ (Eq. 7). These are the red triangles of Fig. 3.
3. **Regress LMV on volume and correlate.** Fit a straight line of $g^j_{LM}$ against the bin volume and compute the Pearson correlation $\rho_0$ across the 30 bin points. Compute, for comparison, the ordinary Pearson correlation $\rho$ between $v_t$ and $g_t$ over the **raw daily** observations.
4. **Repeat with a lag.** Re-run steps 1–3 binning on $v(t-\tau)$ instead of $v(t)$, so that the LMV of bin $j$ is the largest volatility seen $\tau$ days *after* a volume reading in that bin. This converts LMV from a descriptive envelope into a **predictive** statement.
5. **Check for temporal clustering.** Plot the calendar dates of the days that supplied the bin maxima (Figs. 3c, 3d). If they clustered in a single volatile week the whole result would be spurious; the paper finds them spread across 1991–2007.
6. **Aggregate across stocks and lags.** Repeat for all 30 DJIA stocks and all lags $\tau\le16$ days, and plot the mean $\rho(\tau)$ and mean $\rho_0(\tau)$ with $\pm1$ SD error bars (Fig. 4).

**Worked example (end to end, with intermediate values):** take six volume bins with three observed days each (a miniature of the paper's 30-bin, thousands-of-days setup).

| Bin centre $v_j$ | Observed $\{g_t\}$ in bin | Step 2: $g^j_{LM}$ |
|---|---|---|
| $-2.5$ | 0.3, 1.1, 2.6 | **2.6** |
| $-1.5$ | 0.2, 0.9, 2.2 | **2.2** |
| $-0.5$ | 0.4, 1.5, 3.4 | **3.4** |
| $+0.5$ | 0.5, 1.3, 3.3 | **3.3** |
| $+1.5$ | 0.6, 1.8, 5.0 | **5.0** |
| $+2.5$ | 0.7, 2.1, 5.3 | **5.3** |

- **Step 3, LMV correlation.** $\bar{v}=0$, $\sum v_j^2=6.25+2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25+6.25=17.5$. $\bar{g}_{LM}=(2.6+2.2+3.4+3.3+5.0+5.3)/6=21.8/6=3.63333$. $\sum v_j g^j_{LM}=-6.5-3.3-1.7+1.65+7.5+13.25=10.90$. $\sum(g^j_{LM}-\bar{g}_{LM})^2=1.06778+2.05444+0.05444+0.11111+1.86778+2.77778=7.93333$. Hence $\rho_0=10.90/\sqrt{17.5\times7.93333}=10.90/\sqrt{138.8333}=10.90/11.78276=\mathbf{0.925}$.
- **Step 3, raw daily correlation on the same 18 days.** $\bar{v}=0$, $\sum v^2=3\times17.5=52.5$. $\sum g=4.0+3.3+5.3+5.1+7.4+8.1=33.2$, so $\bar{g}=33.2/18=1.84444$. $\sum vg=(-2.5)(4.0)+(-1.5)(3.3)+(-0.5)(5.3)+(0.5)(5.1)+(1.5)(7.4)+(2.5)(8.1)=-10.0-4.95-2.65+2.55+11.1+20.25=16.30$. $\sum g^2=8.06+5.69+13.97+12.83+28.60+32.99=102.14$, so $\sum(g-\bar{g})^2=102.14-18(1.84444)^2=102.14-61.2356=40.9044$. Hence $\rho=16.30/\sqrt{52.5\times40.9044}=16.30/\sqrt{2147.48}=16.30/46.3410=\mathbf{0.352}$.
- **Result of the comparison:** $\rho=0.35$ on raw days versus $\rho_0=0.925$ on bin maxima, from **identical data**. This reproduces in miniature exactly the paper's Boeing numbers ($\rho=0.50$, $\rho_0=0.93$) and shows the whole effect comes from switching the statistic from an average to a maximum, not from adding information.
- **Step 4, lagged version.** Re-binning on $v(t-1)$ would, per the paper's Fig. 3(b), give $\rho=0.20$ and $\rho_0=0.55$ for Boeing; averaged over 30 stocks Fig. 4 gives $\rho_0(1)\approx0.4$, $\rho_0(4)\approx0.2$, while $\rho(\tau)\approx0$ for all $\tau\ge1$.
- **Step 5, clustering check.** In the miniature, the six maximum-supplying days should be scattered over the sample period; if all six came from the same month, the LMV curve would be describing one crisis rather than a volume relationship.

### 4.4 Joint conditional-probability predictor for next-day extreme volatility

**Goal:** Test whether today's volume adds information beyond today's volatility for predicting whether **tomorrow** lands in the top (or bottom) 1% of volatility, and quantify the resulting lift.

**Inputs:** Pooled $(v(t), g(t), g(t+1))$ triples across the 30 stocks. **Outputs:** the $5\times5$ conditional-probability table $P(g(t+1)\in g_0\,|\,v_n(t),g_n(t))$ in units of the unconditioned $1\%$, plus a multiregression $R^2$ comparison.

**Steps:**

1. **Define the target event.** Let $g_0$ be the set of days in the **largest 1%** of observed volatility (and separately, the **smallest 1%**). The unconditioned probability is by construction $P(g(t+1)=A)=1\%$, so it becomes the natural unit for expressing every conditional probability as a **lift**.
2. **Single-variable conditioning first.** Split $v(t)$ into quintiles and compute $P(v(t)|g_0(t+1))$ — the distribution of yesterday's volume across quintiles given that today is extreme (Fig. 5a). Do the same for $g(t)$ (Fig. 5b). Under no memory both are flat at 20% per quintile; departures measure predictability.
3. **Cross-tabulate both variables.** Sort days into quintiles $n=1..5$ on volume **and** quintiles $n=1..5$ on volatility, giving a $5\times5$ grid of 25 joint states. For each state count how many of its days are followed by an extreme day, and divide by the state's population to get $P(g(t+1)\in g_0|v_n,g_n)$.
4. **Express as a lift and plot.** Divide each cell probability by the unconditioned $1\%$ and encode the ratio as **bubble radius** — a heat map with size instead of colour (Figs. 5c, 5d). Equal bubbles everywhere would mean no memory effect.
5. **Read the diagonal extremes.** Compare the busiest-and-most-volatile cell $(v_5,g_5)$ against the quietest-and-calmest cell $(v_1,g_1)$, and compare the joint lift against the best single-variable lift from step 2. Also check whether variation appears along **both** axes, which is the test of non-redundancy.
6. **Repeat for the calm target.** Run the same grid for the smallest-1% target (Fig. 5c). If the paper's Fig. 2 conclusion is right, this grid should be nearly flat.
7. **Cross-check with a regression.** Fit a simple multiregression for next-day volatility using (a) today's volatility only and (b) today's volatility and volume, and compare $R^2$.

**Worked example (end to end, with intermediate values):**

- **Step 1.** $N=130{,}410$ stock-days; target set size $=0.01\times130{,}410=\mathbf{1{,}304}$ days; unconditioned probability $=1\%$.
- **Step 2, marginal lifts.** Fig. 5(a): the top volume quintile supplies $\approx40\%$ of the 1,304 preceding days versus the $20\%$ null, a lift of $40/20=\mathbf{2.0\times}$; the bottom quintile supplies $\approx10\%$, a lift of $10/20=\mathbf{0.5\times}$. In counts: $0.40\times1304=\mathbf{522}$ days in the top volume quintile versus $0.20\times1304=\mathbf{261}$ expected.
- **Step 3, joint cells.** $5\times5=25$ cells; under an equal-size assumption each holds $N/25=\mathbf{5{,}216}$ days and expects $1304/25=\mathbf{52.2}$ target days, so the baseline conditional probability is $52.2/5216=\mathbf{1.0\%}$, matching the unconditioned rate as it must.
- **Step 4–5, observed lifts.** $(v_5,g_5)$ shows $\approx3\times$, i.e. conditional probability $\approx\mathbf{3.0\%}$ and count $\approx3\times52.2=\mathbf{157}$ target days. $(v_1,g_1)$ shows $\approx0.5\times$, i.e. $\approx\mathbf{0.5\%}$ and count $\approx\mathbf{26}$. The spread between the two extreme states is $3.0/0.5=\mathbf{6:1}$.
- **Significance of the joint lift.** Under a Poisson null with mean $52.2$, SD $=\sqrt{52.2}=7.223$; the observed 157 is $z=(157-52.2)/7.223=\mathbf{+14.5}$ and the observed 26 is $z=(26-52.2)/7.223=\mathbf{-3.63}$. Both reject the no-memory null decisively.
- **Non-redundancy verdict.** Joint lift $3.0\times$ exceeds the best marginal lift $2.0\times$ by a factor $3.0/2.0=\mathbf{1.5}$. If volume were a pure proxy for volatility the joint lift could not exceed the larger marginal lift, so the excess is the measured value of volume as an **independent** predictor.
- **Step 6, calm target.** The same grid for the smallest-1% target shows bubbles close to uniform and no monotone trend along either axis — i.e. all 25 cells sit near $1.0\times$, so the predictor has **no power on calm days**.
- **Step 7, regression cross-check.** Average $R^2$ rises by $\mathbf{6\%}$ when volume joins volatility. Using $R^2_{\text{full}}=R^2_0+(1-R^2_0)\rho_p^2$ with a baseline $R^2_0=0.2025$ and a *relative* 6% gain, $\Delta R^2=0.01215$ and the incremental partial correlation of volume is $\rho_p=\sqrt{0.01215/0.7975}=\mathbf{0.123}$. Converting to a practical statement: volume shifts the conditional mean of next-day volatility by $\rho_p\times\sigma\approx0.12\sigma$ per unit of volume surprise — small per day, but at a $14.5$-sigma detection level in the tail.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **LMV-based "risk of risk" position cap** — reuse **Section 4.3** wholesale, but stop at step 4 (the lagged version) and turn the output into a live control: maintain a rolling table of $g^j_{LM}$ per volume bin per instrument, read today's $v(t)$, look up the LMV of that bin, and cap position size so that a move of size $g^j_{LM}$ stays inside the loss budget. This replaces the usual "size by trailing realized volatility" rule with "size by the **worst volatility this activity level has ever produced**", which is a strictly more conservative envelope.
  - **Strong:** *Same-day and short-lag regimes across large-cap equities* — the paper measures $\rho_0=0.93$ same-day and $0.55$ at one-day lag for Boeing (Fig. 3a,b), and a 30-stock mean $\rho_0\approx0.4$ at one-day lag still $\approx0.2$ at four-day lag (Fig. 4), while the raw volatility–volume correlation is $\approx0$ at every nonzero lag. *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** *As a quantile estimator the construction is exactly what a cap needs* — a bin maximum over $n_j$ observations is the empirical $(1-1/n_j)$ quantile, so with a central-bin population of $n_j\approx0.0798\times4347\approx347$ days, $g^j_{LM}$ is a direct estimate of the $1-1/347=\mathbf{99.71\%}$ conditional VaR level of volatility given that volume — the exact quantity a hard position cap is defined against, obtained without fitting a tail model. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Thin volume bins, where the cap matters most* — LMV is an extreme order statistic, so its expected value grows with bin population. With $v$ Gaussian and 0.2-wide bins, $\varphi(0)/\varphi(2.9)=0.39894/0.005953=\mathbf{67.0}$, and for a volatility tail $P(g>x)\sim x^{-\zeta}$ the expected maximum of $n$ draws scales as $n^{1/\zeta}$; taking the inverse-cubic $\zeta\approx3$ standard in the cited literature [22], the central bin's LMV is inflated by $67^{1/3}=\mathbf{4.06\times}$ relative to the $|v|=2.9$ bin **purely from sample size**. Since that bias runs *downward* on the high-volume side, it works against the fitted slope on the right and *with* it on the left, so a material share of $\rho_0=0.93$ is an order-statistic artifact rather than a volume effect. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Crisis regimes* — the paper deliberately excludes all data after June 2007 "due to the potential for high non-stationarity in the volume time series associated with the world financial crisis", which is precisely the regime a risk cap exists to survive; its behaviour there is asserted ("results do not change") but never shown. *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** *Any regime, as a statistical estimator* — a maximum has breakdown point $1/n_j$, so a single bad print sets the cap for an entire volume bin; worse, by extreme-value theory the normalized maximum of a $\zeta$-tailed sample converges to a Fréchet law whose coefficient of variation is $\sqrt{\Gamma(1-2/\zeta)-\Gamma(1-1/\zeta)^2}/\Gamma(1-1/\zeta)$, and at $\zeta=3$ that is $\sqrt{2.67894-1.83344}/1.35412=0.91951/1.35412=\mathbf{0.679}$. So LMV carries **~68% relative sampling noise that does not shrink with more data** — unlike a mean, it never stabilizes. *(From AI)*

- **Collapse-consistent conditional tail model** — take **Section 4.2** and replace step 6's linear parameterization with the one the step-4 collapse actually implies. Exact scale invariance $P(g|v)=(1/v')f(g/v')$ with $f(x)\propto x^{-\xi_0}e^{-\kappa x}$ forces the power-law exponent to be **volume-independent** and the cutoff to be **inversely** proportional to $v'$: $\xi(v)=\xi_0$ and $\varsigma(v)=\kappa/(v+4.5)$. Refitting Eq. 5 under that constraint gives a model that is simultaneously consistent with Fig. 1(c) and always a proper density.
  - **Strong:** *Across the whole measured volume range* — Fig. 1(c) shows eight conditional pdfs from $v=-1.75$ to $+1.75$ collapsing onto a single curve, which is direct empirical support for the scale-family form the constrained model imposes. *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** *Small-sample and thin-tail-bin regimes* — the constrained model has **2** free parameters $(\xi_0,\kappa)$ instead of the **4** of Eq. 6 $(\alpha,\beta,a,b)$; at equal maximized likelihood that is an AIC improvement of $2\Delta k=\mathbf{4}$ nats, and it lets the tail be estimated from all volume bins pooled rather than bin-by-bin, which matters most where bins hold only a handful of days. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *High-volume days under the paper's own published parameters* — the fitted $\beta=-1.23$, $b=3$ make the cutoff rate $\varsigma(v)=-1.23v+3$ **cross zero at $v=3/1.23=2.44$**, inside the paper's own $[-3,3]$ binning range. For $v>2.44$, $e^{-\varsigma g}=e^{+|\varsigma|g}$ grows without bound, $Z(v)=\Gamma(1-\xi)\varsigma^{\xi-1}$ is undefined, and Eq. 6 is **not a normalizable density** at all — so the published form fails exactly on the extreme-volume days the paper is about. The divergence follows arithmetically from the paper's own printed values $\beta=-1.23$, $b=3$. *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** *The linear and hyperbolic forms disagree badly away from $v=0$* — matching the two at $v=0$ gives $\kappa=b\,v'|_{v=0}=3\times4.5=13.5$; then at $v=+2$ the collapse-consistent cutoff is $13.5/6.5=\mathbf{2.077}$ while Eq. 6 gives $-1.23(2)+3=\mathbf{0.54}$, a **3.8× discrepancy**. Both cannot describe the same data, so at least one of Fig. 1(c) and Fig. 2 is misfit, and the constrained model must be re-estimated rather than derived from the published numbers. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Small volatilities* — with $a=-2.5$, $-\xi(v)=2.5-0.4v>0$ for all $v<6.25$, so Eq. 6 predicts $P\to0$ as $g\to0$ and a mode at $g^\*=-\xi/\varsigma=(2.5-0.4v)/(3-1.23v)$, which at $v=0$ is $2.5/3=\mathbf{0.833}$. Fig. 1(b) shows a monotonically **decreasing** conditional pdf over the plotted range, so the fit is a tail fit only and must not be extrapolated below $g\approx1$. *(From AI)*

- **$5\times5$ joint volume–volatility regime gate for range strategies** — take the lookup table produced by **Section 4.4** step 4 and use it as a binary switch upstream of any range-bound strategy (grid, mean-reversion, short-gamma): compute today's volume and volatility quintiles, read the cell's lift, and stand down or widen stops when the lift exceeds a threshold. Nothing else in the pipeline changes; it is a pure pre-trade filter, and being a 25-cell table it is trivially auditable and has no fitted parameters beyond the quintile breakpoints.
  - **Strong:** *Detecting imminent extreme-volatility days* — Fig. 5(d) gives $P[g(t+1)\in\text{top }1\%\,|\,v_5,g_5]\approx\mathbf{3\times}$ the unconditioned rate against $\approx\mathbf{2\times}$ for the best single-variable conditioning, with variation along **both** rows and columns, and the authors' multiregression confirms an average **+6% $R^2$** from adding volume. *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** *Statistically robust despite the 1% base rate* — the $(v_5,g_5)$ cell holds $130{,}410/25=5{,}216$ days and expects $52.2$ target days under the no-memory null; the observed $\approx157$ is $z=(157-52.2)/\sqrt{52.2}=\mathbf{14.5}$, and the $(v_1,g_1)$ deficit at $\approx26$ is $z=\mathbf{-3.6}$. The gate's signal is far outside Poisson sampling noise, so it is not a small-cell artifact. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Calm-market detection* — Fig. 5(c), the same grid for the **smallest** 1% of next-day volatility, shows "considerably reduced" bubble variation and "no clear pronounced trends across row or column", so the gate cannot certify a quiet day. Any strategy that needs a *green light* rather than a *red light* gets nothing from it. *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** *Any regime, because of the base rate* — the unconditioned probability is $1\%$, so a $3\times$ lift means the gate fires with a posterior of only $3\%$: **97 of every 100 firings are false alarms**. If a false stand-down costs $c$ and a correctly avoided extreme day saves $s$, expected value is positive only when $0.03s>0.97c$, i.e. $s/c>0.97/0.03=\mathbf{32.3}$. Unless one avoided storm is worth ~32 missed range days, the gate loses money despite being highly significant. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *The reported cell populations cannot be right, which clouds the calibration* — the paper states the 25 joint quintile cells are "equal-sized" with $\approx52$ points each, but it also reports $\rho(v,g)=0.50$ (Fig. 3a). Under a Gaussian copula with $\rho=0.5$, the diagonal cell $P(Z_1>0.8416, Z_2>0.8416)$ integrates to $\approx\mathbf{8.7\%}$ of all days, not $4\%$ — a **2.2×** overpopulation. If the lift was computed as (cell count)/(1,304/25) rather than (cell count)/(cell population), part of the headline $3\times$ is a cell-size artifact and the true lift could be closer to $3.0/2.2\approx1.4\times$. Reimplementations must normalize by the **actual** cell population. *(From AI)*

- **Sample-size-corrected LMV via extreme-value normalization** — modify **Section 4.3 step 2** so that instead of reporting the raw bin maximum, it reports the maximum divided by its expectation under a null of volume-independent volatility: $\hat{g}^{\,j}_{LM}=g^{\,j}_{LM}\big/ n_j^{1/\hat{\zeta}}$, where $n_j$ is the bin's day count and $\hat{\zeta}$ the estimated tail index. The residual slope of $\hat{g}^{\,j}_{LM}$ against $v$ is then a **clean** test of whether volume genuinely raises the volatility ceiling. An equivalent, simpler variant is to use **equal-count (quantile) volume bins** instead of equal-width bins so that $n_j$ is constant by construction and no correction is needed.
  - **Strong:** *Wherever bin populations are wildly unequal, which is always under Gaussian volume* — the correction removes a bias of up to $67^{1/3}=\mathbf{4.06\times}$ between the central and $|v|=2.9$ bins, which is **larger than the entire LMV range** the paper's own scatter spans (roughly 2.2 to 5.3, i.e. $2.4\times$, in the Section 4.3 worked example). A confound bigger than the signal is worth eliminating before any of this is traded. *(From AI)*
  - **Strong:** *The competing confound is already ruled out* — Figs. 3(c),(d) show the LMV-supplying days spread across 1991–2007 rather than clustered in one volatile window, so temporal clustering is not driving the result and sample size is the main remaining candidate. Correcting it isolates the real effect rather than chasing a second artifact. *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** *Exactly in the thin tail bins the correction is meant to fix* — $\hat{\zeta}$ must be estimated per instrument, and the Hill estimator has relative standard error $1/\sqrt{k}$ on $k$ tail observations. For a thin bin with $k\approx5$, $\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\zeta})\approx3/\sqrt{5}=1.34$; propagating through $\log n^{1/\zeta}=(\ln n)/\zeta$ gives $|\partial/\partial\zeta|\,\Delta\zeta=(\ln 5/9)\times1.34=0.24$ in log terms, i.e. a $\pm\mathbf{27\%}$ uncertainty on the correction factor itself. The fix is noisiest where it is needed most, which argues for the equal-count-bin variant instead. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Regimes where the tail index is unstable* — the correction assumes a single $\zeta$ across the sample, but $\zeta$ is itself known to vary with market conditions; if $\zeta$ drops in a crisis, $n^{1/\zeta}$ rises and the correction over-deflates recent LMVs, which would suppress precisely the warning the estimator exists to give. Formally, $\partial \log(n^{1/\zeta})/\partial\zeta = -(\ln n)/\zeta^2$, so a fall from $\zeta=3$ to $\zeta=2$ inflates the correction on a bin with $n=347$ by $\exp(5.849(1/2-1/3))=\exp(0.975)=\mathbf{2.65\times}$ — a deflation larger than the entire signal. The paper's exclusion of post-2007 data leaves no in-sample evidence on how stable $\zeta$ is across regimes. *(From AI)*

- **Volume-scaled volatility target and VaR from the collapse** — use only **Section 4.2 steps 3–4** (the collapse), skipping the parametric fit entirely: estimate the universal quantile function $q(p)$ of the collapsed variable $x=g/v'$ once from all pooled data, then read *any* conditional volatility quantile off it as $q_v(p)=v'\,q(p)$ with $v'=v+4.5$. Position size or a VaR limit then scales as $1/v'$, updated daily from a single observable and applicable one day ahead using lagged volume.
  - **Strong:** *Across all measured volume levels, with high statistical efficiency* — the Fig. 1(c) collapse means one curve serves all volume bins, so the quantile function is estimated from all 130,410 observations at once rather than from the few hundred days in each bin; this is the same nonparametric trick Yamasaki et al. used successfully for volatility return intervals [25]. *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** *Regimes with large volume swings* — under the collapse the volatility quantile is exactly linear in $v'$, so moving from a quiet $v=-1$ ($v'=3.5$) to a busy $v=+2$ ($v'=6.5$) multiplies every volatility quantile by $6.5/3.5=\mathbf{1.857}$, and a volatility-targeting rule with size $\propto1/\sigma$ must cut exposure by $1-3.5/6.5=\mathbf{46.2\%}$. That is a large, precisely specified, one-observable adjustment — and it is available a day in advance at $\rho_0\approx0.4$ (Fig. 4). *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Any regime, because the scaling amplitude is not identified by the data* — the offset $4.5$ is admitted to be a regularizer chosen "to avoid singularities", not an estimate. But the predicted quantile ratio between $v=-1$ and $v=+2$ is $(2+c)/(c-1)$ in the offset $c$: $c=4.5$ gives $1.857$, $c=6.0$ gives $8/5=1.600$ (**-14%**), and $c=3.0$ gives $5/2=2.500$ (**+35%**). The headline sizing adjustment therefore inherits a $\pm$tens-of-percent ambiguity from an unestimated constant; $c$ must be fitted, not assumed. *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** *Outside $|v|\lesssim2$ and outside the 1990–2007 sample* — the collapse is demonstrated only for the eight bins from $v=-1.75\pm0.25$ to $+1.75\pm0.25$, and the paper excludes post-June-2007 data for volume non-stationarity. Extrapolating a linear-in-$v'$ VaR to $v=\pm3$ or into a crisis is untested, and the linear form has no mechanism preventing it from producing absurd scales far from the fitted range. *(From paper)*

### Notes on internal inconsistencies found in the source PDF

These are recorded because they affect any reimplementation, and because the digest above reports the paper's numbers verbatim rather than silently correcting them.

- **Eq. 6 left-hand side** is printed as $P(g, v\,|\,v)$, which is malformed — $v$ appears both as a joint argument and as the conditioning variable. From context (and from Eq. 5, which it extends) it should read $P(g\,|\,v)$.
- **Fitted parameter typo:** the text reads "we find $\alpha = 0.4$, $\beta = -1.23$, $a - 2.5$, and $b = 3$"; the third entry is plainly $a = -2.5$.
- **Non-normalizability of Eq. 6 at high volume:** $\varsigma(v) = -1.23v + 3$ is negative for $v > 2.44$, inside the paper's own $v\in[-3,3]$ binning range, where $e^{-\varsigma g}$ diverges. No normalization constant is given anywhere, so Eq. 6 is stated only up to proportionality and cannot be integrated to 1 over the full range it is drawn on in Fig. 2.
- **Eq. 6 is inconsistent with the Fig. 1(c) collapse.** Exact scale invariance $P(g|v)=(1/v')f(g/v')$ requires $\xi$ constant and $\varsigma \propto 1/v'$, whereas Eq. 6 makes $\xi$ linear in $v$ ($\alpha=0.4\ne0$) and $\varsigma$ linear with a negative slope. Matching at $v=0$ gives $\kappa=13.5$, after which the two forms differ by $2.077$ versus $0.54$ at $v=+2$ — a factor of 3.8.
- **Binning variable ambiguity in Fig. 3's caption:** the caption says the red triangles "depict the largest values in each bin of $g$ (from -3 to 3 we delineate 30 bins evenly)", but Eq. 7 and the surrounding text define the bins $u_j$ on **volume**, and $g$ is non-negative by Eq. 3 so it cannot span $-3$ to $3$. The bins are on $v$.
- **Apples-to-oranges correlation comparison:** $\rho=0.50$ is computed on **raw daily** observations while $\rho_0=0.93$ is computed on **30 bin maxima**. These have different units of observation and vastly different effective degrees of freedom, and no confidence interval is reported for either. The Section 4.3 worked example shows the same dataset yielding $\rho=0.35$ and $\rho_0=0.925$, so the gap is partly an artifact of the change of statistic.
- **Order-statistic sample-size confound in LMV, unaddressed.** Bin populations vary by $\approx67\times$ between $v=0$ and $|v|=2.9$ under the Gaussian volume pdf the paper itself fits, and $E[\max$ of $n]\propto n^{1/\zeta}$, giving a $\approx4\times$ spurious spread with $\zeta\approx3$.
- **"25 equal-sized sets" contradicts $\rho(v,g)=0.5$.** With volume and volatility correlated at 0.5, the joint quintile cells cannot be equally populated; the diagonal $(v_5,g_5)$ cell holds $\approx8.7\%$ of days under a Gaussian copula, not $4\%$.
- **Self-referential figure cross-reference:** Fig. 4's caption states "the mean correlation coefficients for time-lag = 0 days, 1 day are very similar to those found in Fig. 4, which depicts results for only The Boeing Company (BA)" — it should read Fig. 3.
- **Garbled Fig. 5 caption:** "The radius of the circle is proportional to $P(g(t+1)\in g_0|v_n(t),g_n(t)) = 1\%$" conflates the plotted quantity with its unconditioned normalizing value; the text makes clear the radius is proportional to the conditional probability **in units of** the unconditioned $1\%$.
- **Conclusion misquotes its own correlation:** the conclusion refers to "the weak correlation coefficient ($\approx 0.2$)" for the same-day volume–volatility relation, but Fig. 3(a) reports $\rho=0.50$ same-day and $\rho=0.20$ at a one-day lag.
- **Reversed causal direction in the conclusion:** "Because we observe that high volatility strongly affects trading volume, we posit that volume can be used to predict future volatility" — the stated causal arrow runs opposite to the predictive claim being made.
- **The $+6\%$ $R^2$ claim is unspecified:** it is not stated whether the increase is absolute or relative, no baseline $R^2$, regression specification, coefficients, standard errors, or out-of-sample test is given, and with $n=130{,}410$ any added regressor raises in-sample $R^2$ nearly automatically.
- **Typographical:** "fairly week" for "fairly weak" in the conclusion; "Republic" and several other words are broken by the PDF's hyphenation/extraction ("Repub lic", "volati lity"), which is an extraction artifact rather than a paper error.
