## 2. Summary

1. **Motivation — the Epps effect, and the paper's refusal to call it a bias.** Covariance is a core input to portfolio optimisation, risk management and intraday state detection, but high-frequency data does not deliver it cleanly. Two obstacles are named. First, price *discovery* differs by time scale: a daily closing price comes from a closing auction that approximates a supply-demand equilibrium, whereas intraday transaction prices come from a continuous double auction driven by order flow, with no equilibrium interpretation. Second, tick data is **asynchronous** — assets trade at their own random times — so any covariance estimate requires an implicit choice of synchronisation, interpolation or averaging, each of which injects bias. The **Epps effect** (Epps 1979) is the empirical decay of measured correlation as the sampling interval shrinks. The literature attributes it variously to small sampling intervals, lead-lag, asynchrony, or "market micro-structure noise" (bid-ask bounce producing negative short-horizon autocorrelation). The authors deliberately **avoid the micro-structure-noise framework** and adopt an event-driven, exploratory-data-analysis stance (explicitly in the spirit of Tukey 1977): trading events are fundamentally discrete, order flow is the top-down driver, and mapping event time into a single continuous calendar clock smears events, creating leads, lags and diluted correlation. Their thesis is that the Epps effect is a **real property of averaging over order flow**, not an artefact to be corrected away.

2. **Method part 1 — the Malliavin-Mancino (MM) Fourier estimator.** MM works in the frequency domain: it expresses the Fourier coefficients of the volatility process in terms of the Fourier coefficients of the log-price process. After rescaling trading times from $[0,T]$ to $[0,2\pi]$, Fourier convolution gives, for all $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $N$ samples,
   $$\mathcal{F}(\Sigma_{ij})(k) = \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2\pi}{2N+1}\sum_{|s|\le N}\mathcal{F}(dp_i)(s)\,\mathcal{F}(dp_j)(k-s). \tag{2.1}$$
   Here $\mathcal{F}(*)(\star)$ is the $\star$-th Fourier coefficient of process $*$, $dp_i$ is the log-price increment process of asset $i$, $s$ and $k$ are integer frequencies, and $N$ is the cutoff frequency. Using previous-tick interpolation (to avoid the downward bias documented by Barucci and Renò 2002) and a simple function approximation for the coefficients, the **integrated covariance** becomes
   $$\int_0^{2\pi}\Sigma_{ij}(t)\,dt = \frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{|s|\le N}\ \sum_{\substack{j=1,\,i=1}}^{n-1,\,n-1} e^{\mathrm{i}s(t_i-t_j)}\,\delta_1(I_i)\,\delta_2(I_j), \tag{2.2}$$
   where the trade intervals are $I_i:=[t_i,t_{i+1})$ and $I_j:=[t_j,t_{j+1})$, and the price fluctuations are $\delta_1(I_i):=p_1(t_{i+1})-p_1(t_i)$ and $\delta_2(I_j):=p_2(t_{j+1})-p_2(t_j)$ for the $i$-th and $j$-th asset respectively. Setting $k=0$ in (2.1) and using $\mathcal{F}(dp)(s)\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_j e^{-\mathrm{i}st_j}\delta(I_j)$ reproduces (2.2) exactly, since the two factors of $2\pi$ cancel the two factors of $\frac{1}{2\pi}$ — the equations are internally consistent. Note that the printed (2.2) uses $\mathrm{i}$ both as an asset/trade index and as the imaginary unit; this digest writes $\mathrm{i}$ upright for the imaginary unit.
   *Worked example on dataset D.* Rescale time with $\tau=2\pi(t-t_{\min})/(t_{\max}-t_{\min}) = \pi t/3$, giving asset 1 times $\tau=0,\frac{2\pi}{3},\frac{4\pi}{3},2\pi$ and asset 2 times $\tau=0,\pi,2\pi$. The sparser asset has $n=3$ trades, so the average-based Nyquist cutoff is $N=\lfloor 3/2\rfloor = 1$ and $K=\{-1,0,1\}$ with $|K|=2N+1=3$. Fourier coefficients (each return is attached to the *left* endpoint of its interval): $c^+_1(1)=0.00995033\,e^{\mathrm{i}0}-0.00496278\,e^{\mathrm{i}2\pi/3}+0.01481506\,e^{\mathrm{i}4\pi/3} = 0.00502419-0.01712818\mathrm{i}$; $c^+_0(1)=0.01980261$; $c^+_1(2)=0.00399202-0.01581073=-0.01181871$; $c^+_0(2)=0.01980275$. Then $\Sigma_{11}=\frac{1}{3}\left[2(0.00502419^2+0.01712818^2)+0.01980261^2\right]=\frac{1}{3}(1.0293774\times10^{-3})=\mathbf{3.43126\times10^{-4}}$; $\Sigma_{22}=\frac{1}{3}\left[2(0.01181871)^2+0.01980275^2\right]=\frac{1}{3}(6.715127\times10^{-4})=\mathbf{2.23838\times10^{-4}}$; $\Sigma_{12}=\frac{1}{3}\left[2(0.00502419)(-0.01181871)+0.01980261\times0.01980275\right]=\frac{1}{3}(2.733832\times10^{-4})=\mathbf{9.11277\times10^{-5}}$. Hence $R_{12}^{\text{MM}} = \frac{9.11277\times10^{-5}}{\sqrt{3.43126\times10^{-4}}\sqrt{2.23838\times10^{-4}}} = \frac{9.11277\times10^{-5}}{0.01852365\times0.01496121}=\mathbf{0.3288}$.

3. **Method part 2 — the Hayashi-Yoshida (HY) overlap estimator.** HY needs no prior synchronisation. It is the cumulative cross-product of all return pairs whose intervals overlap:
   $$U_n := \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta P_1(J_i)\,\Delta P_2(J_j)\,\mathbf{1}_{\{J_i\cap J_j\neq\emptyset\}}. \tag{2.3}$$
   Using the Kanatani weight matrix and a simple integral approximation, the integrated covariance is
   $$\int_0^{T}\Sigma_{ij}(t)\,dt = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} w_{ij}\,\delta_1(J_i)\,\delta_2(J_j), \tag{2.4}$$
   with left-open intervals $J_i:=(t_{i-1},t_i]$, $J_j:=(t_{j-1},t_j]$, price fluctuations $\delta_1(J_i):=p_1(t_i)-p_1(t_{i-1})$, $\delta_2(J_j):=p_2(t_j)-p_2(t_{j-1})$, and Kanatani weights
   $$w_{ij}=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if } J_i\cap J_j\neq\emptyset\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases} \tag{2.5}$$
   *Worked example on dataset D.* Asset 1 intervals are $J_1=(0,2]$, $J_2=(2,4]$, $J_3=(4,6]$; asset 2 intervals are $J'_1=(0,3]$, $J'_2=(3,6]$. The overlap pattern gives $w=1$ for $(J_1,J'_1)$, $(J_2,J'_1)$, $(J_2,J'_2)$, $(J_3,J'_2)$ and $w=0$ for $(J_1,J'_2)$, $(J_3,J'_1)$ — four of six pairs contribute, and asset 1's middle return $\delta^1_2$ contributes **twice** (the "multiple contributions" the paper criticises). Then $\Sigma_{12}^{\text{HY}} = (0.00995033)(0.00399202) + (-0.00496278)(0.00399202) + (-0.00496278)(0.01581073) + (0.01481506)(0.01581073) = 3.97220\times10^{-5} - 1.98096\times10^{-5} - 7.84666\times10^{-5} + 2.342782\times10^{-4} = \mathbf{1.75724\times10^{-4}}$. The diagonals are plain realised variances: $\Sigma_{11}^{\text{HY}} = 0.00995033^2+0.00496278^2+0.01481506^2 = \mathbf{3.43124\times10^{-4}}$ and $\Sigma_{22}^{\text{HY}} = 0.00399202^2+0.01581073^2=\mathbf{2.65915\times10^{-4}}$. Hence $R_{12}^{\text{HY}} = \frac{1.75724\times10^{-4}}{0.01852361\times0.01630998}=\mathbf{0.5818}$. Side by side on identical data, **MM = 0.329, HY = 0.582** — HY reports 1.77 times the MM correlation, which is precisely the paper's central empirical claim in miniature. Note also that MM's $\Sigma_{11}=3.43126\times10^{-4}$ coincides with the realised variance $3.43124\times10^{-4}$ to rounding: with $N=1$ and equally spaced $\tau$ the normalised Dirichlet kernel $\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{|k|\le N}e^{\mathrm{i}kx}=\frac{1+2\cos x}{3}$ vanishes at $x=\frac{2\pi}{3}$ and $x=\frac{4\pi}{3}$, killing every off-diagonal term — a checkable identity readers can use to validate their own MM code.

4. **Monte Carlo experiment 1 — asynchrony as missing data, benchmark GBM.** The bivariate geometric Brownian motion satisfies
   $$\frac{dS_i(t)}{S_i(t)} = \mu_i\,dt + \sigma_i\,dW_i(t),\qquad i=1,2, \tag{3.1}$$
   with $\mu_i$ the drift, $\sigma_i$ the volatility, $W_i$ correlated Brownian motions with $\mathrm{Corr}(dW_1,dW_2)=\rho_{12}$. Parameters (daily): $\mu_1=\mu_2=0.01$, $\sigma_1^2=0.1$, $\sigma_2^2=0.2$, $\rho_{12}$ swept over $(-1,1)$, start price R100, **10,000 realisations** each notionally one second apart. Asynchrony is manufactured by randomly deleting a percentage of observations from each path. Result: at **0% missing** both estimators recover the induced correlation exactly; at **10%, 20% and 40% missing** MM develops a progressively stronger downward bias toward zero while HY continues to recover the induced correlation at every level. The authors read this as a fork in the road rather than a verdict: *if* prices are discrete samples of a latent continuous Itô process and asynchrony is a missing-data problem, HY is right and MM is downward biased; *if* the world is genuinely discrete, disconnected and asynchronous — with events as the fundamental measurables — then MM is right (it performs lossless interpolation between events) and HY is **upward** biased through its multiple contributions. They side with the second reading because TAQ data is truly discontinuous and event-driven. Two further HY problems are imported from the literature: under high asynchrony HY effectively **deletes observations** through its overlap rule, and its core assumption — that correlation does not extend beyond overlapping return intervals — fails in practice, biasing it under lead-lag.
   *Worked example of the simulator (Algorithm 5) with these parameters.* $\sigma_1=\sqrt{0.1}=0.316228$, $\sigma_2=\sqrt{0.2}=0.447214$, and with $\rho_{12}=0.35$ the covariance is $0.35\times0.316228\times0.447214=0.0494975$, so $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.1 & 0.0494975\\ 0.0494975 & 0.2\end{pmatrix}$ with Cholesky factor $A_{11}=0.316228$, $A_{21}=0.0494975/0.316228=0.156525$, $A_{22}=\sqrt{0.2-0.156525^2}=\sqrt{0.1755}=0.418927$. One second is $\Delta t=1/86400$ day, $\sqrt{\Delta t}=3.40262\times10^{-3}$. Draw $Z=(0.5,-1.2)$. Asset 1 exponent $=(0.01-0.05)(1.157407\times10^{-5}) + 3.40262\times10^{-3}\times(0.316228\times0.5) = -4.62963\times10^{-7}+5.38007\times10^{-4}=5.37544\times10^{-4}$, so $S_1=100\,e^{5.37544\times10^{-4}}=\mathbf{100.0538}$. Asset 2 exponent $=(0.01-0.1)(1.157407\times10^{-5}) + 3.40262\times10^{-3}\times(0.156525\times0.5+0.418927\times(-1.2)) = -1.041667\times10^{-6}-1.444437\times10^{-3}=-1.445479\times10^{-3}$, so $S_2=100\,e^{-1.445479\times10^{-3}}=\mathbf{99.8556}$.

5. **Monte Carlo experiment 2 — does the driving process matter? (Merton, Variance Gamma, GARCH, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck).** Four alternative bivariate processes are simulated to test whether jumps, pure-jump behaviour, stochastic volatility or mean reversion — rather than asynchrony — can separate MM from HY. The **Merton jump-diffusion** is
   $$\frac{dS_i(t)}{S_i(t^-)} = \mu_i\,dt + \sigma_i\,dW_i(t) + dJ_i(t),\qquad i=1,2, \tag{3.2}$$
   $$J_i(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{N(t)}\left(Y_j-1\right), \tag{3.3}$$
   where $N(t)$ is a Poisson process of intensity $\lambda_i$, the jump multipliers are $Y_j\sim \mathrm{LN}(a,b)$ i.i.d. and independent of $N(t)$, and $t^-$ marks the càdlàg left limit. Parameters: $\mu_i=0.01$, $\sigma_1^2=0.1$, $\sigma_2^2=0.2$, $a_1=a_2=0$, $b_1=b_2=100$, $\lambda$ varied over $0,\,0.2,\,0.5$, 10,000 seconds, start R100. The **Variance Gamma** process is
   $$S_i(t) = U(t)-D(t),\qquad i=1,2, \tag{3.4}$$
   $$U(t_{i+1})-U(t_i)\sim \mathrm{Gamma}\big(\alpha(t_{i+1}-t_i),\beta\big),\qquad D(t_{i+1})-D(t_i)\sim \mathrm{Gamma}\big(\alpha(t_{i+1}-t_i),\beta\big), \tag{3.5}$$
   with $U,D$ independent gamma processes sharing shape rate $\alpha$ and scale $\beta$, which permits the equivalent subordinated representation $W(G(t))$ with $W$ a standard Brownian motion and $G$ a gamma time change; $\beta_1=\beta_2=1$. The **bivariate GARCH(1,1)** diffusion limit is
   $$dp_i(t) = \sigma_i(t)\,dW_i(t),\qquad i=1,2, \tag{3.6}$$
   $$d\sigma_1^2(t) = \theta_1\left[w_1-\sigma_1^2\right]dt + \sqrt{2\lambda_1\theta_1\sigma_1^2(t)}\;dW_3(t),\qquad d\sigma_2^2(t) = \theta_2\left[w_2-\sigma_2^2\right]dt + \sqrt{2\lambda_2\theta_2\sigma_2^2(t)}\;dW_4(t), \tag{3.7}$$
   where $\theta_i$ is the mean-reversion rate of variance, $w_i$ the long-run variance and $\lambda_i$ the volatility-of-volatility parameter; the authors use $\theta_1=0.035$, $\theta_2=0.054$, $w_1=0.636$, $w_2=0.476$, $\lambda_1=0.296$, $\lambda_2=0.48$ from Andersen and Bollerslev (1998), and note explicitly that Renò (2001) wrote a *different* SDE while borrowing the same parameters — both specifications are therefore simulated and compared. The **Ornstein-Uhlenbeck** process is
   $$dp_i(t) = \theta_i\left(\mu_i - p_i(t)\right)dt + \sigma_i\,dW_i(t),\qquad i=1,2, \tag{3.8}$$
   with $\theta_1=0.035$, $\theta_2=0.054$, $\mu_1=\mu_2=100$, $\sigma_1^2=0.1$, $\sigma_2^2=0.2$. Findings: with 20% down-sampling, **synchronous MM and synchronous HY agree exactly for every process**, asynchronous HY recovers the synchronous value, and asynchronous MM sits below it. As $\lambda$ rises in the Merton model both *synchronous* estimates fall toward zero together — that fall is not estimator bias but the genuine dilution of correlation by a jump component independent of the diffusion (and when trades are synchronous HY collapses to Realised Volatility, which is consistent under jumps). Conclusion: **neither jumps, nor pure-jump dynamics, nor volatility clustering, nor mean reversion separates the two estimators — only asynchrony does.**
   *Worked examples.* (a) *Merton (Algorithm 6)*, $\lambda=0.2$/day, $\Delta t=1/86400$ day so $\lambda\Delta t=2.3148\times10^{-6}$; suppose the rare event happens and $N_1=1$, with $Z_2=0.01$: the jump term is $M=a_1N_1+b_1\sqrt{N_1}Z_2 = 0 + 100\times1\times0.01 = \mathbf{1.0}$ in log space, i.e. the price is multiplied by $e^{1.0}=2.718$ in a single second. (b) *Variance Gamma (Algorithm 8)* with $\beta=1$, $\Delta t=1/86400$: $Y\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(1.157407\times10^{-5},1)$ with $\mathbb{E}[Y]=1.157407\times10^{-5}$; drawing $Y_1=1.0\times10^{-5}$ and $Z=(0.5,-1.2)$ gives $X_1(t_1)=100+0.01\times10^{-5}+\sqrt{10^{-5}}\times(0.316228\times0.5)=100+1\times10^{-7}+5.00119\times10^{-4}=\mathbf{100.0005002}$. (c) *GARCH (Algorithm 7)* starting at the long-run level $\sigma_1^2(t_0)=w_1=0.636$ (so $\sigma_1=0.797496$), the drift term is exactly zero and with $Z=1.5$ the diffusion term is $\sqrt{2\times0.296\times0.035\times1.157407\times10^{-5}}\times0.797496\times1.5=4.89425\times10^{-4}\times0.797496\times1.5=5.85473\times10^{-4}$, so $\sigma_1^2(t_1)=\mathbf{0.636585}$. (d) *OU (Algorithm 9)* starting at $X_1(t_0)=\ln 100=4.605170$ with $\ln\mu_1=\ln 100$, the drift is zero and $X_1(t_1)=4.605170+3.40262\times10^{-3}\times0.316228\times0.5=4.605708$, so $S_1=e^{4.605708}=\mathbf{100.0538}$.

6. **Monte Carlo experiment 3 — arrival-time asynchrony, the Fourier cutoff $N$, and the analytic Epps curve.** Deleting observations does not reproduce the randomness of real event arrivals, so the second family of experiments samples the simulated paths at **exponential inter-arrival times**. Replicating Renò (2001): 86,400 one-second realisations of bivariate GARCH(1,1), asset 1 sampled with mean inter-arrival **15 s** and asset 2 with mean **45 s**; the synchronous control forces asset 1 onto asset 2's timestamps (both at 45 s). Sweeping $N$ from **10 to 160** and averaging over **10,000 replications**, the asynchronous correlation falls with increasing $N$ (increasing sampling frequency) while the synchronous correlation stays flat — because every SDE used here has a *dimensionless* correlation independent of sampling interval. The extended experiment (10,000 seconds, means 30 s and 45 s, induced $\rho=0.35$, **1,000 replications**) adds the Nyquist frequency as a reference line and shows error bars (standard deviation across replications) shrinking as $N$ grows, i.e. MM becomes more precise with more coefficients. Two important methodological choices are stated: the Nyquist line in these figures is computed from the *average* sampling frequency, whereas **every other MM estimate in the paper uses the "true" cutoff derived from the highest sampling frequency present in the data**, because choosing a deliberately low $N$ smooths the data by aliasing away high frequencies — useful if you believe in micro-structure noise, but destructive if the object of study is the co-movement of events. With the true cutoff, all asynchronous MM correlations decay to zero. HY is not a function of $N$ and serves as a flat baseline; it recovers the synchronous value for GARCH, GBM, Merton and VG, but **not for Ornstein-Uhlenbeck**, where HY itself displays an Epps effect — mean reversion combined with non-uniform exponential sampling generates spurious lead-lag relations that HY's overlap rule cannot handle. The paper then quotes the closed form derived by Mastromatteo, Marsili and Zoi (2011) for an Epps effect arising from asynchrony as a function of $\Delta t$:
   $$\tilde\rho^{12}_{\Delta t} = c\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta t}\left(e^{-\lambda\Delta t}-1\right)\right), \tag{3.9}$$
   where $c$ is the true (scale-free) correlation, $\lambda$ is the trade-arrival intensity and $\Delta t$ the sampling interval. Its structural limitation is the paper's launching point for the empirical sections: **(3.9) is monotone and can only decrease the correlation — it can never change its sign**, yet real data does change sign across scales.
   *Worked example of (3.9).* Take $c=0.35$ and $\lambda=1/30$ s$^{-1}$ (mean inter-arrival 30 s). Write $x=\lambda\Delta t$. At $\Delta t=10$ s, $x=1/3$, $e^{-x}=0.716531$, so $\tilde\rho=0.35\left(1+\frac{0.716531-1}{1/3}\right)=0.35(1-0.850407)=\mathbf{0.0524}$. At $\Delta t=60$ s, $x=2$, $e^{-2}=0.135335$, $\tilde\rho=0.35(1-0.432332)=\mathbf{0.1987}$. At $\Delta t=600$ s, $x=20$, $\tilde\rho=0.35(1-0.05)=\mathbf{0.3325}$. At $\Delta t=3600$ s, $x=120$, $\tilde\rho=0.35(1-1/120)=\mathbf{0.3471}$. The limits are checkable: as $x\to0$, $1+\frac{e^{-x}-1}{x}\to \frac{x}{2}\to 0$; as $x\to\infty$ it tends to 1, so $\tilde\rho$ rises monotonically from $0$ to $c$ and never crosses zero.

7. **Data and a first diagnostic that the market is not Brownian.** The empirical data is **Bloomberg TAQ for 10 JSE equities over the week 31/05/2019 to 07/06/2019**: FirstRand (FSR), Shoprite (SHP), Absa (ABG), Nedbank (NED), Standard Bank (SBK), Sasol (SOL), Mondi (MNP), Anglo American (AGL), Naspers (NPN) and British American Tobacco (BTI). Only **Automated Trades** forming the continuous trading process are kept; overnight data, auction data and overnight returns across days are removed. Because Bloomberg stamps only to the second, trades sharing a timestamp are collapsed with a volume-weighted average (Algorithm 10). Table 1 reports ADV and daily-rescaled volatilities: **MNP is the least liquid (ADV 340,381.4)** and **FSR the most liquid (ADV 7,698,648.0)** — a 22.6-fold spread that drives every later synchronisation result. The daily volatility columns are produced by rescaling the MM volatility estimated from 1-hour, 10-minute and 1-minute bars by $\sqrt{24}$, $\sqrt{6\times24}$ and $\sqrt{60\times24}$ respectively, and the authors' point is that **the three rescaled numbers do not line up**, which they read as direct evidence that continuous-double-auction price discovery is not well represented by Brownian motion.
   *Worked example of the rescaling, for BTI.* Reported daily-equivalent volatilities are $\hat\sigma_{D,1\text{HR}}=0.0075$, $\hat\sigma_{D,10\text{MIN}}=0.0135$, $\hat\sigma_{D,1\text{MIN}}=0.0501$. Inverting the stated scalings gives the per-bar volatilities $0.0075/\sqrt{24}=0.0015309$ per hour, $0.0135/12=0.001125$ per 10 minutes and $0.0501/\sqrt{1440}=0.0013202$ per minute. Put all three on a per-minute footing under Brownian scaling ($\sigma_\Delta\propto\sqrt{\Delta}$): $0.0015309/\sqrt{60}=1.9766\times10^{-4}$, $0.001125/\sqrt{10}=3.5576\times10^{-4}$, and $1.3202\times10^{-3}$. Per-minute *variance* therefore inflates from $3.907\times10^{-8}$ to $1.266\times10^{-7}$ to $1.743\times10^{-6}$ — a factor of **44.6** between the 1-hour and 1-minute estimates, whereas Brownian motion predicts a factor of exactly 1. A caveat readers should carry forward: the columns are labelled $\hat\sigma^2_{D,\cdot}$ (variances) but are produced by multiplying by $\sqrt{\cdot}$ (a volatility scaling), and the factors 24, 144, 1440 imply a **24-hour** day while Section 5.2 uses 8, 48 and 480 volume buckets per day for the same bar lengths, implying an **8-hour** day. The internal comparison across columns is unaffected, but the absolute level is inflated by $\sqrt{3}\approx1.73$.

8. **Empirical result 1 — calendar time averaging: the Epps effect at every frequency, plus sign flips.** Sampling intervals are stepped down from 1 hour to 10 minutes to 1 minute to raw asynchronous TAQ, under two aggregation rules: **closing bars** (decimate to the last trade in each bar and shift it to the bar end) and **VWAP bars** (volume-average all trades in the bar and shift to the bar end). Average absolute correlation $|\rho_{ij}|$ across the 10x10 matrix, plus or minus one sample standard deviation, tells the story. Closing bars: MM gives **0.2829 ± 0.2101** (1 h), **0.2334 ± 0.2110** (10 min), **0.1384 ± 0.0954** (1 min), **0.0355 ± 0.0166** (TAQ); HY gives **0.2912 ± 0.2235**, **0.2347 ± 0.2069**, **0.1466 ± 0.1033**, **0.1051 ± 0.0635**. VWAP bars: MM gives **0.3172 ± 0.2192**, **0.2560 ± 0.2221**, **0.1269 ± 0.1071**, **0.0355 ± 0.0166**; HY gives **0.3201 ± 0.2365**, **0.2441 ± 0.2270**, **0.1450 ± 0.1185**, **0.1051 ± 0.0635**. Four readings follow. (i) The Epps effect is present for **both** estimators and **both** aggregations, at frequencies far higher than Epps (1979) could examine. (ii) At the highest available frequency MM collapses toward zero (0.0355) as the Epps effect predicts, but HY barely decays past its 1-minute value (0.1051 versus 0.1466) — the authors read this as HY **manufacturing correlation**, and as direct empirical refutation of the claim that HY is immune to the Epps effect. (iii) Structural change, not just decay: Table 2 gives the BTI-NPN pair as $\rho^{\text{MM}} = -0.0265,\;0.1070,\;0.0514,\;0.0321$ and $\rho^{\text{HY}} = -0.1450,\;0.1445,\;0.0701,\;0.0409$ across 1 hour, 10 minutes, 1 minute and TAQ — the 10-minute-to-TAQ leg is textbook Epps decay, but the **1-hour-to-10-minute leg flips sign from negative to positive and grows in magnitude**, which no monotone asynchrony model such as (3.9) can produce. (iv) MM and HY should be identical on truly synchronous bar data, and they nearly are, but not exactly — the residual gap is a **liquidity effect**: illiquid names do not trade often enough inside every bar, so the "synchronous" bars are not really synchronous. Separately, closing and VWAP aggregation are conceptually very different operations yet produce very similar correlation structures (VWAP simply accentuates what closing prices already show), which the authors argue makes sense if order flow preserves an averaged top-down correlation structure rather than correlation being encoded event by event from the bottom up. The banking-sector block (SBK, NED, ABG, FSR and neighbours) is visibly the most correlated region of every heat map, matching prior domain expectations.

9. **Empirical result 2 — intrinsic (stock-specific) volume time.** Following Derman (2002), each stock is given its own volume clock. Defining a stock's trading frequency $v_j$ as the number of intrinsic time ticks per calendar second, the two clocks are linked by
   $$d\tau_j = v_j\,dt, \tag{5.1}$$
   where $\tau_j$ is intrinsic time for asset $j$ and $t$ is calendar time. Derman's key results are that each stock's intrinsic time scale is **constant through time** and that the intrinsic-time correlation $\pi_{ij}$ equals the calendar-time correlation $\rho_{ij}$ — which, if true, would let one build synchronous price paths in intrinsic time and read off calendar-time correlations. The authors reinterpret $v_j$ as trades per unit of the *sampling interval considered* rather than per second, and implement the clock as **8, 48 and 480 volume buckets per day**, which are on average the equivalents of 1-hour, 10-minute and 1-minute calendar bars. Results: MM gives $|\rho_{ij}|=$ **0.2209 ± 0.1583** (8 buckets), **0.0879 ± 0.0633** (48), **0.0230 ± 0.0217** (480); HY gives **0.2660 ± 0.1896**, **0.0914 ± 0.0676**, **0.0338 ± 0.0363**. Three conclusions. The **Epps effect survives the change of clock entirely** — it is not a calendar-time artefact. The correlation *structures* change with the bucket count, so correlations are **not dimensionless** as Derman's framework requires, and the whole correlation structure in intrinsic time differs markedly from the calendar-time structure, so correlations are **not preserved across clocks** as Derman proved they should be. Two explicit criticisms of the framework are recorded: constancy of the intrinsic time scale is a strong assumption (real trading frequency varies with time of day, news, announcements and order-flow shocks), and (5.1) imposes a linear, continuity-preserving map from calendar into intrinsic time, so it does not truly deliver event time in which a unit intrinsic interval is a stochastic calendar interval terminated by a stopping rule. A practical consequence is also noted: because bucket sizes come from *average* daily volume, a day with above-average volume yields more buckets and a below-average day fewer, which de-synchronises key events and injects leads and lags. Finally, because this aggregation produces near-synchronous data, the two estimators behave similarly — so it cannot settle which estimator is better, which motivates the paper's own scheme.
   *Worked example of (5.1) and the bucket sizing.* For MNP with $\mathrm{ADV}=340{,}381.4$ shares and $N=48$ buckets per day, $v_{\text{MNP}} = 340{,}381.4/48 = \mathbf{7{,}091.3}$ shares per bucket; for FSR, $7{,}698{,}648/48 = \mathbf{160{,}388.5}$ shares per bucket — a ratio of **22.6**, so the same nominal "10-minute equivalent" clock counts 22.6 times more shares per tick for FSR than for MNP. Reading (5.1) forward over an 8-hour (28,800 s) session, MNP's average trade flow is $340{,}381.4/28{,}800 = 11.82$ shares per second, so one MNP bucket takes $7{,}091.3/11.82 = \mathbf{600}$ s of calendar time on an average day — exactly the intended 10 minutes, confirming the construction. On a day with 20% above-average volume the same bucket fills in $600/1.2 = \mathbf{500}$ s, producing $28{,}800/500 = 57.6$ rather than 48 buckets, which is the de-synchronisation mechanism the authors describe.

10. **Empirical result 3 — the paper's own synchronised volume clock, and the concluding position.** The proposed scheme keeps a **common volume-bucket size across assets** but lets each asset's bucket close at its own calendar moment: the most liquid asset (FSR) and then the least liquid (MNP) are each used in turn as the baseline "clock", every asset accumulates traded shares in calendar time until its bucket reaches the shared threshold, and at that instant an aggregated price is emitted with its own calendar timestamp. Prices are thus synchronised **in volume time** but not in calendar time, which deliberately preserves the relative asynchrony of events while retaining the partial recovery of Gaussian fluctuations that volume clocks provide. The 1-hour equivalent had to be dropped when synchronising to FSR for liquidity reasons. Results ($|\rho_{ij}|$): MM gives **0.0034 ± 0.0050** (48-bucket, least liquid baseline), **0.0103 ± 0.0084** (480, least liquid), **0.0073 ± 0.0196** (48, most liquid), **0.0046 ± 0.0068** (480, most liquid); HY gives **0.2416 ± 0.1994**, **0.1284 ± 0.0929**, **0.4265 ± 0.2773**, **0.2996 ± 0.2343**. This is the sharpest separation in the paper: under maximally asynchronous sampling MM sits essentially at zero (and, with the Nyquist cutoff in force, shows no clear Epps gradient when synchronised to the least liquid ticker), whereas HY not only retains an Epps gradient but reaches **0.4265** for the 48-bucket most-liquid case — higher than *any* calendar-time or intrinsic-time figure in the paper, which the authors take as direct evidence that HY's multiple contributions compensate for missing observations and thereby manufacture correlation, while MM treats missing observations as genuinely missing. **Concluding remarks and stated limitations.** The authors are explicit that they do **not** conclusively establish which estimator is better; they argue for MM on data-informed grounds (lossless interpolation, correct capture of an Epps-like effect) while noting HY remains preferable if one believes prices are samples of a latent continuous process. HY's stated defects are deleting observations and lead-lag bias, and it is unclear what its multiple contributions are really doing. The two estimators differ **only** on tick-by-tick asynchronous data. Across all four visualisation experiments — closing-price decimation, VWAP averaging, intrinsic time, synchronised volume time — the loss of correlation as the averaging scale shrinks looks like a **fundamental property**, plausibly caused by mapping an averaged observable from many event time scales into one calendar clock, which shifts and smears events and mixes scales. A definitive answer would require a framework tying together discrete asynchronous data generation, the continuous-time processes underlying the estimators' asymptotics, a satisfactory TAQ synchronisation method, and clocks that map back into calendar time sensibly. Their proposed next step is to simulate order flow directly with multivariate Hawkes point processes over multiple order types and correlated assets, estimate correlations from the resulting mid-prices, and — for transaction prices — add a simulated order book and matching engine to produce genuine price discovery.

## 3. Key Importance Topics

### The Epps effect is a property of the market, not a bug in the estimator

Hayashi and Yoshida (2005) framed the Epps effect as an estimator bias to which their own estimator is immune, and the paper's first missing-data experiment appears to confirm it: HY recovers the induced correlation at 10%, 20% and 40% decimation while MM drifts to zero. The paper then dismantles that reading from three directions. Closed-form expressions for the Epps effect already exist in the literature (Mastromatteo et al. 2011; Tóth and Kertész 2009), which means the effect is derivable from the data-generating mechanism rather than from any estimator's algebra. HY *does* exhibit an Epps effect once asynchrony arrives through exponential inter-arrival sampling of a mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. And on real JSE TAQ data HY's average absolute correlation only falls from **0.1466** (1 minute) to **0.1051** (raw TAQ), not to zero — decay is present, just incomplete.

**Why it matters:** if the Epps effect is **real**, the right response is to choose a decision time scale and estimate correlation on it; if it were a bias, the right response would be to "correct" it and pretend a single scale-free correlation exists. Those two research programmes lead to opposite portfolio and risk models, and the paper's evidence points firmly at the first.

### The Fourier cutoff $N$ is the sampling-frequency dial, and picking it low silently smooths the data

For the MM estimator the number of Fourier coefficients $N$ in (2.2) *is* the sampling frequency: raising $N$ resolves higher frequencies, and Fourier analysis supplies the exact correspondence rather than requiring an ad hoc bar length. The paper distinguishes two cutoffs. The Nyquist frequency computed from the **average** sampling frequency is what the replication figures display, but it is not the cutoff that avoids aliasing. The **true** cutoff comes from the highest sampling frequency actually present in the data, and this is what every other MM estimate in the paper uses. Choosing a lower $N$ aliases high frequencies and smooths the estimate, which the micro-structure-noise literature treats as a feature (it recovers a "true" signal beneath the noise) and which this paper treats as a defect (it destroys exactly the event co-movement under study). With the true cutoff, all asynchronous MM correlations decay to zero.

**Why it matters:** $N$ is a **normative choice disguised as a technical parameter**. Two researchers running identical MM code on identical data can report a healthy correlation or a zero correlation depending only on their cutoff rule, so the rule must be published alongside the estimate. It also gives practitioners a free multi-scale correlation curve from one estimator, with no rebarring.

### Volume clocks: intrinsic (per-asset) versus synchronised (shared bucket size)

The paper contrasts two ways to replace the calendar clock with a volume clock. Derman's **intrinsic time** gives every asset its own bucket size $v_j=\mathrm{ADV}_j/N$, which makes the sampled paths near-synchronous in calendar time and therefore makes MM and HY behave alike — the resulting figures cannot discriminate between estimators. The authors' **synchronised volume time** inverts the design: one shared bucket size for all assets (set from either the most or the least liquid name), with each asset's bar closing whenever it happens to fill. Volume time is synchronised; calendar time is not. This deliberately preserves relative asynchrony and produces the paper's largest estimator disagreement, MM near **0.0034-0.0103** against HY at **0.1284-0.4265**.

**Why it matters:** the choice of clock is not cosmetic — it decides **what question the estimator can answer**. A clock that accidentally synchronises the data hides the very phenomenon under study, so an experiment designed to compare estimators must be built on a clock that keeps asynchrony alive. This is a transferable experimental-design lesson well beyond the Epps effect.

### HY's multiple contributions manufacture correlation; MM's lossless interpolation does not

Because the Kanatani weight (2.5) fires for every overlapping interval pair, a single return of a slowly trading asset can be multiplied against many returns of a fast trading asset. In the digest's dataset D, asset 1's middle return enters the covariance twice, which is exactly why HY reports **0.582** against MM's **0.329** on identical data. On real data this shows as HY's failure to decay to zero on raw TAQ (0.1051) and as the extreme 0.4265 in the synchronised-volume-time experiment — higher than any calendar-time value in the paper. The mirror-image criticism, imported from Aït-Sahalia, Fan and Xiu (2010), is that under high asynchrony the overlap rule effectively **deletes** observations, so HY neither uses all the data nor weights what it uses neutrally. MM instead performs a lossless Fourier interpolation between events and treats a missing observation as missing.

**Why it matters:** the two estimators are **not two noisy measurements of one quantity** — they encode incompatible ontologies. HY assumes a latent continuous process partially observed; MM assumes events are the primitives. Any covariance number quoted at high frequency is therefore only meaningful together with the modelling assumption that produced it, and the gap between the two is itself informative.

### Reproducibility as a first-class contribution

The authors state plainly that much of the work is not fundamentally new and that the intended contribution is a set of transparent R code patterns that ease replication. The paper cites Ioannidis and Doucouliagos (2013) on the credibility of empirical economics, and it footnotes the replication crisis explicitly when noting that Renò (2001) borrowed GARCH parameters from Andersen and Bollerslev (1998) while writing a **different** SDE — a discrepancy the authors only discovered by re-implementing both and then publishing both specifications side by side (GarchReno.R and GarchAndersen.R). Every figure in the paper names the R script that regenerates it, all thirteen algorithms are printed as pseudocode, and code, project report and dataset each carry their own DOI.

**Why it matters:** the GARCH discrepancy is a concrete demonstration that a **specification error can survive twenty years of citation** when only prose and parameters are published. Naming the script per figure, printing pseudocode for every step, and DOI-stamping code and data is the mechanism that caught it — a template worth copying regardless of subject matter.

## 4. Algorithm Logic — Step-by-Step

Thirteen algorithms are printed in the paper's Section 8. They fall into three groups: the two estimators plus their helpers (Algorithms 1-4), the five path simulators (Algorithms 5-9), and the four data-aggregation schemes (Algorithms 10-13). Each is covered below with its own worked example.

### 4.1 Time-Rescaling (Algorithm 2)

**Goal:** map real trading timestamps onto the interval $[0,2\pi]$ so that Fourier analysis, which needs a $2\pi$-periodic domain, can be applied to irregularly spaced trade times.

**Inputs:** $T$, an $n\times m$ matrix of asynchronous sampled times for $m$ assets. **Outputs:** $\tau$, the same times rescaled to $[0,2\pi]$.

**Steps:**

1. **Find the global window** — set $t_{\min}$ to the minimum and $t_{\max}$ to the maximum value across the *whole* matrix, not per asset. Using one common window is what keeps different assets on a comparable phase axis; rescaling each asset separately would destroy the relative timing information the estimator depends on.
2. **Rescale every timestamp linearly** — for each asset $j$ and each observation $i$,
   $$\tau_{ij} = \frac{2\pi\,(t_j-t_{\min})}{t_{\max}-t_{\min}},$$
   where $t_j$ is the raw timestamp and $\tau_{ij}$ its rescaled position. The map is affine, so it preserves the *ordering* of trades and the *ratios of gaps* between them while discarding the absolute clock.
3. **Return $\tau$** for use by Algorithms 1 and 4.

**Worked example:** dataset D spans $t_{\min}=0$ to $t_{\max}=6$ seconds, so the map is $\tau=2\pi t/6=\pi t/3$. Asset 1's times $0,2,4,6$ become $\tau=0,\;2.09440,\;4.18879,\;6.28319$. Asset 2's times $0,3,6$ become $\tau=0,\;3.14159,\;6.28319$. Check the structure survived: asset 1's gaps were $2,2,2$ seconds and are now $2.09440$ each; asset 2's gaps were $3,3$ and are now $3.14159$ each; and the ratio of asset 2's gap to asset 1's gap is $3.14159/2.09440=1.5$, identical to the original $3/2$.

### 4.2 Malliavin-Mancino Fourier Estimator (Algorithm 1)

**Goal:** estimate the integrated covariance and correlation matrix from asynchronously observed prices without synchronising, interpolating or bar-aggregating the data first.

**Inputs:** $P$, an $n\times m$ matrix of asynchronously sampled prices; $T$, the matching $n\times m$ matrix of times; and a cutoff $N$ that fixes the frequency set $K$. **Outputs:** the integrated covariance matrix $\Sigma$ and the correlation matrix $R$.

**Steps:**

1. **Rescale time** — run Algorithm 2 to move all timestamps to $[0,2\pi]$.
2. **Per asset, extract its own trade times and log prices** — slice the rescaled times $\tilde\tau\leftarrow\tau(i)$ for asset $i$ and take $\tilde\varphi\leftarrow\ln\big(p(\tilde\tau)\big)$. Each asset keeps its own irregular grid; nothing is aligned. This is the step where MM avoids the interpolation bias that afflicts realised-covariance methods.
3. **Compute log returns on that asset's own grid** — $\delta_j\leftarrow\tilde\varphi(\tilde\tau_{j+1})-\tilde\varphi(\tilde\tau_j)$ for $j=1,\dots,n-1$. There is one fewer return than trades.
4. **Compute the complex Fourier coefficients for every frequency $k$** — attaching each return to the *left* endpoint of its interval,
   $$\tilde c^{+}_k \leftarrow \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} e^{\mathrm{i}k\tilde\tau_j}\delta_j, \qquad \tilde c^{-}_k \leftarrow \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} e^{-\mathrm{i}k\tilde\tau_j}\delta_j,$$
   where $\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}$. For real returns $\tilde c^{-}_k$ is the complex conjugate of $\tilde c^{+}_k$, so only one needs computing in practice. Intuitively $\tilde c^{\pm}_k$ measures how much of the asset's return flow oscillates at frequency $k$ over the session.
5. **Gather coefficients per asset** — store $c^{+}(i)\leftarrow \tilde c^{+}$ and $c^{-}(i)\leftarrow \tilde c^{-}$, then repeat steps 2-4 for the next asset.
6. **Combine coefficients into (co-)volatility** — for the pair $(i,j)$,
   $$\Sigma_{ii}\leftarrow \frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{k\in K}\left[c^{+}_k(i)\,c^{-}_k(i)\right],\qquad \Sigma_{ij}\leftarrow \frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{k\in K}\left[c^{+}_k(i)\,c^{-}_k(j)\right],\qquad \Sigma_{ji}\leftarrow \Sigma_{ij},$$
   with $K$ the set of retained frequencies and $|K|$ its cardinality; for $K=\{-N,\dots,N\}$, $|K|=2N+1$ and this expression is algebraically identical to equation (2.2). Symmetrising by $\Sigma_{ji}\leftarrow\Sigma_{ij}$ is legitimate because summing over a symmetric $K$ leaves only the real part, and $c^{+}_k(j)c^{-}_k(i)$ is the conjugate of $c^{+}_k(i)c^{-}_k(j)$.
7. **Normalise to correlation** — $R_{ij}\leftarrow \Sigma_{ij}/(\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii}}\sqrt{\Sigma_{jj}})$, then return $(\Sigma,R)$.
8. **Choose $N$ before step 6, not after** — the paper specifies the rule in prose rather than as an equation. Reconstructing it: the average-based cutoff used for the reference lines in the replication figures is $N_{\text{avg}}=\lfloor n_{\min}/2\rfloor$, with $n_{\min}$ the trade count of the sparser asset, because $n$ points on $[0,2\pi]$ have mean spacing $2\pi/n$ and hence Nyquist wavenumber $\pi/(2\pi/n)=n/2$; whereas the "true" cutoff used everywhere else in the paper is driven by the **shortest** observed gap, $N_{\text{Nyq}}=\lfloor \pi/\min_k\Delta\tau_k\rfloor$. The second is never smaller than the first, so the true cutoff always probes higher frequencies and returns lower correlations.

**Worked example:** on dataset D the arithmetic is carried out in full in Summary point 2; here are the intermediate values in step order. After step 1, $\tau^{(1)}=(0,\,2.09440,\,4.18879,\,6.28319)$ and $\tau^{(2)}=(0,\,3.14159,\,6.28319)$. After step 3, $\delta^1=(0.00995033,\,-0.00496278,\,0.01481506)$ and $\delta^2=(0.00399202,\,0.01581073)$. Applying step 8, the sparser asset has $n_{\min}=3$, so $N=\lfloor 3/2\rfloor=1$, $K=\{-1,0,1\}$, $|K|=3$. After step 4, $c^{+}_1(1)=0.00502419-0.01712818\mathrm{i}$, $c^{+}_0(1)=0.01980261$, $c^{+}_1(2)=-0.01181871$, $c^{+}_0(2)=0.01980275$. After step 6, $\Sigma_{11}=3.43126\times10^{-4}$, $\Sigma_{22}=2.23838\times10^{-4}$, $\Sigma_{12}=9.11277\times10^{-5}$. After step 7, $R_{12}=\mathbf{0.3288}$.

An independent cross-check of step 6 that readers can reuse as a unit test: because $\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{|k|\le N}e^{\mathrm{i}kx}$ is the normalised Dirichlet kernel, which for $N=1$ equals $\frac{1+2\cos x}{3}$, the same $\Sigma_{12}$ can be assembled pairwise as $\sum_{l,m}\delta^1_l\delta^2_m\frac{1+2\cos(\tau^1_l-\tau^2_m)}{3}$. The six kernel weights are $1,\,-\frac13,\,0,\,\frac23,\,0,\,\frac23$, giving $3.97220\times10^{-5}-5.24472\times10^{-5}+0-5.23111\times10^{-5}+0+1.561855\times10^{-4}=9.11492\times10^{-5}$, matching $9.11277\times10^{-5}$ to rounding. The two **zero** weights show concretely that MM does not simply add up every overlapping pair the way HY does — it phase-weights pairs and can cancel those sitting on a kernel zero.

### 4.3 Kanatani Weight Matrix (Algorithm 4)

**Goal:** build the 0/1 matrix that tells the HY estimator which pairs of return intervals overlap in calendar time and therefore contribute to the covariance.

**Inputs:** $\tau$, an $n\times 2$ matrix of rescaled asynchronous sampled times for the two assets being paired. **Outputs:** $W$, an $N_i\times N_j$ matrix of weights.

**Steps:**

1. **Initialise** — set $W$ to an $N_i\times N_j$ matrix of zeros, where $N_i$ and $N_j$ are the numbers of return intervals for assets $i$ and $j$. Starting from zero means non-overlapping pairs are excluded by default.
2. **Test every pair of intervals for overlap** — loop over interval index $k$ for asset $i$ and interval index $l$ for asset $j$, and if
   $$\left(t^{i}_{k-1},\,t^{i}_{k}\right]\cap\left(t^{j}_{l-1},\,t^{j}_{l}\right]\neq\emptyset$$
   then set $w_{kl}=1$. Two left-open intervals overlap exactly when $t^i_{k-1} < t^j_l$ and $t^j_{l-1} < t^i_k$, which is the practical test to code.
3. **Return $W$** for use in Algorithm 3.

*Implementation caveat in the printed pseudocode.* Algorithm 4 as typeset reads "for j = 1 to Ni" then "for i = 1 to Nj" — the loop variables and their bounds are transposed relative to their meaning — and then assigns $w_{kl}$ using indices $k,l$ that the loops never introduce. The intent is unambiguous from equation (2.5), but anyone transcribing the pseudocode literally will index the wrong dimension.

**Worked example:** dataset D gives asset 1 the intervals $J_1=(0,2]$, $J_2=(2,4]$, $J_3=(4,6]$ and asset 2 the intervals $J'_1=(0,3]$, $J'_2=(3,6]$, so $W$ is $3\times2$. Row 1: $J_1$ versus $J'_1$ needs $0<3$ and $0<2$, both true, so $w_{11}=1$; $J_1$ versus $J'_2$ needs $0<6$ (true) and $3<2$ (false), so $w_{12}=0$. Row 2: $J_2$ versus $J'_1$ needs $2<3$ and $0<4$, so $w_{21}=1$; $J_2$ versus $J'_2$ needs $2<6$ and $3<4$, so $w_{22}=1$. Row 3: $J_3$ versus $J'_1$ needs $4<3$, false, so $w_{31}=0$; $J_3$ versus $J'_2$ needs $4<6$ and $3<6$, so $w_{32}=1$. The finished matrix has rows $(1,0)$, $(1,1)$, $(0,1)$, with $\sum_{kl}w_{kl}=4$ of 6 possible pairs active. Row 2 carries **two** ones: asset 1's middle return will be counted twice, the multiple-contribution mechanism in its smallest possible form.

### 4.4 Hayashi-Yoshida Estimator (Algorithm 3)

**Goal:** estimate integrated covariance directly from non-synchronous observations by summing the cross-products of every pair of returns whose observation intervals overlap in time.

**Inputs:** $P$, an $n\times m$ matrix of asynchronously sampled prices; $T$, the matching times. **Outputs:** $\Sigma$ and $R$.

**Steps:**

1. **Loop over every entry of the $m\times m$ matrix $\Sigma$** — HY is applied pairwise, so the outer structure is a double loop over assets $i$ and $j$.
2. **Compute each asset's log returns on its own grid** — $\delta_i \leftarrow \ln\big(p_i(t_k)\big)-\ln\big(p_i(t_{k-1})\big)$ and $\delta_j \leftarrow \ln\big(p_j(t_k)\big)-\ln\big(p_j(t_{k-1})\big)$. As printed, the pseudocode reuses the same symbol $t_k$ for both assets even though each has its own timestamps; the intent, fixed by (2.4), is that $\delta_i$ lives on asset $i$'s intervals $J_i=(t_{i-1},t_i]$ and $\delta_j$ on asset $j$'s intervals.
3. **Build the overlap weights** — call Algorithm 4 to obtain the Kanatani matrix $W$ for the pair.
4. **Contract the returns through the weights** — $\Sigma_{ij}\leftarrow \delta_i' W \delta_j$, the matrix form of equation (2.4). Every overlapping pair contributes with weight 1 and every disjoint pair with weight 0; no interpolation, synchronisation or bar construction is involved anywhere. On the diagonal $W$ becomes the identity (an asset's own intervals overlap only themselves), so $\Sigma_{ii}$ reduces to plain **realised variance**.
5. **Normalise** — $R_{ij}\leftarrow \Sigma_{ij}/(\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii}}\sqrt{\Sigma_{jj}})$, then return $(\Sigma,R)$.

**Worked example:** on dataset D, step 2 gives $\delta^1=(0.00995033,\,-0.00496278,\,0.01481506)$ and $\delta^2=(0.00399202,\,0.01581073)$; step 3 gives the $W$ built in 4.3. Step 4, term by term over the four active pairs: $(1,1)$ contributes $0.00995033\times0.00399202=3.97220\times10^{-5}$; $(2,1)$ contributes $-0.00496278\times0.00399202=-1.98096\times10^{-5}$; $(2,2)$ contributes $-0.00496278\times0.01581073=-7.84666\times10^{-5}$; $(3,2)$ contributes $0.01481506\times0.01581073=2.342782\times10^{-4}$. Running total: $3.97220\times10^{-5}\to1.99124\times10^{-5}\to-5.85542\times10^{-5}\to\mathbf{1.75724\times10^{-4}}=\Sigma_{12}$. The diagonals from step 4 are $\Sigma_{11}=0.00995033^2+0.00496278^2+0.01481506^2=\mathbf{3.43124\times10^{-4}}$ and $\Sigma_{22}=0.00399202^2+0.01581073^2=\mathbf{2.65915\times10^{-4}}$. Step 5 gives $R_{12}=1.75724\times10^{-4}/(0.01852361\times0.01630998)=\mathbf{0.5818}$, against MM's 0.3288 on the identical data — a **77% higher** correlation, produced entirely by the double counting of $\delta^1_2$ in row 2 of $W$.

### 4.5 Repeated-Trade Aggregation (Algorithm 10)

**Goal:** collapse multiple trades that share the same timestamp into a single volume-weighted observation, because Bloomberg only stamps to the second and block or sliced trades routinely collide.

**Inputs:** $T_i$ trade times, $S_i$ trade prices, $V_i$ trade volumes. **Outputs:** unique times $T^*$, aggregated prices $S^*$, aggregated volumes $V^*$.

**Steps:**

1. **Identify the unique trading times** $t^*_j$, $j=1,\dots,M$ — this defines how many output observations there will be.
2. **Group trades by timestamp** — gather all transactions sharing $t^*_j$ into the set $J_j$, for $j=1,\dots,M$.
3. **Volume-weight the prices within each group** —
   $$s^*_j = \frac{\sum_{i\in J_j}\text{price}_i\times\text{volume}_i}{\sum_i \text{volume}_i},$$
   i.e. a VWAP over the colliding trades. Volume weighting rather than a simple mean means a 10,000-share print is not given the same influence as a 100-share print.
4. **Sum the volumes** — $V^*_j = \sum_{i\in J_j} V_i$, preserving total traded quantity so downstream volume clocks stay correct.
5. **Return** $T^*=\{t^*_j\}$, $S^*=\{s^*_j\}$, $V^*=\{V^*_j\}$.

**Worked example:** suppose three prints arrive at $t=20$ s: $(S=101,V=200)$, $(S=101.5,V=100)$, $(S=100.8,V=50)$. Step 1 gives one unique time, $t^*_1=20$. Step 2 puts all three in $J_1$. Step 3: numerator $=101\times200+101.5\times100+100.8\times50=20{,}200+10{,}150+5{,}040=35{,}390$; denominator $=200+100+50=350$; so $s^*_1=35{,}390/350=\mathbf{101.1143}$. Step 4: $V^*_1=\mathbf{350}$. Sanity check on the weighting: the simple mean of the three prices would be $101.1$, and the VWAP is pulled slightly above it because the highest-priced print carries more volume than the lowest-priced one. Three observations became one, so the asset's return series loses two intra-second returns it never really had.

### 4.6 Bar-Data Construction, Closing and VWAP (Algorithm 11)

**Goal:** aggregate tick data into fixed-length calendar-time bars, producing both an OHLCV summary and a VWAP price, so that the Epps effect can be traced across 1-hour, 10-minute and 1-minute sampling.

**Inputs:** $T_i$ trade times, $S_i$ prices, $V_i$ volumes, and the bar length $\tau$ in units of time. **Outputs:** bar timestamps $t^*$, opens $O$, highs $H$, lows $L$, closes $C$, bar volumes $V^*$ and $\mathrm{VWAP}$.

**Steps:**

1. **Partition trades into bars** — gather trades into sets $\{J_j\}_{j=1}^{M}$ determined by $\tau$.
2. **Set the open** — $O_1=S_1$ for the first bar, and $O_j=C_{j-1}$ thereafter, so bars are chained with no gaps.
3. **Set high, low, close** — $H_j=\max\{S_i\in J_j\}$, $L_j=\min\{S_i\in J_j\}$, $C_j=$ the last $S_i\in J_j$. The **closing** aggregation used in the paper's Figure 6 is exactly $C_j$: it decimates the bar down to its final trade and shifts that trade to the bar end.
4. **Sum volume** — $V^*_j=\sum_{i\in J_j}V_i$.
5. **Compute the VWAP price** —
   $$\mathrm{VWAP}_j = \frac{\sum_{i\in J_j} S_i\times V_i}{\sum_i V_i}.$$
   This is the aggregation used in Figure 7: instead of decimating, it volume-averages every trade in the bar and then shifts the average to the bar end.
6. **Stamp the bar** — $t^*_j = T_1 + j\tau$. Note this anchors the grid on the **first trade time**, not on the session open, so the grid drifts with the first print of the day.
7. **Return** all seven series.

**Worked example** with $\tau=60$ s and a first bar containing three trades: $(t=5,S=100,V=100)$, $(t=20,S=101,V=200)$, $(t=50,S=100.5,V=300)$. Step 2: $O_1=100$. Step 3: $H_1=101$, $L_1=100$, $C_1=\mathbf{100.5}$. Step 4: $V^*_1=600$. Step 5: numerator $=100\times100+101\times200+100.5\times300=10{,}000+20{,}200+30{,}150=60{,}350$; so $\mathrm{VWAP}_1=60{,}350/600=\mathbf{100.5833}$. Step 6: $t^*_1=5+60=\mathbf{65}$ s. The closing price (100.5) and the VWAP (100.5833) differ by 8.3 basis points on the same bar; the paper's empirical finding is that this difference does not materially reorganise the correlation structure, it only accentuates it — compare MM 1-hour closing $|\rho|=0.2829$ with MM 1-hour VWAP $|\rho|=0.3172$.

### 4.7 Intrinsic Volume-Time Averaging (Algorithm 12)

**Goal:** resample each asset onto its **own** volume clock, so that a "bar" is a fixed number of shares traded rather than a fixed number of seconds, implementing Derman's intrinsic-time framework.

**Inputs:** $T_i$ trade times, $S_i$ prices, $V_i$ volumes, and the bucket size
$$v_j = \frac{\mathrm{ADV}_j}{N},$$
where $\mathrm{ADV}_j$ is the Average Daily Volume of asset $j$ over the period and $N$ is the number of buckets per day. **Outputs:** the intrinsic-time price series $P=\{P_1,\dots,P_M\}$.

**Steps:**

1. **Expand each trade into one entry per share** — repeat observation $S_i$ exactly $V_i$ times, preserving the original ordering, giving $I=\sum_i V_i$ entries. This converts a volume threshold into a simple counting problem: a bucket is now "the next $v_j$ entries".
2. **Initialise the intrinsic clock** — set $\tau = 0$.
3. **Advance one intrinsic tick per full bucket** — while $\tau v_j < I$, increment $\tau \leftarrow \tau+1$ and, for all expanded indices $i \in [(\tau-1)v_j+1,\ \tau v_j]$, compute the bucket price
   $$P_\tau = \frac{\sum_i S_i \times V_i}{\sum_i V_i}.$$
   The loop condition stops before a partial final bucket, so trailing volume is discarded rather than producing a short bar.
4. **Return** $P=\{P_1,\dots,P_M\}$ with $M=\tau$ at loop exit. Each asset is sampled uniformly in **its own** average intrinsic time, with $N$ chosen so that all assets produce the same number of intervals per day on average.

*Substantive issue in the printed formula.* Because step 1 has already expanded each trade into $V_i$ identical entries, applying $\sum_i S_iV_i / \sum_i V_i$ **over the expanded index set** weights each original trade by $V_i^2$, not $V_i$ — the volume is counted twice. The companion Algorithm 13 avoids this by taking a plain mean over the expanded entries ($\sum_k S^*_{kj}/v$), which is the correct VWAP. The worked example below quantifies the discrepancy.

**Worked example:** take asset A with three trades $(S=10.0, V=60)$, $(S=10.1, V=70)$, $(S=10.2, V=90)$, so $I=220$, and set $\mathrm{ADV}_A=220$ with $N=2$, giving $v_A=110$. Step 1 produces 60 entries at 10.0, then 70 at 10.1, then 90 at 10.2. Step 3, first pass ($\tau=1$, entries 1-110): 60 entries at 10.0 and 50 at 10.1, so the numerator is $60(10.0\times60)+50(10.1\times70)=36{,}000+35{,}350=71{,}350$ and the denominator is $60\times60+50\times70=3{,}600+3{,}500=7{,}100$, giving $P_1=71{,}350/7{,}100=\mathbf{10.04930}$. The true VWAP of those 110 shares is $(60\times10.0+50\times10.1)/110=1{,}105/110=\mathbf{10.04545}$ — a **3.8 basis point** discrepancy caused by the double volume weighting. Second pass ($\tau=2$, entries 111-220): 20 entries at 10.1 and 90 at 10.2, numerator $20(10.1\times70)+90(10.2\times90)=14{,}140+82{,}620=96{,}760$, denominator $20\times70+90\times90=1{,}400+8{,}100=9{,}500$, so $P_2=96{,}760/9{,}500=\mathbf{10.18526}$ against a true VWAP of $(20\times10.1+90\times10.2)/110=1{,}120/110=\mathbf{10.18182}$. The loop then stops because $2\times110=220$ is not less than $I=220$, so $M=2$. The resulting intrinsic-time log return is $\ln(10.18526/10.04930)=\mathbf{0.0134385}$, versus $\ln(10.18182/10.04545)=\mathbf{0.0134840}$ if the plain VWAP were used — a 0.34% relative error in the return that feeds the covariance estimator.

**Sizing the buckets on the real data:** for MNP ($\mathrm{ADV}=340{,}381.4$) with $N=48$, $v_{\mathrm{MNP}}=7{,}091.3$ shares per bucket; for FSR ($\mathrm{ADV}=7{,}698{,}648$), $v_{\mathrm{FSR}}=160{,}388.5$. The paper uses $N=8,48,480$ to mimic 1-hour, 10-minute and 1-minute bars, which implies an **8-hour** trading day ($8\times1\text{h}=48\times10\text{min}=480\times1\text{min}=480$ minutes).

### 4.8 Synchronised Volume-Time Averaging (Algorithm 13)

**Goal:** the paper's own contribution — sample all assets on a **shared** volume-bucket size while allowing each asset's bucket to close at whatever calendar moment it happens to fill, so that volume time is synchronised across assets but calendar time is not.

**Inputs:** $T_{ij}$ trade time, $S_{ij}$ price and $V_{ij}$ volume for the $j$-th asset, and one common bucket size
$$v = \frac{\mathrm{ADV}^*}{N},$$
where $\mathrm{ADV}^*$ is the Average Daily Volume of the chosen baseline asset — the **least liquid** (MNP) in one experiment and the **most liquid** (FSR) in the other — and $N$ is the number of buckets per day. **Outputs:** per-asset volume-time price series $P_j=\{P_{lj}\}_{l=1}^{M}$, each entry carrying the calendar timestamp at which it filled.

**Steps:**

1. **Build the union of trading times** — collect the unique trading times $\{T^*\}_{i^*=1}^{N^*}$ across all $j$ assets, noting $N^*\ge N_j$ for every asset, where $N_j$ is that asset's own number of trade times. Walking this union is what keeps every asset on one shared event sequence.
2. **Initialise** — set $\tau = 0$.
3. **At each union time, pour that instant's shares into each asset's queue** — expand $S_{i^*j}$ as many times as $V_{i^*j}$ and append to the storage set $A_j = \{S^*_{kj}\}_{k=1}^{K_j}$ for asset $j$. $K_j$ is that asset's current unconsumed share count.
4. **Drain any queue that has exceeded the threshold** — while any $K_j > v$, increment $\tau\leftarrow\tau+1$ and, for each asset $j$: if $K_j > v$, emit the bucket price
   $$P_{\tau j} = \frac{\sum_k S^*_{kj}}{v}$$
   over the first $v$ entries, then remove those $v$ entries from $A_j$; otherwise set $P_{\tau j} = \mathrm{NaN}$. Because the expansion in step 3 already replicated each price once per share, this plain mean **is** the volume-weighted average price — this is the correct construction that Algorithm 12 gets wrong.
5. **Emit NaN deliberately** — the $\mathrm{NaN}$ entries are the mechanism that preserves asynchrony: at volume-time index $\tau$ some assets have filled a bucket and others have not, so the sampled matrix is genuinely ragged and the estimators see real asynchrony rather than a synchronised grid.
6. **Return** $P_j$ with $M=\tau$ at the end of the loops. Each realised bucket carries the calendar timestamp at which it filled.

**Worked example** with a shared bucket $v=100$ shares. Asset A trades $(t=1, S=10.0, V=60)$, $(t=2, S=10.1, V=70)$, $(t=4, S=10.2, V=90)$; asset B trades $(t=1, S=20.0, V=120)$, $(t=3, S=20.4, V=50)$, $(t=4, S=20.2, V=40)$. The union of times is $T^*=\{1,2,3,4\}$.

- $t=1$: queues become $K_A=60$ (all at 10.0), $K_B=120$ (all at 20.0). B exceeds $v$, so $\tau=1$. A: $K_A=60\le100$, so $P_{1A}=\mathrm{NaN}$. B: $P_{1B}=(100\times20.0)/100=\mathbf{20.00}$, stamped at calendar $t=1$; remove 100, leaving $K_B=20$. Re-check: $60,20$ — neither exceeds 100, exit.
- $t=2$: A appends 70 at 10.1, so $K_A=130$. $\tau=2$. A: $P_{2A}=(60\times10.0+40\times10.1)/100=(600+404)/100=\mathbf{10.04}$, stamped at $t=2$; remove 100, leaving $K_A=30$. B: $K_B=20\le100$, so $P_{2B}=\mathrm{NaN}$. Re-check: $30,20$ — exit.
- $t=3$: B appends 50 at 20.4, so $K_B=70$. Neither queue exceeds 100, so **no bucket is emitted at all**.
- $t=4$: A appends 90 at 10.2 ($K_A=120$); B appends 40 at 20.2 ($K_B=110$). $\tau=3$. A: $P_{3A}=(30\times10.1+70\times10.2)/100=(303+714)/100=\mathbf{10.17}$, leaving $K_A=20$. B: $P_{3B}=(20\times20.0+50\times20.4+30\times20.2)/100=(400+1{,}020+606)/100=\mathbf{20.26}$, leaving $K_B=10$. Re-check: $20,10$ — exit.

Final output: $P_A=[\mathrm{NaN},\,10.04,\,10.17]$ and $P_B=[20.00,\,\mathrm{NaN},\,20.26]$. Dropping the NaNs and keeping the calendar stamps, the estimators receive asset A at $(t{=}2,\,10.04)$ and $(t{=}4,\,10.17)$, and asset B at $(t{=}1,\,20.00)$ and $(t{=}4,\,20.26)$ — two series that are aligned in **volume** count but land at different **calendar** moments, which is exactly the asynchrony the design is meant to preserve. Note also that A produced only one usable return and B only one, from three volume-time indices: the scheme trades sample size for realism, which is why the paper had to drop the 1-hour equivalent when synchronising to the most liquid ticker.

### 4.9 Correlated Geometric Brownian Motion Simulator (Algorithm 5)

**Goal:** generate a correlated bivariate GBM price path as the benchmark data-generating process with a known induced correlation.

**Inputs:** $n$ price points to simulate, drift vector $\mu$ ($d\times1$), covariance matrix $\Sigma$ ($d\times d$), and start prices $S(0)$. **Outputs:** the simulated price paths $S$.

**Steps:**

1. **Factor the covariance** — compute the Cholesky decomposition $A$ with $AA'=\Sigma$. This is what converts independent normal shocks into correlated ones with exactly the requested correlation.
2. **Draw independent shocks** — $Z\sim\mathcal{N}_d(0,I_{d\times d})$ at each time step.
3. **Advance the exact GBM solution** —
   $$S_i(t_{k+1}) = S_i(t_k)\exp\left[\left(\mu_i-\tfrac12\sigma_i^2\right)(t_{k+1}-t_k) + \sqrt{t_{k+1}-t_k}\sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{ik}Z_k\right],$$
   where $\mu_i$ is the drift, $\sigma_i^2=\Sigma_{ii}$, and $-\tfrac12\sigma_i^2$ is the Itô correction that makes $\mathbb{E}[S(t)]=S(0)e^{\mu t}$. Using the exact solution rather than an Euler step means there is no discretisation bias to confound the estimator comparison.
4. **Return** $S$.

**Worked example** with the paper's parameters $\mu_1=\mu_2=0.01$, $\sigma_1^2=0.1$, $\sigma_2^2=0.2$, $\rho_{12}=0.35$, $S(0)=\mathrm{R}100$ and a one-second step $\Delta t = 1/86400$ day. Step 1: $\sigma_1=0.316228$, $\sigma_2=0.447214$, off-diagonal covariance $=0.35\times0.316228\times0.447214=0.0494975$; Cholesky gives $A_{11}=0.316228$, $A_{21}=0.0494975/0.316228=0.156525$, $A_{22}=\sqrt{0.2-0.156525^2}=\sqrt{0.1755}=0.418927$. Step 2: draw $Z=(0.5,-1.2)$. Step 3, with $\Delta t = 1.157407\times10^{-5}$ and $\sqrt{\Delta t}=3.40262\times10^{-3}$: asset 1's exponent is $(0.01-0.05)(1.157407\times10^{-5})+3.40262\times10^{-3}\times(0.316228\times0.5)=-4.62963\times10^{-7}+5.38007\times10^{-4}=5.37544\times10^{-4}$, so $S_1=100e^{5.37544\times10^{-4}}=\mathbf{100.0538}$; asset 2's exponent is $(0.01-0.1)(1.157407\times10^{-5})+3.40262\times10^{-3}\times(0.156525\times0.5+0.418927\times(-1.2))=-1.041667\times10^{-6}-1.444437\times10^{-3}=-1.445479\times10^{-3}$, so $S_2=100e^{-1.445479\times10^{-3}}=\mathbf{99.8556}$. Verification of step 1: $A_{21}/\sigma_2 = 0.156525/0.447214 = 0.35 = \rho_{12}$, confirming the Cholesky factor induces exactly the requested correlation.

### 4.10 Merton Jump-Diffusion Simulator (Algorithm 6)

**Goal:** add a compound-Poisson jump component to correlated GBM, to test whether jumps rather than asynchrony separate the two estimators.

**Inputs:** $n$, drift $\mu$, covariance $\Sigma$, Poisson intensity vector $\lambda$, lognormal jump location $a$, lognormal jump standard deviation $b$, start prices. **Outputs:** price paths $S$.

**Steps:**

1. **Draw the diffusion shocks** — $Z\sim\mathcal{N}_d(0,I)$, with $A$ the Cholesky factor of $\Sigma$ as in 4.9.
2. **Draw the jump count** — $N_i\sim\mathrm{Poisson}\big(\lambda_i(t_{k+1}-t_k)\big)$ for each asset independently. Independence across assets is the key modelling choice: the jump components are uncorrelated with each other and with the diffusion.
3. **Draw the jump size shock** — $Z_2\sim\mathcal{N}_1(0,1)$.
4. **Assemble the aggregate jump for the step** — $M = a_iN_i + b_i\sqrt{N_i}\,Z_2$. Summing $N_i$ i.i.d. normal log-jumps of mean $a_i$ and standard deviation $b_i$ gives mean $a_iN_i$ and standard deviation $b_i\sqrt{N_i}$, which is what this line encodes.
5. **Advance the log price** — $X_i(t_{k+1}) = X_i(t_k) + (\mu_i-\tfrac12\sigma_i^2)(t_{k+1}-t_k) + \sqrt{t_{k+1}-t_k}\sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{ik}Z_k + M$, with $X(0)=\ln(\text{start price})$.
6. **Exponentiate and return** — $S=\exp(X)$.

**Worked example** with $\lambda=0.2$, $a=0$, $b=100$, $\Delta t=1/86400$ day. Step 2: $\lambda\Delta t = 0.2\times1.157407\times10^{-5}=2.3148\times10^{-6}$, so in almost every second $N_i=0$ and $M=0$, leaving the pure GBM step of 4.9. On the rare second where $N_i=1$, with $Z_2=0.01$, step 4 gives $M = 0\times1 + 100\times\sqrt{1}\times0.01 = \mathbf{1.0}$ in log space, so step 6 multiplies the price by $e^{1.0}=\mathbf{2.718}$ — a 172% move in one second. Step 5's total exponent that second is $5.37544\times10^{-4}+1.0=1.000538$, so $S_1 = 100e^{1.000538}=\mathbf{271.97}$. Comparing variance contributions over the paper's 10,000-second window ($0.11574$ day): the diffusion contributes $\sigma_1^2\times0.11574 = 0.1\times0.11574=1.1574\times10^{-2}$ of log variance (standard deviation $0.1076$), while the jump component contributes $\mathbb{E}[N](a^2+b^2)=(0.2\times0.11574)\times(0+100^2)=231.5$ (standard deviation $15.21$) — the jump variance is **20,000 times** the diffusion variance. This is a substantive parameter concern: with $b=100$ the "jump-diffusion" experiment is, conditional on any jump occurring, a pure-jump experiment, so the reported collapse of correlation toward zero as $\lambda$ rises is arithmetically guaranteed rather than a subtle finding. A jump standard deviation of $b=0.01$-$0.10$ in log terms would be the economically sensible range.

### 4.11 GARCH(1,1) Simulator (Algorithm 7)

**Goal:** generate correlated paths with stochastic, mean-reverting volatility, to test whether volatility clustering separates the estimators.

**Inputs:** $n$, mean-reversion rates $\theta$ ($2\times1$), volatility-of-volatility $\lambda$ ($2\times1$), long-run variances $w$ ($2\times1$), correlation $\rho$, starting variances and starting prices. **Outputs:** price paths $S$.

**Steps:**

1. **Draw a variance shock** — $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$.
2. **Advance each variance by Euler discretisation of (3.7)** —
   $$\sigma_i^2(t_{k+1}) = \sigma_i^2(t_k) + \theta_i\big(w_i-\sigma_i^2(t_k)\big)(t_{k+1}-t_k) + \sqrt{2\lambda_i\theta_i(t_{k+1}-t_k)}\;\sigma_i(t_k)\,Z,$$
   where the diffusion coefficient factorises as $\sqrt{2\lambda_i\theta_i\sigma_i^2}\sqrt{\Delta t}=\sqrt{2\lambda_i\theta_i\Delta t}\,\sigma_i$. (The printed pseudocode writes $w_1$ inside the loop over $i$; from (3.7) this must be $w_i$.)
3. **Rebuild the instantaneous covariance** — form $\Sigma^*$ from the freshly updated $\sigma^2(t_{k+1})$ and the fixed correlation $\rho$, and take its Cholesky factor $A^*$. Because volatilities move every step, the factor has to be recomputed every step.
4. **Draw price shocks and advance the log price** — $Z^*\sim\mathcal{N}_d(0,I)$ and $X_i(t_{k+1}) = X_i(t_k) + \sqrt{t_{k+1}-t_k}\sum_{k=1}^{d}A^*_{ik}Z^*_k$, with $X(0)=\ln(\text{start price})$. Note (3.6) has no drift term, so the log price is a pure martingale.
5. **Exponentiate and return** — $S=\exp(X)$.

**Worked example** with $\theta_1=0.035$, $w_1=0.636$, $\lambda_1=0.296$, starting at the long-run level $\sigma_1^2(t_0)=0.636$ (so $\sigma_1(t_0)=0.797496$) and $\Delta t=1/86400$. Step 2: the mean-reversion term is $0.035\times(0.636-0.636)\times\Delta t = 0$ exactly; the diffusion coefficient is $\sqrt{2\times0.296\times0.035\times1.157407\times10^{-5}}=\sqrt{2.39537\times10^{-7}}=4.89425\times10^{-4}$, so with $Z=1.5$ the shock is $4.89425\times10^{-4}\times0.797496\times1.5=5.85473\times10^{-4}$ and $\sigma_1^2(t_1)=0.636+0.000585=\mathbf{0.636585}$, i.e. $\sigma_1(t_1)=0.797863$. Step 3 with $\sigma_2^2 = 0.476$ and $\rho=0.35$: $\Sigma^*_{12}=0.35\times0.797863\times\sqrt{0.476}=0.35\times0.797863\times0.689928=0.192663$, and $A^*_{11}=0.797863$, $A^*_{21}=0.192663/0.797863=0.241475$, $A^*_{22}=\sqrt{0.476-0.241475^2}=\sqrt{0.417690}=0.646289$. Step 4 with $Z^*=(0.5,-1.2)$: $X_1(t_1)=X_1(t_0)+3.40262\times10^{-3}\times0.797863\times0.5=X_1(t_0)+1.35750\times10^{-3}$, so from $X_1(t_0)=\ln 100=4.605170$ we get $X_1(t_1)=4.606528$ and $S_1=\mathbf{100.1358}$. A units observation worth carrying: $w_1=0.636$ used directly on log prices implies a long-run **daily volatility of 79.7%**, because the Andersen-Bollerslev parameters were estimated on returns expressed in **percent**. Correlation is scale-invariant here, so the reported $\rho$ recovery is unaffected, but anyone reusing the code for volatility work must rescale by $10^{-4}$ in variance.

### 4.12 Variance Gamma Simulator (Algorithm 8)

**Goal:** generate a **pure jump** process — Brownian motion evaluated on a random gamma time change — to test whether the absence of a diffusion component separates the estimators.

**Inputs:** $n$, drift $\mu$, covariance $\Sigma$, gamma scale $\beta$, start prices. **Outputs:** the paths $X$.

**Steps:**

1. **Draw the random time change** — $Y_i\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\big((t_{k+1}-t_k)/\beta_i,\ \beta_i\big)$, which has mean $\big((t_{k+1}-t_k)/\beta_i\big)\times\beta_i = t_{k+1}-t_k$. Normalising the subordinator to unit mean rate is what keeps calendar time and business time comparable on average; matching against (3.5) implies the shape rate $\alpha=1/\beta$, a value the text never states explicitly (only $\beta_1=\beta_2=1$ is given).
2. **Draw the Brownian shocks** — $Z\sim\mathcal{N}_d(0,I)$, with $A$ the Cholesky factor of $\Sigma$.
3. **Advance with the time change in place of the clock** —
   $$X_i(t_{k+1}) = X_i(t_k) + \mu Y_i + \sqrt{Y_i}\sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{ik}Z_k,$$
   i.e. the same GBM-style increment but with $\Delta t$ everywhere replaced by the random $Y_i$. Because $Y_i$ is itself random and occasionally large, increments are heavy-tailed — the pure-jump behaviour.
4. **Return** $X$. Note the algorithm returns $X$ **without exponentiating**, and Table 10 sets $X(0)=\text{start price}$ rather than $\ln(\text{start price})$, so the VG paths are arithmetic while every other simulator in the paper is geometric — a specification inconsistency, though a harmless one for correlation.

**Worked example** with $\beta=1$, $\mu=0.01$, $\Delta t = 1/86400$, and the same $A$ as 4.9. Step 1: $Y\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(1.157407\times10^{-5},\,1)$ with mean $1.157407\times10^{-5}$; suppose the draw is $Y_1=1.0\times10^{-5}$, so $\sqrt{Y_1}=3.16228\times10^{-3}$. Step 2: $Z=(0.5,-1.2)$. Step 3: $X_1(t_1) = 100 + 0.01\times10^{-5} + 3.16228\times10^{-3}\times(0.316228\times0.5) = 100 + 1\times10^{-7} + 5.00119\times10^{-4} = \mathbf{100.0005002}$, and $X_2(t_1) = 100 + 0.01\times10^{-5} + 3.16228\times10^{-3}\times(0.156525\times0.5 + 0.418927\times(-1.2)) = 100 + 1\times10^{-7} - 1.342196\times10^{-3} = \mathbf{99.9986579}$. The heavy tail is visible if the gamma draw is unlucky: a draw of $Y_1 = 4\times10^{-4}$ (40 times the mean, entirely possible for a gamma with shape $\ll 1$) multiplies the shock by $\sqrt{40}=6.32$, giving $3.16\times10^{-3}$ instead of $5.00\times10^{-4}$.

### 4.13 Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Simulator (Algorithm 9)

**Goal:** generate correlated **mean-reverting** paths, to test whether mean reversion separates the estimators.

**Inputs:** $n$, long-term price vector $\mu$, covariance $\Sigma$, mean-reversion rates $\theta$, start prices. **Outputs:** price paths $S$.

**Steps:**

1. **Draw shocks** — $Z\sim\mathcal{N}_d(0,I)$, with $A$ the Cholesky factor of $\Sigma$.
2. **Advance the log price with a pull toward the long-run level** —
   $$X_i(t_{k+1}) = X_i(t_k) + \theta_i\big(\ln(\mu_i)-X_i(t_k)\big)(t_{k+1}-t_k) + \sqrt{t_{k+1}-t_k}\sum_{k=1}^{d}A_{ik}Z_k,$$
   with $X(0)=\ln(\text{start price})$. The drift is proportional to the distance from $\ln\mu_i$ and points back toward it, so the process is stationary rather than a random walk. Note this discretisation mean-reverts the **log** price toward $\ln\mu_i$, whereas (3.8) is written on $p_i(t)$ with $\mu_1=\mu_2=100$; since Section 2.1 defines $p_i(t)=\ln S_i(t)$, the stated $\mu=100$ should be read as the long-run **price** level, which the algorithm then log-transforms.
3. **Exponentiate and return** — $S=\exp(X)$.

**Worked example** with $\theta_1=0.035$, $\mu_1=100$, $\sigma_1^2=0.1$, $S(0)=\mathrm{R}100$, $\Delta t = 1/86400$. Step 2 at the first step, where $X_1(t_0)=\ln 100=4.605170=\ln\mu_1$: the mean-reversion term is exactly zero, so $X_1(t_1)=4.605170+3.40262\times10^{-3}\times0.316228\times0.5=4.605170+5.38007\times10^{-4}=4.605708$, and $S_1=e^{4.605708}=\mathbf{100.0538}$. To see the reversion bite, suppose the path has drifted to $S_1=105$, i.e. $X_1=4.653960$, a distance of $0.048790$ above $\ln\mu_1$. The drift term is then $0.035\times(-0.048790)\times1.157407\times10^{-5}=-1.9764\times10^{-8}$ per second — tiny per step, but accumulating to $-1.9764\times10^{-8}\times28{,}800=-5.692\times10^{-4}$ over an 8-hour session, i.e. a $-5.7$ basis point pull. Against a session diffusion standard deviation of $\sigma_1\sqrt{28{,}800/86{,}400}=0.316228\times0.577=0.1826$, the reversion is negligible intraday, which is exactly why mean reversion alone does not separate MM from HY in the missing-data experiment; it only shows up once **non-uniform exponential sampling** is combined with it (Figure 5f), where the interaction produces spurious lead-lag and an Epps effect in HY.

## 5. Ideas for Development

- **Estimator-disagreement spread as an asynchrony and liquidity meter** — instead of choosing between MM and HY, run both on the same tick data (Section 4.2 and Section 4.4, sharing the returns produced at step 3 of each) and track the spread $D = R^{\mathrm{HY}}_{ij} - R^{\mathrm{MM}}_{ij}$ as a feature in its own right. The paper treats the gap as evidence in an estimator debate; the same number is a continuously observable statistic that measures how far a pair is from synchronous trading, and it needs no ground truth to compute.
  - **Strong:** highly asynchronous sampling regimes, such as the paper's own synchronised volume clock — MM reports $|\rho_{ij}|=0.0073$ against HY's $0.4265$ on the identical 48-bucket most-liquid sample (Figure 9), a spread of 0.419 that dwarfs any sampling noise *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** pairs with a large trade-rate imbalance — the number of Kanatani weights that fire for one return of the slow asset is on average $\max(n_i,n_j)/\min(n_i,n_j)$, so the HY numerator accumulates that many cross-products per slow return while both HY denominators stay fixed at their own realised variances; with the paper's FSR-to-MNP ADV ratio of $7{,}698{,}648/340{,}381.4 = 22.6$, HY's covariance numerator absorbs roughly 22.6 contributions per MNP return, and $D$ grows monotonically in that ratio *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** synchronous or near-synchronous bar data — at 1-hour closing bars the paper reports MM $0.2829 \pm 0.2101$ against HY $0.2912 \pm 0.2235$, a spread of $0.0083$ that is 4% of one cross-sectional standard deviation and therefore not measurable *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** the spread is not identified until the Fourier cutoff is pinned down — MM's covariance is a Dirichlet-kernel-weighted sum, $\Sigma_{ij}=\sum_{l,m}\delta^i_l\delta^j_m\frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{|k|\le N}e^{\mathrm{i}k(\tau_l-\tau_m)}$, and in the degenerate case $K=\{0\}$ every weight becomes 1, collapsing $\Sigma_{ij}$ to $(\sum_l\delta^i_l)(\sum_m\delta^j_m)$, the product of the two whole-session returns. $D$ therefore ranges from near zero to its maximum purely as a function of $N$, so any deployment must publish the cutoff rule of Section 4.2 step 8 alongside the number *(From AI)*

- **The MM correlation-versus-$N$ curve as a native multi-scale correlation signature** — reuse Section 4.2 exactly as written but, instead of reporting one number, sweep $N$ from small to the Nyquist cutoff and publish the whole curve $R_{ij}(N)$. Steps 1-5 of Algorithm 1 are computed once; only step 6 is re-run per $N$, so the entire multi-scale profile costs barely more than a single estimate. This is the direct analogue of a volatility signature plot for correlation, and it replaces the paper's manual ladder of 1-hour, 10-minute and 1-minute bars with a continuum.
  - **Strong:** high-frequency, densely traded instruments — the paper's Figure 5 plots error bars as the standard deviation across replications and reports that they **decrease as $N$ increases**, so the high-frequency end of the curve is the best-determined part, not the noisiest *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** any setting where re-barring is expensive — building $K$ separate bar grids costs $O(K n)$ passes over the tick data and discards every intra-bar observation, whereas the Fourier coefficients $\tilde c^{\pm}_k$ of Section 4.2 step 4 are computed once in $O(n N_{\max})$ and every $N \le N_{\max}$ is then a partial sum over already-computed terms, using all $n$ observations at every scale *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** pairs with a badly imbalanced trade count — the binding cutoff is the sparser asset, $N_{\mathrm{avg}}=\lfloor n_{\min}/2\rfloor$ (Section 4.2 step 8), so for FSR against MNP the curve saturates at MNP's Nyquist limit and the extra FSR resolution is unusable; pushing past it aliases high frequencies back onto low ones and biases the curve *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** processes whose correlation is dimensionless by construction — the paper states explicitly that every SDE it simulates has a correlation independent of the sampling interval, which is why the Monte Carlo could not study the Epps effect arising from smaller intervals at all; a flat $R_{ij}(N)$ curve therefore proves nothing about the estimator *(From paper)*

- **Port the synchronised volume clock (Algorithm 13) as a general bar engine, with the Algorithm 12 weighting bug fixed** — lift Section 4.8 wholesale as the sampling layer for any multi-asset feature pipeline, execution scheduler or backtest, using its plain-mean-over-expanded-entries rule (step 4) rather than the double-volume-weighted rule that Section 4.7 inherits from the printed Algorithm 12.
  - **Strong:** heteroskedastic intraday data — under the subordinated representation used by the paper's own Variance Gamma simulator (Section 4.12), $X=W(G(t))$ gives $\mathrm{Var}[\Delta X \mid \Delta G = v] = \sigma^2 v$, so a bar defined by a fixed volume increment $v$ has constant conditional variance by construction, whereas a fixed-calendar bar inherits the U-shaped intraday volume profile and is strongly heteroskedastic *(From AI)*
  - **Strong:** the paper reports that the intrinsic-time paradigm delivers partial recovery of Normality and of the IID assumption and copes with the random, asynchronous nature of streaming trade data (Section 5, following Easley, López de Prado and O'Hara 2012) *(From paper)*
  - **Weak:** universes with a wide liquidity spread — with one shared bucket $v=\mathrm{ADV}^*/N$, asset $j$ emits $\mathrm{ADV}_j/v = N\cdot(\mathrm{ADV}_j/\mathrm{ADV}^*)$ bars per day, so anchoring on the most liquid ticker (FSR) gives the least liquid (MNP) only $48/22.6 = 2.1$ bars per day at the 10-minute-equivalent setting and $8/22.6 = 0.35$ bars per day at the 1-hour setting — fewer than one. This is precisely why the paper had to exclude the 1-hour equivalent when synchronising to FSR *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** days whose volume departs from the average — bucket sizes are computed from **average** daily volume, so a high-volume day yields more buckets and a low-volume day fewer, which the paper states de-synchronises key events in the sampled data and creates leads and lags (Section 5.2); numerically, a day 20% above average fills an MNP 10-minute bucket in $600/1.2 = 500$ s, producing 57.6 rather than 48 buckets *(From paper)*

- **Scale-dependent correlation sign flips as a horizon selector for pairs and stat-arb** — take the paper's Table 2 finding as a screening procedure: for each candidate pair, build the correlation at several averaging scales using Section 4.6 (closing and VWAP bars) and flag pairs whose sign or magnitude changes across scales, then trade only at the scale where the relationship is stable. The paper's own point is that a monotone asynchrony model such as (3.9) can never produce a sign change, so a flip is information about market structure rather than about the estimator.
  - **Strong:** pairs that genuinely change regime across scales — BTI against NPN moves from $-0.0265$ at 1 hour to $+0.1070$ at 10 minutes under MM, and from $-0.1450$ to $+0.1445$ under HY, a flip the paper highlights as irreconcilable with lead-lag and asynchrony alone (Table 2) *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** pairs with a large 10-minute correlation and a long enough history — the sample size required to resolve $r$ at three standard errors is $n \approx (3/r)^2 + 1$, so detecting the reported $r = 0.107$ needs about $787$ bars, which at 48 ten-minute bars per day is roughly **17 trading days** — a concrete, checkable data requirement that a screen can enforce before acting *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** short samples at coarse scales — one week of JSE data gives only about $8\times5=40$ one-hour bars, so the standard error of a near-zero correlation is $\mathrm{SE}(r)\approx(1-r^2)/\sqrt{n-1}\approx1/\sqrt{39}=0.160$. The reported $-0.0265$ sits $0.17$ standard errors from zero and the entire $0.1335$ flip is under one standard error, so on this sample the flip is statistically indistinguishable from noise and the screen must be run on months, not days *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** any pair where structural effects and the Epps effect overlap — the paper's own caption to Table 2 warns that the Epps effect "can be clouded by other scale dependent structural effects", and Section 5.1 concludes it is unclear which aggregation method most faithfully represents the correlation structure across scales *(From paper)*

- **Joint volatility-signature and correlation-decay test of the microstructure-noise hypothesis** — combine the two things the paper already measures from one dataset with one estimator: the daily-rescaled volatilities of Table 1 (Section 2 point 7) and the correlation ladder of Section 4.6. Calibrate an i.i.d. noise model on the volatility signature alone, then ask whether it predicts the observed correlation decay. This turns the paper's qualitative argument against the noise framework into a falsifiable numerical test that anyone can rerun.
  - **Strong:** it needs no additional data and no additional estimator — both inputs come from the same MM estimates on the same JSE week, so the test is internally consistent by construction (Table 1 and Figures 6-7) *(From paper)*
  - **Strong:** the test rejects the noise-only explanation by a wide margin on BTI. Write the daily-equivalent realised variance at $n$ bars per day as $\mathrm{RV}(n) = \sigma^2 + n\omega^2$, with $\sigma^2$ the true daily variance and $\omega^2$ the per-observation noise variance contribution. Using an 8-hour day, $n=8$ gives $0.0075^2 = 5.625\times10^{-5}$ and $n=480$ gives $0.0501^2 = 2.51001\times10^{-3}$, so $472\omega^2 = 2.45376\times10^{-3}$, $\omega^2 = 5.19864\times10^{-6}$ and $\sigma^2 = 1.466088\times10^{-5}$ (a plausible $0.383\%$ daily volatility with about 16 basis points of per-trade noise). Since independent noise inflates the two variances but not the covariance, the correlation attenuates by $1/(1+n\omega^2/\sigma^2)$, which is $0.2606$ at $n=8$ and $0.005841$ at $n=480$, predicting a 1-minute-to-1-hour correlation ratio of $0.02241$. The observed MM ratio is $0.1384/0.2829 = 0.4892$ — the noise model **over-predicts the Epps decay by a factor of 21.8**, which is quantitative support for the paper's refusal to reduce the Epps effect to microstructure noise *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** the two-point calibration is exactly identified and therefore untestable on its own — two equations in two unknowns leave zero degrees of freedom, so goodness of fit only exists once a third scale is added, and adding it breaks the model: the fitted parameters predict a 10-minute daily-equivalent variance of $\sigma^2+48\omega^2 = 1.466088\times10^{-5}+2.49535\times10^{-4} = 2.64194\times10^{-4}$, i.e. $\hat\sigma_D = 0.01625$, against the reported $0.0135$ — a 20% over-prediction. The idea must therefore be run as a three-or-more-point over-identified fit with residual diagnostics, never as a two-point solve *(From AI)*
  - **Weak:** the test runs against the paper's own stated methodology — the authors explicitly say they "cautiously avoid the more popular market micro-structure noise frameworks", so results from this idea are a competing-hypothesis check, not an extension the paper endorses, and any conclusion inherits the assumption of i.i.d. additive noise that the paper rejects on principle *(From paper)*
